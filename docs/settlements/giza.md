## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Great Pyramid of Khufu: a true pyramid, originally 146.6 m (280 cubits) high on a base of 230.33 m (440 cubits), a slope of 51°50′40″ [stated: Wikipedia]; cased in white limestone from Tura [stated: Wikipedia; Smarthistory], "slant-faced, but flat-topped, blocks of highly polished white limestone" [stated: Wikipedia (IPFS)]; a capstone that may have been gilded [stated: Smarthistory] — ours is gilded.
- **Moment shown:** completed, c. 2560 BC, before Khafre's pyramid. [stated: dates — Wikipedia]

## 3. Orientation

The pyramid's sides face the cardinal points within minutes of arc [stated: Wikipedia (IPFS)]; the temples and causeway on its east, toward the river.

## 4. The style

Fine white limestone casing; plain limestone walls; red granite pillars and black basalt pavements in the temples; battered mastabas with false doors on their east faces; mudbrick and mud plaster in the town. [stated: materials — Wikipedia, historyofegypt; inferred details]

### 4.2 The boat pits

Seven boat pits at Giza belong to Khufu's complex, five of them directly beside the Great Pyramid [stated: Wikipedia "Ancient Egyptian royal ships"]. The first ship lay dismantled in a pit cut in the rock immediately south of the pyramid, the second in a pit parallel and adjacent to it [stated: Madain Project]; each pit was limestone-lined and sealed with massive blocks after the burial [stated: Madain Project]; over the first, 41 blocks averaging about 14 tons, with key stones at its west end [stated: Waseda University Khufu Boat project]. The other three, boat-shaped, lie east of the pyramid — beside the mortuary temple and along the causeway [inferred: from plans of the site as generally published; positions approximate].
Ours, as sealed at the moment shown: the two southern pits (31 and 32.5 m) beyond the south enclosure wall, each covered by 40–41 limestone beams laid side by side, the joints stopped with gypsum, a kerb round the cutting; the three eastern pits (34–50 m) boat-shaped, their covering beams following the hull's outline — north of the mortuary temple, between it and the first queen's pyramid, and beside the causeway. [inferred: sizes of the covers and kerbs, the gypsum]

### 4.3 The king's ship in the harbour

The first Khufu ship: 43.6 m long, 5.9 m in the beam [stated: Wikipedia]; Lebanese cedar and Egyptian acacia, joined by mortise and tenon and lashed with cords [stated: Madain Project]; papyriform ends — the stem rising sharply, the sternpost higher still and curving toward the bow [stated: indigenousboats, after Landström]; a windowless deckhouse about 9 m long with an inner chamber about 2 m, a wooden framework for an awning extending from it and a second canopy in the prow; twelve oars, ten lashed amidships ahead of the deckhouse, two steering oars at the stern; the deck almost wholly covered, stringers along it to which the oars were lashed [stated: NOVA "Building Pharaoh's Ship"].
Ours: the ship afloat in the harbour beside the landing, as before her burial, bow to the north: the hull with its wale, the posts ending in papyrus umbels, the deckhouse with its anteroom and framed panels, the awning frame with a linen awning over its aft half, the bow canopy, ten oars with their blades in the water, the steering oars against their posts. That she lay at the harbour is [inferred]; her purpose is debated [stated: Madain Project].

### 4.4 The mastaba streets and their chapels

- The Western Cemetery was "laid out in blocks separated by avenues running north to south and intersecting east to west"; its first mastabas were rectangular, cores of well-laid blocks or block-faced with rubble fill, without chapels or rooms inside; "slab stelae were placed into the southern end of their eastern walls", standing for the false door, and "the areas of the slab stelae were often enclosed within small mud-brick walls"; later casing and false doors replaced the slab stelae, and the mud-brick chapels were sometimes replaced or enveloped by stone [stated: emhotep, "Khufu's Western Cemetery"]. Khufu's family and officials lay in the Eastern Cemetery, with eight double mastabas for his children and their spouses [stated: Wikipedia draft "Pyramid complex of Khufu"]. Slab stelae show the owner seated at a table of loaves with the lists of offerings, and were painted (Nefertiabet's is the famous example) [stated: Wikipedia "Nefertiabet"; Harvard Giza Project "Slab stelae of the Giza necropolis"].
- **Ours:** the tombs in rows along streets running north–south (21 m apart) crossed by east–west ones (32 m), each 9–12 m east–west and 18–27 m north–south, 4.2–6.6 m high. In the West Field the core in stepped courses about 0.8 m high, each set back; in the East Field cased smooth in Tura stone. On every east face, 2.6 m from the south end, the slab stela (0.53 × 0.38 m) set in a frame in the second course at 1.05 m: in painted relief the owner on a lion-legged stool, red-brown, his white kilt and black wig, reaching to the table of half loaves, a jar beneath it, columns of the offering list in blue and green under a blue band. Round it a chapel of mud brick 2.9 × 3.4 m, 2.3 m high, its door (0.85 × 1.75 m, palm-trunk lintel) to the east, a roof of palm logs under mud, a beaten floor; before the stela a limestone offering basin with three loaves and a beer jar on it [inferred: the sizes, the stela's position in height, the chapel's form and roof, the furnishings].

### 4.5 The temples' walls and ceilings

- Khufu's mortuary temple: a large court took most of its area, its floor "plastered with irregular slabs of basalt"; "rectangular granite pillars supported the roof which surrounded the court"; "the limestone walls of the temple were richly decorated with painted reliefs and hieroglyphic inscriptions" [stated: Wikipedia draft]; fragments of the court's wall reliefs show the king's thirty-year jubilee, relief-carved and painted [stated: AERA, "The Great Pyramid Temple"].
- **Ours (all the pyramid temples):** on the inner faces of the walls under the roofed ambulatory, registers 1.95 m high of offering bearers in painted raised relief (red-brown skin, white kilts, black wigs) striding toward the middle of each wall, bringing a loaf, a beer jar, a lotus or a trussed fowl, on blue ground lines; under the roof a band of blue and a frieze of khekher knots; the roof's undersides painted as the night sky, blue with yellow stars, stopping short of the architraves [inferred: the subjects (offering bearers stand in for the jubilee scenes), the sizes, the star ceiling — known from later Old Kingdom buildings].

### 4.6 The pyramid's interior, as at the burial

The entrance in the north face 16.98 m above the base and 7.29 m east of the centre line; the descending passage at 26°31' and 1.04 m wide; the ascending passage at 26°02' from 28.2 m down it, 39.3 m long; the Grand Gallery 46.68 m long and 8.6 m high, 2.06 m wide over its benches (0.51 m wide, 0.53 m high), with 27 slots a side in the benches, the walls rising 2.29 m and then corbelled in seven courses of about 7.6 cm a side to 1.04 m at the roof, the roof slabs each set a little steeper than the floor in its own notch; the Great Step 0.9 m high at its head; the antechamber 2.97 m long and 3.78 m high with granite wainscots, the grooves of the granite leaf and three portcullis slabs, and the half-round hollows over them; the King's Chamber of red granite 10.47 × 5.234 m and 5.974 m high in five courses, roofed by nine granite beams, the mouths of the two shafts in the north and south walls about 0.91 m up; the lidless granite sarcophagus 2.28 × 0.98 × 1.05 m by the west wall, its rim cut back for a sliding lid [stated: Petrie 1883, ch. 7; Maragioglio and Rinaldi 1965]. The three granite plugs stored in the gallery on beams across the slots, to be slid down after the burial, and the portcullis slabs held up on ropes over logs [inferred: the plugs are still in the ascending passage and the slots and hollows fit this use, Lehner 1997, 112–113]; the timber block before the Great Step, the stair over the plugs, the lid laid out on timbers and the oil lamps [inferred]. The sloping and low passages are given a walker's headroom of 2.1 m (real 1.05–1.2 m) [inferred, a concession to the walker]; the descending passage ends in the dark 60 m in (the subterranean chamber is not built).

## 5. The town

Long galleries (halls with a central colonnade and sleeping platforms), houses with a court, an oven and bins; flat roofs of logs and mud. [inferred]

**Bakeries.** Dozens of bakeries able to make thousands of loaves a day; thousands of heavy bell-shaped pots (bedja), heated in open fires, filled with dough and stacked [stated: The Archaeologist]; the bakery rooms excavated in 1991 had low stone walls, large vats and hearth platforms, attached to a large mudbrick building [stated: NOVA "Pyramids — the inside story"]. Ours: bakers squat at most of the hearths [inferred]. Sixteen bakeries, four at the south end of each gallery block — a room of low stone walls, the hearth platform along the south wall with rows of bread pots bedded in ash (some lidded with an upturned pot), a stack of pots heating upturned over a fire in a corner, two vats in mudbrick rings. [inferred: placement, sizes, the arrangement of the pots]

## 5a. People

Funerary (ka) priests served the tombs' offering cults [inferred: general Old Kingdom practice]. Ours: a shaven priest in white with a sash at about one chapel door in five; offering bearers — women in white sheath dresses, men in white — with baskets or jars on their heads walking the cemetery streets; bakers at the bakeries [inferred: numbers and dress, within the shapes the figure library offers].

## 6. Ground and water

The plateau level round the pyramid, falling 12 m east to the valley and 5 m south to the town; the harbour basin in the valley. (`ground.js`)

## 7. Planting

None on the plateau; date palms and sycamore figs in the valley by the harbour. [inferred]

## 8. Sky and weather

Clear and hot, haze now and then, the khamsin's dust in spring. Latitude 30° N. [inferred]

## 9. Sound
