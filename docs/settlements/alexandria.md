## 1. The moment and the real example

- **Moment shown:** about 250 BC, the reign of Ptolemy II Philadelphus (285/283–246 BC) turning to Ptolemy III Euergetes (246–222). The city was founded by Alexander in April 331 BC, to the plan of the architect Dinocrates of Rhodes, a Hippodamian grid; Ptolemy I made it his capital in 305 BC [stated: Wikipedia "Alexandria"]. The Pharos was begun by Ptolemy I and finished under Ptolemy II (between 284 and 246 BC, twelve years, 800 talents) [stated: Wikipedia "Lighthouse of Alexandria"; Britannica "Pharos of Alexandria" (finished c. 280 BC, Sostratos of Knidos)]. The Library became a physical institution under Ptolemy II [stated: Wikipedia "Library of Alexandria"]; Ptolemy III founded the Serapeum (its daughter library came in his reign) [stated: Wikipedia "Serapeum of Alexandria"]. Eratosthenes (third head librarian, from c. 245) measured the Earth; the Septuagint was translated in the reign of Ptolemy II (Letter of Aristeas puts the translators on the island of Pharos) [stated: Wikipedia, as above; the letter is a tradition, [inferred] as colour].
- **Landmarks (two, one settlement):** the Pharos on its island, and the Museion with its Library in the royal quarter on the Great Harbour.
- **Sources:** Strabo, *Geography* 17.1.6–10 (written c. 20 BC, so late-Ptolemaic and early-Roman; used for the plan, [inferred] for 250 BC) — https://websites.uta.edu/hunnicut/reading-list/readings-western-civilization/strabo-description-of-alexandria ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighthouse_of_Alexandria ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Alexandria ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serapeum_of_Alexandria ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heptastadion ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria ; https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pharos-of-Alexandria .

## 3. The Pharos (the first landmark)

- Three tiers, each sloping a little inward: a square tower with a central core, an octagon, a cylinder [stated: Britannica; Wikipedia]. At least 100 m high; reports run 103–118 m [stated: Wikipedia]. Base 30 × 30 m (Balawi: 45 ba per side; Ibn Battuta 30.8 m) [stated: Wikipedia]; octagon 16.4 m across, cylinder 8.7 m across, the lantern chamber 4.3 m [stated: Wikipedia, from Arab descriptions]. Tier heights in the usual reconstruction (Thiersch 1909) about 56 m, 27 m and 9 m with the lantern above [inferred: Thiersch's drawing, no figures on the pages read]; built here at 56 / 28 / 10 m, total about 104 m with the statue.
- Light-coloured limestone (and granite) in large blocks, lead run between the blocks at the foot [stated: Wikipedia, Idrisi]. A broad inner ramp (the horsemen's ramp), roofed with masonry, wide enough for two riders [stated: Wikipedia, Balawi]; rooms on the upper storeys [stated: same].
- On top: a fire at night, a mirror by day [stated: Wikipedia]; a statue of Zeus (Soter) or Poseidon at the top, with the inscription to Zeus on the seaward face [stated: Wikipedia, Mas'udi]. A Triton at each corner of the first tier (from coins of the 2nd century AD, so [inferred] for the 3rd century BC).
- The island: the Pharos is on the island's east end; the Heptastadion joins the island's middle to the town [stated: Wikipedia]. [inferred] a quay and a mole at the tower's foot, a few fishermen's and keepers' houses.

## 4. The Museion and the Library (the second landmark)

- The Library was built in the Brucheion as part of the Mouseion, a part of the royal palaces [stated: Strabo; Wikipedia]. The Museion had "a public walk (peripatos), seating (exedra) and a large hall where the scholars take a common meal"; its property was held in common and a priest presided [stated: Strabo 17.1.8]. The exact layout is unknown: Greek columns, a peripatos, a shared dining room, a reading room, meeting rooms, gardens, lecture halls [stated: Wikipedia "Library of Alexandria"]; the circular domed dining hall of the popular picture is [inferred] and not used here: the common meal is taken in a long hall with the couches along the walls (an oikos of the Greek kind).
- A hall of shelves for the scrolls (bibliothekai, "the place of the cure of the soul" over them, a late tradition [stated: Wikipedia]); the shape of the shelves is [inferred] from Hellenistic and Roman book-stores: wall niches and pigeonholes in which the scrolls lie flat with a tag (sillybos) on each, and baskets and chests on the floor [inferred: Roman libraries of Celsus, Pergamon].
- The collection: 40,000–700,000 scrolls by estimates; 500,000 the aim of Ptolemy II [stated: Wikipedia]. Scrolls of papyrus, rolled, about 25–30 cm high [inferred: standard].
- Scholars: 30–50 early on, paid, fed and exempt from tax [stated: Strabo; Wikipedia]: Zenodotos (first librarian), Callimachus (the Pinakes in 120 books), Apollonios of Rhodes, Eratosthenes [stated: Wikipedia]. Shown at tables with scrolls, walking and talking in the peripatos, [inferred] in the himation.
- Architecture built here (all [inferred] in form, from the Greek stoa and the Hellenistic gymnasium): a large peristyle garden court with Doric colonnades on four sides (the peripatos), the reading halls behind the north and east colonnades with a row of Ionic columns down the middle and pigeonholed walls; an exedra (a semicircular seat under a roof) on the garden; the dining hall on the south with couches. Ptolemaic touch: the doors framed with the Egyptian cavetto cornice and the roof-edges with palm-leaf capitals in the inner court, [inferred: Ptolemaic temples combine the orders].

## 5. The Great Harbour, the ships and the quays

- The harbours: the Great Harbour (east of the mole) for the king's and the merchants' ships; the Eunostos (west) and the Kibotos basin; the lake harbour on Mareotis, trade of the Nile [stated: Strabo; Wikipedia].
- Ships [inferred: Hellenistic types]: the merchantman (a broad round-hulled sailer, one mast with a square sail, steering oars at the stern, a cabin aft; the *Syracusia* was the extreme), the war-galley (a long low hull with a ram, one or two banks of oars), the Nile boats and the small fishing boats; the swan-neck stern. Dress of the sails: white or pale linen, some with a red band.
- Quays: stone-faced [inferred], with bollards (stone or wooden), mooring rings; warehouses behind the Emporion (the merchants' harbour) [inferred]; Egyptian grain went out and Greek wine and oil came in (the amphorae) [inferred].

## 6. Streets, colonnades, houses

- Streets cut at right angles, the grid with attendant subterranean canals under each street [stated: Wikipedia "Alexandria"]. Streets wide enough for chariots and horse [stated: Strabo, "more than a plethron"].
- The Canopic Way was lined with colonnades [stated: Wikipedia: "colonnaded streets"; late] — here a Doric colonnade of limestone with shops behind (stoa with rooms), the pavement of limestone slabs. [inferred for 250 BC: the great colonnades are largely later, but the Gymnasium's porticoes and the royal quarter's stoas are stated for the late Ptolemies; here the first stretches only].
- Houses: Hellenistic courtyard houses with a peristyle of two or four sides, rooms round the court (the andron, the dining room), a staircase to an upper floor, painted plaster walls in the Masonry style (blocks of colour imitating ashlar: ochre, red, white, blue) — the style began in the Hellenistic East [inferred: Masonry style, 3rd c. BC, Delos, Alexandria]; walls of mudbrick on limestone footings and plastered; flat roofs of beams and clay in Egypt (no tile) with a few pitched tile roofs in the Greek houses [inferred]. Limestone from the local ridge and from Tura and Mokattam; stucco.
- Rhakotis: smaller Egyptian mudbrick houses, flat roofs with roof-top rooms and open stairs, palm-trunk beams, little windows high up, drying linen on the roofs [inferred: Egyptian town houses, as `oldkingdom` mud_house; Greek-Egyptian continuity].
- The Jewish quarter: houses of the same kind with a proseuche (prayer house), a hall with benches and a stone chest for the scrolls, a ritual bath [inferred: later proseuche finds; the Septuagint shows a Greek-speaking community].

## 7. The Serapeum

- Founded by Ptolemy III Euergetes (246–222), architect Parmeniskos, on the rocky plateau above Rhakotis, looking north to the sea and south to the lake [stated: Wikipedia "Serapeum of Alexandria"]. Foundation plaques in Greek and Egyptian: gold, silver, bronze, faience, mud-brick and glass [stated: same]. Daughter library in the precinct [stated: same]. Subterranean galleries [stated: same].
- Built here (inferred in form): a stair up to the plateau, a walled precinct with a colonnade, the temple of Serapis on a podium, Doric front with an Egyptian cavetto cornice, a pair of sphinxes before the stair. (The great column known as Pompey's Pillar is of AD 298–302 and not shown [stated: same].)

## 8. Materials and climate

- Local limestone (pale, shelly, from the coastal ridge), granite from Aswan for the columns and the great blocks (Pharos), the white marble of Greece for the finest work, mudbrick, lime plaster painted, cedar from Lebanon for beams, palm beams, tile only in Greek houses. [inferred]
- Climate: Mediterranean, mild and humid; the damp and salt of the sea air weather the limestone and the plaster fast (rising damp, salt crust, algae at the waterline, pitting) — `damp` 0.75, waterline at the sea [inferred]. Planting: date palms, sycamore-fig, tamarisk by the shore, olive, vine in the gardens, papyrus and reed at the lake [inferred].

## 9. People and sound

- Greek: chiton and himation, the broad hat (petasos), sandals; women in long chiton with himation over the head; Egyptian: the linen kilt (shendyt) for men, the sheath dress (kalasiris) or a wrap for women, shaved heads and wigs, bare feet; workers in loincloth; Jewish: the same Greek dress, the hair and beard worn, a tasselled mantle [inferred: from the period's art; the Jews of Alexandria were Greek-speaking]. Sailors in short chitons with caps; porters with sacks and amphorae; scholars in himation with a scroll; soldiers of the Ptolemies in the chlamys and the broad hat or helmet with a spear.
- Sound: the sea and the harbour, gulls, the shouts of the quays, the bells and the sistra of Isis, the flutes and the lyre of the Greek houses, the drone and voices of the Egyptian temples. Music in `music.js`: a Greek mode (the Dorian-like minor) with the lyre and aulos standing in as a plucked string and a reed, a drone in the Egyptian part. [inferred]

## 10. Life, smoke, fauna

- Smoke: kitchen smoke at the three meals, the Pharos' fire (burning day and night as a column of smoke by day, a flame by night), the potters' kiln, the bakeries, the incense on the Serapeum's altar. [inferred; stated for the Pharos: "a fire burned at night"]
- Fauna: gulls and terns over the harbour, kites (black kites in Egypt), pigeons, swallows, sparrows, ibis and egret on the lake shore, mullet in the harbour; donkeys, a few camels, dogs, cats (sacred, Egyptian), goats. [inferred]

## 11. What is not shown

The Soma (Alexander's tomb) is shown only as a walled precinct; the Gymnasium and the palaces as masses with colonnades; the Hippodrome, Nicopolis and the Mareotic canals beyond the disc; the Library's circular dining hall, the zoo and the observatory are [inferred] and omitted.

## 12. Deepening pass (the Suzhou standard): what is built at eye level

Sources used for the form of the small things: Strabo 17.1.6–10 (the harbours, the street grid, the Pharos), the Wikipedia articles already cited, Bengtsson's reconstruction of the harbour (ancientportsantiques.com, "AlexandriaReconstruction Bengtsson2000") for the quays and the emporium; the rest is [inferred] from Hellenistic and Roman practice.

- **Houses.** A courtyard house of the Greek quarter is entered by a street door into a passage between a room on the street and the court. The room beside the door is a shop (a counter at a wide window, shutters folded up as a hood, goods on the sill, a sign, a cloth awning) or a store; the court has a well-head, an altar, a pot of herbs and a fig or date palm, a peristyle on the back range; the rooms off it are the kitchen (clay oven, hearth, water jars, firewood, a quern), a store (pithoi, amphorae) or the loom room (a warp-weighted loom, as in Greek houses of the 3rd c. BC), and the back range has the andron with its couches along the walls (kline, table, krater) and the bedroom. [inferred: Delos and Priene houses; the Alexandrian houses are known mostly from tombs, the Hadra hydriai and Roman-period finds, Strabo's "many-storeyed" streets being late] Upper storeys solid with shuttered windows; some with a timber loggia over the street [inferred: Egyptian houses of the Delta had roof-top rooms and loggias; Alexandria's later houses are described with balconies].
- **Weathering.** The salt air of the harbour eats plaster fast: a damp band at the foot of every front, patches where plaster fell and the mudbrick shows, streaks under the sills [inferred, see §8 `damp`].
- **Rhakotis.** The Egyptian houses face the street with their full width (the old model left a bare yard in front): ground room with a mat bed, a quern, jars and baskets, the stair up the side wall to the roof room, a walled yard behind with a round mud granary silo, a bread oven and a palm [inferred: Egyptian town houses of the New Kingdom to the Roman period (Amarna, Karanis); round silos are Egyptian].
- **The quay.** Goods heaped on the emporium's quay (amphorae of Rhodian and Knidian wine and oil, sacks of Egyptian grain, bales of cloth, cedar logs from Lebanon, ingots of bronze and lead), a crane, nets and split fish drying on racks at the Eunostos, mooring gear, and the horrea behind the front (warehouses of open bays on piers). [inferred; Egyptian grain and papyrus went out, wine and oil came in — Wikipedia "Alexandria"; the crane is drawn as a treadwheel crane, which is attested for the Romans (1st c. BC and later) and is [inferred] here as the form of a Hellenistic windlass crane (Vitruvius 10.2 describes the trispastos and polyspastos)].
- **The Pharos.** Hollowed: the door on the harbour side opens on a ground hall; the inner ramp ("broad ramp, roofed with masonry, wide enough for two riders", Balawi, per Wikipedia "Lighthouse of Alexandria") winds up round a solid core in four legs a turn, four turns, to the platform of the first tier (about 56 m), where the octagon rises and the Tritons stand at the corners. [stated: that there was an inner ramp and rooms; the plan of the ramp is inferred]
- **The Serapeum.** The cella's door stands open: the cult image of Serapis enthroned (the modius on his head, the sceptre, Cerberus at his side, after the type of Bryaxis [stated: Wikipedia "Serapis"; Ptolemy I's foundation story]) with an altar of offerings, lampstands, stelae and offering tables; colonnades on the court's north and south (the daughter library [stated: Wikipedia "Serapeum of Alexandria"]) and a sphinx avenue before the pylon [inferred; the Egyptian approach avenue is the form of the Serapeum at Saqqara]. The temple's form is [inferred] as before.
- **Streets.** The cross streets are flagged with a stone gutter along the middle (the "subterranean canals" under each street [stated: Wikipedia "Alexandria"] have drains), the alleys are beaten pebbles; street shrines of Isis at the crossings; washing across the alleys; a fountain house and stelae in the agora; date palms on the promenade [inferred].
- **Life.** Jars, sacks and baskets by the shop's trade at each front; donkeys with loads, dogs, cats (sacred), fowl and goats in Rhakotis; fishermen sitting at the nets, workers at the crane, porters with sacks on the quay; water-carriers, basket-women and priests among the walkers; the bakers', potters' and bronze-smiths' lots smoke all day. [inferred]
