# Nan Madol, Pohnpei, c. 1300 — dossier

Settlement id: `nan-madol` · Style: `nanmadol` · Region: eastern Caroline Islands (Temwen island, Pohnpei, Micronesia) · Code: `src/settlements/nan-madol/`, style `src/styles/nanmadol/`

## 1. The real example and the moment

- **The place.** Nan Madol ("within the intervals", the canals) is a site of 92 artificial islets of stone and coral fill bordered by tidal canals, enclosed by walls, about 1.5 × 0.5 km in its core, on the reef flat off the south-east shore of Temwen island [stated: Wikipedia "Nan Madol"]. The wider district covers more than 18 km², stone architecture on the reef flat and the Pohnpei coast [stated: Wikipedia]. The islets are "built above tide level", up to about 115 × 100 m [stated: NRHP nomination, 1974]. About 130 buildings with walls of basalt laid crosswise [stated: NPS "Nan Madol"].
- **The moment shown: c. 1300, the Saudeleur dynasty at its height.** Megalithic construction began about AD 1180–1200 [stated: Wikipedia; McCoy & Athens 2012 give AD 1200–1300]; the dynasty ruled roughly 500 years and fell to Isokelekel about 1628 [stated: Wikipedia]. The first monumental burial, of a chief in a stone-and-coral tomb, was about 1200 [stated: Smarthistory]. So c. 1300 Nandauwas stands new, the sea wall is still being added to (Pahnwi, the south corner of the L-shaped sea wall, "was built late, after A.D. 1200" [stated: NRHP]) and the Saudeleur still rule from here [inferred: the date chosen by us within the stated span].
- **Who lived here.** The elite centre's population almost certainly did not exceed 1,000, perhaps less than half that [stated: Wikipedia]; Madol Powe, the north-east mortuary sector, holds 58 islets, most once home to priests [stated: Wikipedia]; the south-east district held commoners' homes [stated: NPS]. Chiefs were required to live there [stated: Wikipedia].
- **No fresh water or food.** All water and food came by boat from inland Pohnpei under the Saudeleur; Peiniot and later Usennamw were where the Saudeleur received food [stated: Wikipedia]. So canoes unloading and food islets are part of the picture [inferred: the unloading shown].
- **Sea level.** Research of 2023 found sea level nearly one metre lower when the city was first built [stated: Atlas Obscura, citing it]. Our islet tops stand 1.3 m over the water [inferred: platforms "above tide level"; the water a little higher than at the first building].

## 3. Orientation and layout (radius 190 m; +x east, +z south)

We compress this to a block of rectangular islets in three columns and four rows round Nandauwas, with the sea wall along the east and south.

## 4. The landmark: Nandauwas (Nan Dauas), the royal mortuary

- **Size.** The islet is 50 × 60 m [stated: NRHP]; its walls reach 8.1 m, the tallest in the complex [stated: NRHP]; other sources give 7.5 m (Atlas Obscura), 5.5–7.5 m (Wikipedia), 25 ft (Smarthistory). The walls stand on a foundation of boulders, some over 20 tonnes [stated: NRHP]. About 4,500 m³ of basalt and 13,000 m³ of coral fill [stated: NRHP].
- **Our plan.** An outer wall 50 × 60 m (the NRHP figure taken for the wall's outline, the islet itself 62 × 72 so that a landing apron runs round it [inferred]); the walls 2.6 m thick [inferred]; 5.8 m in the middle of each side and rising to 8.1 m at the corners — the "upturned corners" of the brief, where the courses step up and the corner columns project in alternate courses [inferred from the brief; the corner projection from the crib method]. An inner enclosure 26 × 32 m with walls to 4.6 m round the tomb [inferred: "a central tomb lies within the outer walls", NRHP]; the central tomb a platform of laid columns with a sunk crypt, its floor reached by steps, partly roofed with columns laid across [inferred: "cribbed crypts", Megalithic Builders; "a central crypt column", McCoy & Athens].
- **Entrances.** A gap in the middle of the outer wall's south side (a way 3.2 m wide through the 2.6 m thickness); the inner enclosure entered by a gap at the east end of its south side, so that one crosses the outer court before turning in [inferred: the walls' plan is not described in our sources].
- **Courts.** The outer court of coral gravel; in it a long basalt slab on stones — a sakau stone (*peitehl*) with pounding stones laid on it — and mats with offerings of breadfruit, yams and coconuts [inferred: sakau and feasts are the later Pohnpeian ritual; sources: Dana Lee Ling "Sakau ceremony", Google Arts "Pohnpeian ceremonial yam feast"]. In the inner court the tomb alone, bare, with a few offerings; no figures carved (Pohnpei has no carving of the kind: nothing is recorded) [inferred].

## 5. Materials and construction

- **Columnar basalt.** Prisms of basalt from a volcanic plug on the far side of Pohnpei [stated: Wikipedia]; columns average about 2.5–3.1 m long and 0.8–1.3 t, some far larger, up to 20 ft and 80–90 t [stated: McCoy & Athens; Smarthistory]; "some nearly a metre in diameter" [stated: NRHP]. Ours: 0.42–0.62 m across (mostly pentagonal and hexagonal), 2.0–3.4 m long [inferred within the stated figures].
- **Header-and-stretcher.** "Columnar basalt wall construction using typical header-and-stretcher architectural style" [stated: McCoy & Athens, fig. 2]; alternating layers of headers (ends facing out) and stretchers (sides facing out), no mortar, held by weight, with some coral fill [stated: Megalithic Builders; Smarthistory]. Ours: header courses show the hexagonal ends in a close grid; stretcher courses two columns thick on each face; the inside left as core of coral rubble [inferred].
- **Foundations.** Large boulders [stated: McCoy & Athens, NRHP]; we set big rounded basalt boulders at the waterline of the islet edges.
- **Islet edges.** Retaining walls of the same crib at the edge, the platform filled with coral [stated: NRHP — 13,000 m³ of coral fill at Nandauwas].
- **Surfaces.** Dark grey-black basalt (palette `dolerite_spotted` and `granite_black`: the nearest to the stone), dark and wet near the water, lichen and the pale salt of spray [inferred]. Coral rubble and gravel on the islets (the ground's sand cover and the `coral` and `coral_rag` materials).

## 6. The houses

- **The nahs** is the traditional Pohnpeian house: a rectangular plan with three partial walls and one wholly open end wall, which is the main ceremonial entrance; inside a raised platform in a U along the three walls where the family sits and sleeps; the head of the household at the far end; cooking in outbuildings; a thatch roof with a deep overhang [stated: Univ. of Oregon FSM architecture]. The platform may be built up with stone [stated: same]. Ours: a platform of stacked basalt prisms (the chief's higher), posts, half-walls of lashed reed and thatch, a hip roof of thatch with a ridge cap [inferred: the house form of the 13th century is taken from the modern one].
- **Thatch.** Frondlets of the ivory palm (*Metroxylon amicarum*, Pohnpeian *oahs*), midrib removed, pierced and sewn on 45° over rafters of *Phragmites karka* (*lirau*); on Pohnpei not netted down; the crown unit is the *pwarapar* [stated: Dana Lee Ling]. Palette: `thatch_palm`; lashings `rope_fibre`.
- **Cook-houses** apart (umu earth ovens) [stated: Oregon; inferred: umu].

## 7. People and work

- Dress: skirts and wraps of hibiscus-fibre and pandanus mats, bare above the waist, hair long or in a bun, shell and flower ornaments [inferred: the common dress of Micronesia; no source in this pass]; the Saudeleur's people in finer mats [inferred]. The pacific kit's loincloth and wrap figures stand in.
- Work shown: canoe-building on the Dapahu islet, coconut oil, food preparation in earth ovens, sakau pounding at the peitehl (pounders on the stone, the kohlo wrap of hibiscus bark; the call "Mut!" and the ringing of the stone [stated: Dana Lee Ling]), food landed by canoe, nets and fish, priests in the courts.
- Canoes: dugout hulls with an outrigger float (*ama*) on booms [inferred: the outrigger canoe of Micronesia; the large Pohnpeian sailing canoe is left out].
- Animals: pigs, chickens (brought by the Polynesian-Micronesian settlers), dogs [inferred]; sea and shore birds below.

## 8. Planting and shore

Coconut palms, breadfruit, banana, ivory palms (for thatch), hibiscus (*Hibiscus tiliaceus*: its bark for the sakau wrap [stated: Dana Lee Ling]) on the islets [inferred]; mangroves with stilt roots along the shore and canal edges ("most canals overgrown with mangrove": NRHP). The palm kinds of the plant set (date palm for the coconut, fig and camphor for breadfruit) stand in; mangroves are built by the style.

## 9. Sky, water, sound

- Equatorial maritime: hot, humid, trade-wind cumulus and frequent showers, Pohnpei among the wettest places on Earth [inferred]; the ground's `damp` high; water level one fixed value (the tide is not shown).
- Sound: surf on the reef at the sea wall, water slapping the basalt, wind in coconut palms, the pounding of sakau (stone on stone, in a steady rhythm), voices, pigs, roosters, terns; the music of the courts a slow chant with a conch (the *kahlap*) [inferred: Pacific conch trumpet].

## 10. Deepening pass (Nov 2026): what was added

- **Houses in two rows.** Where an islet is deep enough (≥ 9 m behind the first row) a second row of nahs stands behind, its open end toward the first row's back yard; 129 nahs in all [inferred: the elite islets held under 1,000 people; houses of priests and commoners on most islets, Wikipedia/NPS].
- **Eye-level dressing of every nah** (`yard`): stone steps, coconut bunches hung from the front beam, leaned paddle or spear, a hung basket; at the sides a net on poles or a line of mats; behind, drying racks (breadfruit slices, copra), a basalt pounding board with pestle and bowl, coconut heaps, a pig pen, log stacks, low boundary walls of coral stones [inferred: Pohnpeian household work — breadfruit and yam preparation by pounding; pigs and fowl, Dana Lee Ling food and ethnobotany pages; the pen and rack forms are ours]. Inside: ridge pole, tie beams and rafters, a second mat, a mat roll, wooden cups, hung basket, bananas, strung fish, a coconut-shell oil lamp.
- **The works of the special islets** (`works`): shipyard on Dapahu (a shed over a hull on stocks and a second begun, adze chips, logs, rope) [stated: Dapahu "used for canoe construction", Wikipedia]; oil works on Peinering (copra racks, two levered presses with fibre bags, a grating bench, bamboo tubes, nut heaps) [stated: "coconut oil preparation", Wikipedia; forms inferred]; the long cook-shed on the kitchen islet over a row of earth ovens with hot stones and leaf piles [stated: food preparation on some islets, Wikipedia]; the chief's hall on Idehd (a raised platform under a great hip roof, the chief's seat at its far end, mats and cups) [stated: Idehd "a royal residence", Megalithic Builders; the hall inferred from the nahs' form]; the food landing's store on Peiniot (baskets, yams, poles on a coral platform) [stated: Peiniot where food was received, Wikipedia].
- **The tomb islet** (Peinkitel-like): a walled court of crib walls 3.3 m high with a gap in the west wall where the causeway comes in, a platform of columns with a crypt (a sibling of Nandauwas's), lamps, tabu markers of braided frond, offerings, bark cloth on the bier [stated: high tomb walls on Peinkitel, Karian, Lemenkou, Wikipedia; contents inferred]. ## 10b. Fix and deepening pass 2 (Nov 2026): the arrival and the forecourt of Idehd

- **The arrival moved.** The browser review found the walls unreadable from the landing islet: Idehd's nahs (roofs to about 5.3 m) stand between Peiniot and Nandauwas, whose south wall is 5.8 m (8.1 m at the corners) [stated: NRHP, §4]. The causeway's axis (x −10) is the one clear line, so the arrival stands at the crossroads of Idehd (−10, 28), 36 m from the wall, looking down it: the whole 50 m south wall, its gate and both upturned corners lie in a 66° window between the nahs of the forecourt [inferred: geometry of our plan].
- **The forecourt** (`FORECOURT` in layout.js; `things`, `court` lamp/offering elements): a processional way of packed coral gravel 2.8 m wide, kept clear of trees and roofs; either side worked ground — breadfruit-slice racks, nets on poles, mat lines, pounding boards, coconut heaps, a pig pen, a canoe hull on rollers, two cook-houses [inferred]. Facts used: breadfruit is pounded hot on a board (Chuuk *nif*, *po*), and in Pohnpei the first of it must go to the Nahnmwarki; yam, pig and sakau are the tribute foods men bring to feasts [stated: Dana Lee Ling, "Food presentations", https://www.comfsm.fm/~dleeling/ethnobotany/e23foodpresentations.html; modern customs read back to c. 1300 — [inferred]]. Dapahu's hull-building (Wikipedia) is shown here in small as a hull being dug out near the canal [inferred].
- **Canal life before the wall.** Two new canoe landings on Idehd's north edge (steps, mooring posts, canoes with a boatman aboard) and two wading fishers on the causeway [inferred: the canals were used by canoe, NRHP; eel and fish catching, Smarthistory].
- **The walls read from afar.** Course banding (stretchers dark, headers' ends paler), log-cabin corners whose alternate courses run 0.55 m past the crossing wall, headers standing proud by uneven amounts (0.12 m), a pale coral skin on the crown [stated for the header-and-stretcher method: McCoy & Athens 2012; the tones and the projection are [inferred] from photographs of the corners].
