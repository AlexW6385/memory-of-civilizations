## 1. The real example

- **Pā:** fortified Māori settlements, often hillforts with palisades and defensive terraces [stated: Wikipedia "Pā"], almost all on prominent raised ground, especially volcanic hills [stated: Wikipedia]. The simplest had one palisade and elevated fighting stages [stated: Wikipedia]. Late 18th-century pā had extensive earthworks: storage pits, ditches, earth ramparts and multiple terraces [stated: Wikipedia]. Ōtūmoetai pā (Bay of Plenty, 1769) had ditches, banks, palisaded ramparts and fighting stages on several terraces [stated: the-history-girls].
- **Inside:** most pā had a tihi, a flat summit where important people lived, with terraces for buildings and rectangular or round kūmara pits [stated: LEARNZ]. The chief's house on the summit could be about 4.5 × 4 m [stated: Wikipedia text]. Common houses stood on the lower or outer land, sometimes sunk 30–40 cm into the ground [stated: Wikipedia]. Pits were about a metre deep under pitched roofs [stated: LEARNZ]. Missionaries recorded pā with one or two hundred houses [stated: ruapekapeka.co.nz].
- **Moment shown:** the later 18th century, before muskets changed the pā's design [stated: LEARNZ].

## 4. The style

Palisades of close-set split posts with taller carved posts (red ochre); fighting stages on posts; houses with low walls, steep gabled roofs of raupō thatch running down low, a front porch under carved bargeboards meeting at a carved face; pātaka raised on posts; kūmara pits under low roofs; carved gateways. ## 4a. Detail of the carving, the houses, the canoes and the gardens

- **Era check.** The fully carved meeting house (whare whakairo) appeared after contact, in the mid-19th century, and is "not an ancient form of architecture"; earlier houses were small, often partly sunk, with a low door and few windows; the wharepuni (from about the 15th century) gained a front porch and was usually unadorned unless it belonged to a leader, when it might have a carved pare, tekoteko or poutokomanawa [stated: Te Ara "Māori architecture"]. Cook's crew in the 18th century saw houses about 10 m long, entirely carved [stated: Wikipedia "Wharenui"]. So only the chief's house is carved; common houses are plain [inferred].
- **The carved house's parts** (`whare`, rank 2): koruru at the gable's point (the ancestor's head); maihi, the diagonal bargeboards (arms), their ends the raparapa ("fingers"); amo, the uprights holding the maihi's ends; paepae, the threshold beam on the ground at the front; poupou, carved slabs lining the porch's walls and the inside; heke, the rafters (ribs); tāhuhu, the ridge beam (backbone); poutokomanawa, the central post; the kūwaha (door) with its pare (lintel); windows [stated: Wikipedia "Wharenui"]. Tukutuku reed panels between the poupou [inferred: their use in the period; the `koshi` lattice material stands in]. Sizes: 5 × 9 m, walls 1.6 m, porch 1.6 m deep, door 0.64 × 1.15 m [inferred]. Flax mats on the floor and a hearth of stones inside the door [stated: earth floors covered with flax mats, Te Ara; the central fire, The History Girls].
- **Common houses:** a wooden frame covered with raupō, toetoe or nīkau; small, low door [stated: Te Ara]; side walls carried forward to make the porch, a door left of centre and a small window [inferred].
- **Carved figures** (`figure`: post, slab and face): the head about two-fifths of the figure, slanting eye rings inlaid with pāua, the mouth open with the tongue out, three-fingered hands on the belly, bent legs, haehae grooves [inferred from museum carvings in the Te Arawa style; pāua stands in as `glaze_green`]. Painted with kōkōwai (red ochre) [inferred].
- **Palisade:** the pou whakarae is the prominent, intricately carved post of a pā's palisade [stated: Wikipedia "Pou whakarae"]. Ours: every ninth post, 0.44 m thick, crowned by a 1.3 m figure looking outward [inferred].
- **Pātaka:** raised above the ground, often on one or more poles, reached by a removable ladder or ramp, richly carved especially round the entrance [stated: Te Ara]. Ours: four posts, a board deck, board walls, carved maihi, amo, koruru and paepae, a notched pole [inferred]. Food was kept in raised storehouses [stated: The History Girls].
- **Waka taua** (Parkinson's engraving, 1769): the tauihu (prow) with a large figurehead; the huge taurapa (sternpost); the sides (rauawa) finely carved and lashed to the hull [stated: Te Ara "Waka taua, 1769"]. Ours: 10–16 m, hull hollowed with rocker toward the ends, ochred washstrakes, thwarts and paddles, a prow board with a forward-looking figure and two pierced spirals, a 2.9 m sternpost with two spirals [inferred: proportions]. The feathers on the sternpost are left out (no material).
- **Kūmara gardens** (`mara`): mounds (puke) of loosened soil in rows or a quincunx; sunny, northerly slopes preferred; gravel and sand added to heavy soils; simple fences as windbreaks and against pūkeko [stated: Te Ara "Kūmara"]; carved god sticks (atua kiato) round the gardens [stated: The History Girls]. Ours: 80 fenced plots of 18 × 12 m, mounds 0.75–0.9 m across at 1.25 m spacing, a god stick with a small figure at two corners [inferred].

## 4b. Second pass: the welcome, the cooking place, flax and weaving, fish racks, the canoe builders, the stages manned

- **Pōwhiri on the marae ātea**: the marae ātea is the courtyard in front of the meeting house; the welcome begins once the visitors (manuhiri) have assembled, with the karanga, a call from a woman of the hosts (tangata whenua) answered by one of the visitors, and the visitors move onto the marae as the calling goes on; for a particularly important visitor a ritual challenge (wero) comes first, the challenger laying down a baton that the guest picks up; speeches (whaikōrero) follow, each with a waiata, and the hongi at the end [stated: Te Ara "Te kawa o te marae"]. In the pā the open space before the chief's house on the tihi serves [inferred: the formal marae with a wharenui is a later form, §4a]. Ours: before the chief's house the hosts — seven old men seated in a row on a bench of split logs (`paepae`), nine women standing with the caller among them, six more behind; the visitors in a body at the summit's gateway, ten women in front, twelve men behind, their chief at the front in a white dog-skin cloak; between them the challenger with his taiaha, a dart (rākau) laid on the ground before the visitors' chief [inferred: numbers; women in front of the visitors and the seating as now commonly practised]. The walk up from the gate is left clear.
- **Hāngī and kāuta** (`hangi`): a pit is dug and stones heated in it with a large fire; the embers are removed, the food set on the hot stones, covered and sealed with earth, and cooks for two to four hours; before European contact food was wrapped in bark and large leaves (wire baskets came in the 1800s) [stated: Wikipedia "Hāngī"]. Ours, on the east of the tihi: an open pit ringed with thrown-up earth, a fire of logs heaped with basalt stones; a second oven already closed, a low earth mound with the leaves showing round its foot; flax baskets of kūmara and fern root; a wooden bowl of water; firewood stacked; a cooking shelter (kāuta), a lean-to of raupō on four posts with a back wall [inferred: the layout; the kāuta as the cooking shed of the period]. Four women at it.
- **Flax and weaving** (`harakeke`, `weaving`): a pā harakeke is a stand of flax, cultivated or natural, cropped by weavers, the leaves cut so as not to harm the centre shoot, the edges and midrib stripped, the fibre (muka) drawn with a shell's edge; weaving was mainly the women's work [stated: Te Ara "The pā harakeke"]. Ours: seventy flax clumps (fans of stiff, keeled leaves bending over at the tip, flower stalks on some) on the lake shore; on the west of the tihi three weavers' places, each a mat (whāriki) half plaited on the ground with the loose strips at its working edge, bundles of prepared strips tied at one end, a small basket, a woman seated at it and sometimes a second squatting by [inferred: the arrangement].
- **Fish racks** (`whata`): Māori dried fish, a stingray among them, on elevated racks [stated: Te Ara "Drying fish" — watercolour of Aorere, Golden Bay, early 1840s]. Ours: three racks of posts in pairs, two rails a side at 2 m and 1.3 m, split fish hung over them, on the lake shore by the canoe builders, a woman at one (lake fish and eels in Rotorua) [inferred: the form].
- **The canoe being hollowed** (`waka_build`): a big stone adze on a long shaft swung by several men felled the tree; an ordinary stone adze chipped out the wood between the grooves; fire was used too [stated: Te Ara "Constructing a waka", after A. H. Messenger's sketch]. Ours: an 11 m trunk on three skids on the shore, its top adzed flat and its outside shaped, hollowed from the stern for half its length, the bow still solid; chips round it; three adzes (toki) with basalt blades lashed to bent handles; three men at it, stripped to the belt [inferred: the stage of the work, finishing at the village].
- **The stages manned and the taiaha** (`taiaha`, `rakau`): fighting stages are the pā's stated defence (§1). Ours: a warrior on every other stage looking out, stripped to the belt and kilt (drawn as a short skin-coloured garment with a flax belt), a taiaha — the long staff with its blade at the foot, the head at the top and its collar of white dog hair — upright in his hand [inferred: the dress and the weapon from museum taiaha].
- **People's dress**: cloaks of flax fibre (pale gold), of dyed fibre (brown, black) and of dog skin (white, brown) for the chiefs, worn over the shoulders; men's hair in a topknot, women's long [inferred].

## 4c. Third pass (L3): rauponga on the carved fronts, eel pots, kurī

- **Rauponga** (`rauponga` in src/styles/maori/index.js): the surface pattern "consists of a row of dog-tooth notches (pakati) on each side of which are parallel grooves (haehae) and ridges (raumoa)"; in some districts it "is used on pataka (storehouses) and only rarely on other buildings" [stated: Te Ara 1966, "Maori art"]. Ours: the faces of the maihi carved in two bands of rauponga — two ridges each side of a row of teeth pointing alternately either way — on the chief's house and on every pātaka (whose front is the same builder); a band along the paepae; bands either side of the face on the pare [inferred: the bands' places on the chief's house, which the period's carved leader's house may have had — §4a]. The plain raised rib on the maihi is gone. Spirals (pītau) and manaia are still to come.
- **Hīnaki** (`hinaki`): "a basket-like pot"; its entrance (akura) "a ring of sticks … pointed inwards, touching in the centre, so the eel could push through to enter, but could not get back out"; of mangemange in the north, kiekie roots and aka vine elsewhere, flax for a temporary one; anchored with stones and tied to a stake in the stream [stated: Te Ara "Te hopu tuna — eeling"]. Ours: two or three pots of woven vine, 1.0–1.3 m, bound with hoops, the cone of sticks in the mouth, the far end tied shut, lying on the shore by each fish rack [inferred: sizes, places; Rotorua's eels — §4b].
- **Kurī** (life `animal: dog`): "Kurī were small, long-haired dogs about the size of a border collie", with "a small head, pricked ears, a terrier-like snout", heavy shoulders, short legs and a bushy tail; coats black, white, mixed, or yellow [stated: Te Ara "Kurī — Polynesian dogs"]. Ours: four on the tihi (one on the marae ātea between hosts and visitors, the walk kept clear) and about a dozen in the kāinga, black, white and yellow-tan [inferred: places; the shared dog figure stands in for the kurī's coat].

## 4d. Fourth pass: the inside of the common house, hot ground, household work, living systems

- **Common house inside** (`whare` rank 0, `commonHouse`): earth floors covered with fern and flax mats; an interior hearth with a passage past it; sleeping places down both sides of the single space; a low door (kūwaha); some houses with a window (matapihi); some with earth banked against the walls; no chimneys before the 1880s, so smoke leaves by the door and thatch [stated: Te Ara "Māori housing – te noho whare"]. Ours: a mat floor, a stone-ringed hearth with ash and brands, a fern-and-rush bed with folded cloaks along each wall, a box and kete at the back, kete hung from the rafters, earth banked on the outside of the side walls [inferred: the furnishings' number and places].
- **Pātaka side panels**: carved slabs on both side walls between the posts [inferred from the pātaka's richly carved fronts, Te Ara "Māori architecture"].
- **Hot ground** (`ngawha`, smoke `steam`): food cooked in a flax basket lowered into a boiling pool, in steam boxes and vents, "for hundreds of years" at Whakarewarewa, on Rotorua's south shore [stated: whakarewarewa.com "Geothermal meals"; Te Ara "Cooking food in hot springs"]. Ours: five vents on the flat between the hill and the lake, two or three with a sinter-rimmed pool, steam box and kete on a cord; others only steam [inferred: places].
- **Household work in the kāinga**: before every few houses a cooking place with kāuta, a weaver, a fish rack, or a man at his door; flax at each house's corner, eel pots by the wall [inferred; types as §4b].
- **Living systems**: walkers (`movers.js`) in kilt of flax strands, cloaks of fibre and dog skin, women in wrap and shawl, bare feet, kete on the head, men with a pole of fish, elders, children, men with the taiaha; ways up the path, round each terrace, the foot, the shore and the gardens [inferred from Cook/Banks/Parkinson 1769 as in §4a]. Smoke: hearths, the summit's hāngī, vents, lake mist. Fauna: eels and fish in the shallows, welcome swallows, a white heron and an egret on the shore; no crows or sparrows, which came with Europeans [stated: Te Ara "Birds"; the egret stands for the kōtuku].
- Sources added: [Māori housing — Te Ara](https://teara.govt.nz/en/maori-housing-te-noho-whare/print), [Whakarewarewa geothermal meals](https://whakarewarewa.com/experiences-traditional-food/).

## 4e. Fifth pass: the pā filled — houses by footprint, doors you can walk through, household things, trees, the way in

Brought toward the Suzhou standard (docs/suzhou-method.md). What was below it: about 90 houses, sparse on the terraces and scattered on the flat; every common house solid (its 0.64 m door too narrow for the walker, its inside shut under a roof the walker could not stand in); bare fronts; no trees but flax; one canoe party; few people at the arrival.

- **Number and grouping.** Missionaries recorded pā with one or two hundred houses [stated: ruapekapeka.co.nz, §1]; now about 195 houses, 75 pātaka and 70 pits. On the terraces the houses stand in two rows along each ring, their doors toward the summit, each laid by its true footprint with its porch yard; outside the ditch the kāinga is grouped in about twenty whānau compounds, three to six houses round one yard with its cooking place (hāngī), a pātaka and a pit at its rim [inferred: the grouping, from the open-village pattern in Te Ara "Māori housing" and from the household work of §4b].
- **Walking in.** The door is now 0.95 m (was 0.64 m, after the doorway recorded as low and narrow, Te Ara "Māori architecture") so that a visitor can enter; the inside of the thatch has an underlay so that the roof is a ceiling over the space [inferred: a compromise for the walker; the historical door was smaller]. The headmen's houses (rank 1) are furnished like the common ones; the chief's house has, besides the carved walls, sleeping places with folded cloaks, a carved box (waka huia) on its stand, dog-skin cloaks on pegs, a taiaha on the back wall and kete hung from the rafters [inferred; sleeping places down both sides, Te Ara].
- **Household things at every front** (`yard`): firewood stacked against stakes; a line between two posts with cloaks of flax fibre drying, their borders of taniko in black; kete of kūmara and fern root and a wooden bowl; gourds (hue) on the ground and on a stake; an adze leaning on a stump with a beating stone and bundles of fern root; strung fish on a small gallows; a windbreak of reed between stakes (pākaru); a mat with bowl, kete and carved box [stated: dried fish on racks, Te Ara "Drying fish"; kete, §4b; inferred: the forms and places].
- **Wall materials.** Walls of raupō, toetoe or nīkau [stated: Te Ara, §4a]: about two houses in five have the paler woven wall (`reed_mat` stands in for toetoe/nīkau), the rest raupō.
- **Trees.** Cabbage trees (tī kōuka, Cordyline australis) and tree ferns (ponga) drawn at 3–7 m and 1.6–5 m: a stout pale trunk forking into a few limbs, each with a drooping head of strap leaves; a slender dark trunk with a crown of arching fronds. Cabbage trees along the lake shore, round the compounds and lining the way in; tree ferns behind the compounds, along the ditch's bank and on the terraces' scarps with flax [stated: both are native to the North Island; inferred: their places — a search of Te Ara pages found no statement of their siting in a pā].
- **Carved posts** (`pou`): four on the marae ātea and two pairs on the path where it comes through the gardens, red ochre with a figure at the head [inferred from the pou whakarae of §4a and the boundary posts known from ethnography].
- **The way in.** One arrives at the gardens' end; the clear corridor through the plots (x −11…10) now has four gardeners' huts facing the path, a fish rack and a store, cabbage trees along both sides, gardeners squatting at their plots in the lanes between them, two men and a kurī by the path [inferred; gardeners' huts: temporary shelters at the gardens are recorded for kūmara growing, Te Ara "Kūmara"].
- **Terraces and compounds, people.** A person at about every third door on the terraces, a cooking place on some, women and children at each compound's yard, a man at work, a dog [inferred].
- **The lake.** Four canoes out on the water, three small fishing canoes with two paddlers each and a waka taua with eight seated; drawn up on the shore the six already there [inferred: the numbers; Parkinson's engraving §4a for the waka taua].
- **Ground.** Trodden bare earth round every house and across each yard, the grass between [inferred]. Planting keeps off it.
- **Fauna.** More fish in the shallows, swallows over the gardens and each kāinga, three herons and egrets by the shore (kōtuku, §4d).
- **Sound.** The chief's house is a hall room (voices, flute with reverberation); the summit and the rest open.

## 4f. Sixth pass: the arrival on the summit, spirals and manaia, the working summit, the gates

Brought toward the Suzhou standard. What was below it: the arrival stood in the gardens 20 m outside the outer gate, the view filled by a 4 m palisade; no life in the frame; the summit (the place the pā is for) was bare apart from the welcome and one oven; the carving had no spirals; the ground was one grass.

- **The arrival** (`src/planet/arrivals.js`): inside the summit's gateway (0, 33) facing the chief's carved house across the marae ātea, the pōwhiri under way at the left (the visitors' body 5–10 m off, the challenger and the hosts beyond), the cooking place, the gong and the carver at the right [inferred: composition].
- **Spirals and manaia** (`spirals.js`): the koru / pītau, "the unfurling fern frond", and the manaia, the bird-headed profile figure, are named by Te Ara as common motifs of Māori carving [stated: Te Ara "Māori art" — spiral forms, manaia]; the carved maihi of Te Arawa houses are cut in runs of pītau [inferred: after museum maihi]. Ours: the lower half of each maihi in a run of alternating pītau, the upper half still in rauponga; a manaia either side of the chief's door; pītau along both faces of every gateway's lintel; a pītau and a manaia down each standing pou [inferred: places and proportions].
- **The carver** (`whakairo`): a post for the marae being cut — foot in bark, the shaft adzed square, the head blocked out, chips, adzes on a stump; a man kneeling at it, an old man and a boy by [stated: carving with stone adzes and chisels, Te Ara "Whakairo"; inferred: the stage and the placing].
- **Cloak rack** (`kakahu`): cloaks of flax fibre and dog skin hung to air with their borders, as the cloaks of §4b/§4e [inferred: the rack].
- **The gong** (`pahu`): the alarm gong, a hardwood slab hung from a frame of two carved posts, a mallet on a cord [inferred: from the pahu of museum collections; no source read for its siting in a pā].
- **Stones and spears** (`kamaka`; stage detail): heaps of throwing stones by every gate and on the fighting stages, spears racked against a post and leaning at the stage's breastwork [inferred: that a pā's defenders kept stones and spears at the gates and stages — the fighting stage is the stated defence, §1].
- **Summit life**: five children at a string game between marae and oven, others running; the carver's party; two more storehouses (carved like the first, each with its yard of firewood, kete and gourds); cabbage trees and tree ferns at the rim; sentries at the gates; at each terrace gate an old man with a kurī and a woman with her kete.
- **Ground**: the marae ātea, the work places and the gates' surrounds beaten bare; a worn lane along each terrace behind the stages [inferred].
- **Houses**: a common house's plain bargeboards and a ridge-board standing out over its porch; about one roof in four of straw-coloured thatch beside the raupō [inferred: variation].
- **Life**: walks to the carver, the gong and the weavers; swallows low over the marae [stated: swallows, Te Ara "Birds"].

## 4c. Inside the houses and the fronts (round 5)

[inferred, after Te Ara "Raranga — Māori weaving", "Hī ika — fishing", "Toki — adzes" and the museum models of whare] Every ordinary whare of the pā (105 within 120 m of the arrival, all with a door a visitor can walk through) is furnished for the household's trade: the weaver's frame with the warp hanging, muka bundles and a stack of mats; the fisher's net over the rafters, paddle, gourd floats and eel pot; the carver's adzes on their shelf, a slab half cut and the shavings; the cook's kete of kūmara, calabashes and hāngi stones. ## 4g. Seventh pass: lashed palisades and stages, tukutuku panels, canoe sheds, storehouses, pits, roof frames, gardens

## 4i. Brought to the reference (Suzhou): the walk, the night, the sound, the animals, the wear

Living culture (DESIGN §7): only what is publicly documented; no sacred chant, named song, ancestor or tapu place is imitated or entered. The shell could not reach Te Ara or other pages in this pass, so every line below is [inferred] unless it names a source already cited above.

**Walk.** The visitor's walk runs from the arrival inside the summit's gateway (0, 33), across the marae ātea, to the chief's carved house and down the path through the terraces' gates. Twelve routes in the landmark plan (`plan.js`, `walks`) go from the way in before the door of each of the ten ordinary houses nearest the arrival, and of the chief's house, through the door to the hearth: the houses already stood open to a walker (§4e, §4c) and now are held so by `walk-check`. The ordinary house's household and trade, its furnishings, the yards, the cooking places, the weavers, the carver, the gong and the gates' stones were built in the passes above (§4b–§4h) [inferred: nothing further added to the town's geometry in this item].

**Night** (`nightlight.js`). What lit a pā at night was fire: the hearth of each house, whose smoke and glow left by the door and the thatch because the houses had no chimneys [stated: Te Ara "Māori housing – te noho whare", §4d, no chimneys before the 1880s]; the cooking places' fires and the glowing hāngī pit [stated: Wikipedia "Hāngī", §4b]. Ours: through the door and the window of each lit house (nine in ten) a warm sheet, the ball of the hearth and its pool on the mat, the thatch over the hearth faintly lit by the smoke, a spill on the earth before the door; the chief's house the same, larger; the open pit of each hāngī with the glow of its fire and the kāuta's floor; a watch fire inside each of the three upper gates and the outer gate for the sentries [inferred: that sentries kept a fire; the stages are the stated defence, §1]; a torch (rama) of bound dry flax or reed at the bow of each of the three small fishing canoes, with its reflection on the lake [inferred: torch-fishing at night on a lake is a recorded Māori practice, not read in this pass; the war canoe carries none]. No lamp, no candle, no glass; the moon and the stars are the sky's. Every glow is the `halo` material, at night only, no light of its own, so the day view is unchanged and the low preset shows it.

**Sound** (`soundmap.js`, `src/engine/voices-maori.js`). Placed where made: the karanga, the woman's call of the welcome with an answering call, and the waiata after the speeches, on the marae ātea [stated: the karanga, whaikōrero and waiata of the pōwhiri, Te Ara "Te kawa o te marae", §4b]; the pūkāea (wooden trumpet) from the summit's gate, the kōauau and the pūtōrino (flutes) in the evening [stated: the instruments, §9 above; inferred: the hours and the places]; the stone adze (toki) on the canoe being hollowed and at the carver's post [stated: adzes, Te Ara "Constructing a waka", §4b]; flax stripped and plaited at the weavers' places and in the weavers' houses [stated: weaving, §4b]; fern root pounded with a beater at the cooking places in the morning [inferred]; children at their string game [inferred]; the hearth's pops in every house; steam and boiling pools at the vents of the hot ground, all day and night [stated: the cooking pools, Te Ara, §4d]; the lake on its shingle; paddles of the fishing canoes by day and night; by day tūī and korimako (bellbird) in the cabbage trees and tree ferns, at dawn and again at dusk; by night the ruru (morepork) and, far off, the kiwi [stated: native birds, Te Ara "Birds", §4d; inferred: the hours]. The houses one can be inside have their own hush, their trade's sound (weaver's strokes, the carver's adze, the cook's beater) and the thatch's soft rain. All synthesised, the pitches and patterns ours; no recording of a pā of the 1770s exists. No bell, no rooster, no horse, no cat, no sparrow.

**Animals** (`fauna.js`, `critters`). Only what Aotearoa had before the Europeans (§4d): the kurī (Polynesian dog) lying in the porch of six of the houses nearest the arrival and at the cooking places of the five nearest whānau yards, on the dog's rig so that it lifts its head and stands as the visitor nears [stated: the kurī, Te Ara "Kurī — Polynesian dogs", §4c; inferred: the places]; the grey duck (parera) in two flocks on the lake's shallows off the beach, clear of the canoes [inferred: the species' presence on Lake Rotorua not checked; the model stands in for the brown duck]. Not added: cat, goat, pig, hen, rooster (brought by Cook and after). Left to the older static figures: eels and fish, herons and egrets.

**Anachronism found, left in place.** The dossier (§4d, §4f) lists welcome swallows (warou), citing Te Ara "Birds"; that bird colonised Aotearoa from Australia only in the twentieth century, so it did not fly over a pā of the 1770s. Not changed here (it is the dossier's stated choice and the flights are in `fauna.js`); the next pass should replace the swallows with the small native birds (pīwakawaka, the fantail, as a near-ground flyer) and say so.

**Wear and writing** (`wear.js`). On each ordinary house: a tongue of soot on the front wall above the lintel where the hearth's smoke leaks out, and the earth before the sill trodden dark in the porch by bare feet; at each hāngī the ground blackened with ash and char round the open pit [inferred: the effects; all in `bark_dark`, a material the plan already holds]. Not added: moss and damp on the thatch and the palisade (no material for it; the palisade's five thousand posts are instanced). Writing: none — the Māori had no script before the Europeans; the record was carved, woven and sung, drawn in the carved fronts, tukutuku and kōwhaiwhai of §4a–§4h. No inscription, sign or tablet is invented.
