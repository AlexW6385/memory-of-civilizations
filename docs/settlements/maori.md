## 1. The real example

- **Pā:** fortified Māori settlements, often hillforts with palisades and defensive terraces [stated: Wikipedia "Pā"], almost all on prominent raised ground, especially volcanic hills [stated: Wikipedia]. The simplest had one palisade and elevated fighting stages [stated: Wikipedia]. Late 18th-century pā had extensive earthworks: storage pits, ditches, earth ramparts and multiple terraces [stated: Wikipedia]. Ōtūmoetai pā (Bay of Plenty, 1769) had ditches, banks, palisaded ramparts and fighting stages on several terraces [stated: the-history-girls].
- **Inside:** most pā had a tihi, a flat summit where important people lived, with terraces for buildings and rectangular or round kūmara pits [stated: LEARNZ]. The chief's house on the summit could be about 4.5 × 4 m [stated: Wikipedia text]. Common houses stood on the lower or outer land, sometimes sunk 30–40 cm into the ground [stated: Wikipedia]. Pits were about a metre deep under pitched roofs [stated: LEARNZ]. Missionaries recorded pā with one or two hundred houses [stated: ruapekapeka.co.nz].
- **Moment shown:** the later 18th century, before muskets changed the pā's design [stated: LEARNZ].

## 4. The style

Palisades of close-set split posts with taller carved posts (red ochre); fighting stages on posts; houses with low walls, steep gabled roofs of raupō thatch running down low, a front porch under carved bargeboards meeting at a carved face; pātaka raised on posts; kūmara pits under low roofs; carved gateways. ## 4a. Detail of the carving, the houses, the canoes and the gardens

- **Era check.** The fully carved meeting house (whare whakairo) appeared after contact, in the mid-19th century, and is "not an ancient form of architecture"; earlier houses were small, often partly sunk, with a low door and few windows; the wharepuni (from about the 15th century) gained a front porch and was usually unadorned unless it belonged to a leader, when it might have a carved pare, tekoteko or poutokomanawa [stated: Te Ara "Māori architecture"]. Cook's crew in the 18th century saw houses about 10 m long, entirely carved [stated: Wikipedia "Wharenui"]. So only the chief's house is carved; common houses are plain [inferred].
- **The carved house's parts** (`whare`, rank 2): koruru at the gable's point (the ancestor's head); maihi, the diagonal bargeboards (arms), their ends the raparapa ("fingers"); amo, the uprights holding the maihi's ends; paepae, the threshold beam on the ground at the front; poupou, carved slabs lining the porch's walls and the inside; heke, the rafters (ribs); tāhuhu, the ridge beam (backbone); poutokomanawa, the central post; the kūwaha (door) with its pare (lintel); windows [stated: Wikipedia "Wharenui"]. Tukutuku reed panels between the poupou [inferred: their use in the period; the `koshi` lattice material stands in]. Sizes: 5 × 9 m, walls 1.6 m, porch 1.6 m deep, door 0.64 × 1.15 m [inferred]. Flax mats on the floor and a hearth of stones inside the door [stated: earth floors covered with flax mats, Te Ara; the central fire, The History Girls].
- **Common houses:** a wooden frame covered with raupō, toetoe or nīkau; small, low door [stated: Te Ara]; side walls carried forward to make the porch, a door left of centre and a small window [inferred].
- **Carved figures** (`figure`: post, slab and face): the head about two-fifths of the figure, slanting eye rings inlaid with pāua, the mouth open with the tongue out, three-fingered hands on the belly, bent legs, haehae grooves [inferred from museum carvings in the Te Arawa style; pāua stands in as `glaze_green`]. Painted with kōkōwai (red ochre) [inferred].
- **Palisade:** the pou whakarae is the prominent, intricately carved post of a pā's palisade [stated: Wikipedia "Pou whakarae"]. Ours: every ninth post, 0.44 m thick, crowned by a 1.3 m figure looking outward [inferred].
- **Pātaka:** raised above the ground, often on one or more poles, reached by a removable ladder or ramp, richly carved especially round the entrance [stated: Te Ara]. Ours: four posts, a board deck, board walls, carved maihi, amo, koruru and paepae, a notched pole [inferred]. Food was kept in raised storehouses [stated: The History Girls].
- **Waka taua** (Parkinson's engraving, 1769): the tauihu (prow) with a large figurehead; the huge taurapa (sternpost); the sides (rauawa) finely carved and lashed to the hull [stated: Te Ara "Waka taua, 1769"]. Ours: 10–16 m, hull hollowed with rocker toward the ends, ochred washstrakes, thwarts and paddles, a prow board with a forward-looking figure and two pierced spirals, a 2.9 m sternpost with two spirals [inferred: proportions]. The feathers on the sternpost are left out (no material).
- **Kūmara gardens** (`mara`): mounds (puke) of loosened soil in rows or a quincunx; sunny, northerly slopes preferred; gravel and sand added to heavy soils; simple fences as windbreaks and against pūkeko [stated: Te Ara "Kūmara"]; carved god sticks (atua kiato) round the gardens [stated: The History Girls]. Ours: 80 fenced plots of 18 × 12 m, mounds 0.75–0.9 m across at 1.25 m spacing, a god stick with a small figure at two corners [inferred].

## 4b. Second pass: the welcome, the cooking place, flax and weaving, fish racks, the canoe builders, the stages manned

- **Pōwhiri on the marae ātea**: the marae ātea is the courtyard in front of the meeting house; the welcome begins once the visitors (manuhiri) have assembled, with the karanga, a call from a woman of the hosts (tangata whenua) answered by one of the visitors, and the visitors move onto the marae as the calling goes on; for a particularly important visitor a ritual challenge (wero) comes first, the challenger laying down a baton that the guest picks up; speeches (whaikōrero) follow, each with a waiata, and the hongi at the end [stated: Te Ara "Te kawa o te marae"]. In the pā the open space before the chief's house on the tihi serves [inferred: the formal marae with a wharenui is a later form, §4a]. Ours: before the chief's house the hosts — seven old men seated in a row on a bench of split logs (`paepae`), nine women standing with the caller among them, six more behind; the visitors in a body at the summit's gateway, ten women in front, twelve men behind, their chief at the front in a white dog-skin cloak; between them the challenger with his taiaha, a dart (rākau) laid on the ground before the visitors' chief [inferred: numbers; women in front of the visitors and the seating as now commonly practised]. The walk up from the gate is left clear.
- **Hāngī and kāuta** (`hangi`): a pit is dug and stones heated in it with a large fire; the embers are removed, the food set on the hot stones, covered and sealed with earth, and cooks for two to four hours; before European contact food was wrapped in bark and large leaves (wire baskets came in the 1800s) [stated: Wikipedia "Hāngī"]. Ours, on the east of the tihi: an open pit ringed with thrown-up earth, a fire of logs heaped with basalt stones; a second oven already closed, a low earth mound with the leaves showing round its foot; flax baskets of kūmara and fern root; a wooden bowl of water; firewood stacked; a cooking shelter (kāuta), a lean-to of raupō on four posts with a back wall [inferred: the layout; the kāuta as the cooking shed of the period]. Four women at it.
- **Flax and weaving** (`harakeke`, `weaving`): a pā harakeke is a stand of flax, cultivated or natural, cropped by weavers, the leaves cut so as not to harm the centre shoot, the edges and midrib stripped, the fibre (muka) drawn with a shell's edge; weaving was mainly the women's work [stated: Te Ara "The pā harakeke"]. Ours: seventy flax clumps (fans of stiff, keeled leaves bending over at the tip, flower stalks on some) on the lake shore; on the west of the tihi three weavers' places, each a mat (whāriki) half plaited on the ground with the loose strips at its working edge, bundles of prepared strips tied at one end, a small basket, a woman seated at it and sometimes a second squatting by [inferred: the arrangement].
- **Fish racks** (`whata`): Māori dried fish, a stingray among them, on elevated racks [stated: Te Ara "Drying fish" — watercolour of Aorere, Golden Bay, early 1840s]. Ours: three racks of posts in pairs, two rails a side at 2 m and 1.3 m, split fish hung over them, on the lake shore by the canoe builders, a woman at one (lake fish and eels in Rotorua) [inferred: the form].
- **The canoe being hollowed** (`waka_build`): a big stone adze on a long shaft swung by several men felled the tree; an ordinary stone adze chipped out the wood between the grooves; fire was used too [stated: Te Ara "Constructing a waka", after A. H. Messenger's sketch]. Ours: an 11 m trunk on three skids on the shore, its top adzed flat and its outside shaped, hollowed from the stern for half its length, the bow still solid; chips round it; three adzes (toki) with basalt blades lashed to bent handles; three men at it, stripped to the belt [inferred: the stage of the work, finishing at the village].
- **The stages manned and the taiaha** (`taiaha`, `rakau`): fighting stages are the pā's stated defence (§1). Ours: a warrior on every other stage looking out, stripped to the belt and kilt (drawn as a short skin-coloured garment with a flax belt), a taiaha — the long staff with its blade at the foot, the head at the top and its collar of white dog hair — upright in his hand [inferred: the dress and the weapon from museum taiaha].
- **People's dress**: cloaks of flax fibre (pale gold), of dyed fibre (brown, black) and of dog skin (white, brown) for the chiefs, worn over the shoulders; men's hair in a topknot, women's long [inferred].

## 4c. Third pass (L3): rauponga on the carved fronts, eel pots, kurī

- **Rauponga** (`rauponga` in src/styles/maori/index.js): the surface pattern "consists of a row of dog-tooth notches (pakati) on each side of which are parallel grooves (haehae) and ridges (raumoa)"; in some districts it "is used on pataka (storehouses) and only rarely on other buildings" [stated: Te Ara 1966, "Maori art"]. Ours: the faces of the maihi carved in two bands of rauponga — two ridges each side of a row of teeth pointing alternately either way — on the chief's house and on every pātaka (whose front is the same builder); a band along the paepae; bands either side of the face on the pare [inferred: the bands' places on the chief's house, which the period's carved leader's house may have had — §4a]. The plain raised rib on the maihi is gone. Spirals (pītau) and manaia are still to come.
- **Hīnaki** (`hinaki`): "a basket-like pot"; its entrance (akura) "a ring of sticks … pointed inwards, touching in the centre, so the eel could push through to enter, but could not get back out"; of mangemange in the north, kiekie roots and aka vine elsewhere, flax for a temporary one; anchored with stones and tied to a stake in the stream [stated: Te Ara "Te hopu tuna — eeling"]. Ours: two or three pots of woven vine, 1.0–1.3 m, bound with hoops, the cone of sticks in the mouth, the far end tied shut, lying on the shore by each fish rack [inferred: sizes, places; Rotorua's eels — §4b].
- **Kurī** (life `animal: dog`): "Kurī were small, long-haired dogs about the size of a border collie", with "a small head, pricked ears, a terrier-like snout", heavy shoulders, short legs and a bushy tail; coats black, white, mixed, or yellow [stated: Te Ara "Kurī — Polynesian dogs"]. Ours: four on the tihi (one on the marae ātea between hosts and visitors, the walk kept clear) and about a dozen in the kāinga, black, white and yellow-tan [inferred: places; the shared dog figure stands in for the kurī's coat].

## 4d. Fourth pass: the inside of the common house, hot ground, household work, living systems

- **Common house inside** (`whare` rank 0, `commonHouse`): earth floors covered with fern and flax mats; an interior hearth with a passage past it; sleeping places down both sides of the single space; a low door (kūwaha); some houses with a window (matapihi); some with earth banked against the walls; no chimneys before the 1880s, so smoke leaves by the door and thatch [stated: Te Ara "Māori housing – te noho whare"]. Ours: a mat floor, a stone-ringed hearth with ash and brands, a fern-and-rush bed with folded cloaks along each wall, a box and kete at the back, kete hung from the rafters, earth banked on the outside of the side walls [inferred: the furnishings' number and places].
- **Pātaka side panels**: carved slabs on both side walls between the posts [inferred from the pātaka's richly carved fronts, Te Ara "Māori architecture"].
- **Hot ground** (`ngawha`, smoke `steam`): food cooked in a flax basket lowered into a boiling pool, in steam boxes and vents, "for hundreds of years" at Whakarewarewa, on Rotorua's south shore [stated: whakarewarewa.com "Geothermal meals"; Te Ara "Cooking food in hot springs"]. Ours: five vents on the flat between the hill and the lake, two or three with a sinter-rimmed pool, steam box and kete on a cord; others only steam [inferred: places].
- **Household work in the kāinga**: before every few houses a cooking place with kāuta, a weaver, a fish rack, or a man at his door; flax at each house's corner, eel pots by the wall [inferred; types as §4b].
- **Living systems**: walkers (`movers.js`) in kilt of flax strands, cloaks of fibre and dog skin, women in wrap and shawl, bare feet, kete on the head, men with a pole of fish, elders, children, men with the taiaha; ways up the path, round each terrace, the foot, the shore and the gardens [inferred from Cook/Banks/Parkinson 1769 as in §4a]. Smoke: hearths, the summit's hāngī, vents, lake mist. Fauna: eels and fish in the shallows, welcome swallows, a white heron and an egret on the shore; no crows or sparrows, which came with Europeans [stated: Te Ara "Birds"; the egret stands for the kōtuku].

## 5. The town

About 130 houses, a dozen pātaka and the pits, on the terraces and in the kāinga. [inferred; within the range recorded]

## 6. Ground and water

Grass and bracken on the terraces; bare earth on the path; kūmara gardens in mounds; reeds and scrub by the lake (mānuka stands in as scrub). [inferred]

## 7. Sky and climate

Mild and changeable, with showers. [inferred]

## 8. Paths

From the gardens up the south face, through the outer palisade and each terrace's gateway to the tihi. [inferred]

## 9. Sound
