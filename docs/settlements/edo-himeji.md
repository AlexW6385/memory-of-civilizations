## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** Himeji Castle on Himeyama hill, 45.6 m above sea level [stated: Wikipedia]; rebuilt by Ikeda Terumasa 1601–1609, who added three moats [stated: Wikipedia]; the Nishinomaru added by Honda Tadamasa 1617–1618 [stated: Wikipedia].
- **Moment shown:** about 1620, after Honda's works.

## 3. Orientation

The castle faces south: the main gate and the high-ranking residences on the south side [stated: MLIT]; the keep cluster on the hill's top.

## 4. The style

White plaster walls (hence "White Heron Castle") [stated: Wikipedia]; grey tiled roofs, hip-and-gable (irimoya) at the top, triangular gables (chidori-hafu) on the lower roofs [inferred from the castle as it stands]; stone ramparts with the curved batter (ōgi-no-kōbai) [inferred]; plastered earthen walls with tile copings (dobei).

**The main keep inside (L3, built).** Five storeys outside, six floors and a basement inside [stated: Wikipedia; himejicastle.jp]; the basement, about 385 m², in the stone base, with lavatories, a drain board and a kitchen [stated: Wikipedia]; two great pillars, east and west, rising from the foundation to the bottom of the sixth floor [stated: web-japan], about 24.5 m and about 1 m across, the east one of fir [stated: ermakvagus]; weapon racks (bugukake) on the floors, the first floor once holding over 280 matchlocks and 90 spears [stated: photoguide.jp; rss pplpod]; 605 gun racks in the complex [stated: jpsearch]. The Osakabe shrine on the top floor was moved there only after the Second World War [stated: MLIT], so it is left out. Ours: the base is a stone ring round the basement, entered by a door on its west side from the courtyard between the keeps [inferred: the real entrance is from that side through the basement]; a stone sink in the basement; each storey hollow, the walls plastered between posts, barred windows lit from outside, boarded floors on exposed joists, a ring of posts 1.85 m in from the walls marking the warriors' corridor (musha-bashiri) [inferred], gun racks between the windows on the three lower storeys, steep open stairs from floor to floor on alternating sides, the two great pillars from the basement to the top storey's floor (our five storeys stand for the six floors).

**The castle's exterior details (L3, built).**
- *Windows:* the keep's and turrets' windows are dark openings in a plastered frame standing out of the wall, filled with thick plastered vertical bars (renji windows) [inferred: the castle as it stands]; the hollow keep now has them outside too, at the places of its lit windows inside.
- *Shachi:* the ridge ends carry ceramic shachi — creatures with the head of a tiger and the body of a fish, set in pairs at the ends of the ridge as a charm against fire, their upturned tails part of the keep's silhouette [stated: MLIT "Shachi"]; eleven on the keep, the two on the top ridge the largest [stated: MLIT "Castle Craftsmanship: Tiles"]; the present pair about 1.9 m and 300 kg, after a design of 1687 [stated: MLIT "Shachi"]. Ours: 1.9 m on the main keep, smaller on the small keeps; standing on the head, the body arched up, the tail flung forward over it, dorsal spines, side fins, an open jaw, in dark tile [inferred: the form].
- *Gables:* the roofs combine the curved kara-hafu and the triangular chidori-hafu [stated: World History Encyclopedia]. Ours: kara-hafu — a bow of tiled roof rising in the middle and turning up at its ends, plastered under, a plastered bargeboard and gable face, a pendant (gegyo) under the crown — on the second roof's south and north faces; chidori-hafu with straight, slightly hollowed rakes, a bargeboard, pendant and demon tile on the first and third roofs [inferred: placement].
- *Stone-drop bays:* the lowest storey has large drop openings in overhangs at the corners [stated: World History Encyclopedia]. Ours: eight plastered bays standing 0.7 m out near the corners, tiled on top, open below [inferred: sizes].
- *Loopholes:* 997 loopholes in the walls — tall rectangles for arrows, squares, circles and triangles for guns — at different heights for standing, kneeling or lying [stated: MLIT "Defending the Castle: Loopholes"]; rectangles for arrows and triangles for guns [stated: World History Encyclopedia]. Ours: the castle walls (not the residence's) have them through the plaster every 3.6 m, in a repeating triangle–square–slit–circle pattern, at kneeling height [inferred: spacing and order].

**The way into the keeps (built).** The four keeps are joined by narrow passages (watari-yagura) "completing an enclosure containing a courtyard" [stated: Agency for Cultural Affairs, nomination text]; the castle had 84 gates, 21 survive, 13 named with the letters i, ro, ha, ni… [stated: ermakvagus]; the "water gates" (mizu-no-mon) follow one another, six of them, to the keep's entrance [stated: tabi-mag "Mizu-no-ichi-mon"]; the route is made to wind, the 130 m in a straight line from the Hishi gate to the keep becoming a path of 325 m [stated: ermakvagus]. Ours: the courtyard between the keeps is entered by a gate through the ground storey of the south-west passage (between the west small keep and the main keep), where the steps from the Honmaru come up — plastered piers, the storeys carried over it on a lintel, two dark posts and a head beam with iron bands at the outer mouth, two leaves of planks sheathed in iron strips with rows of bosses standing open, a plank ceiling [inferred: one gate standing for the last of the water gates; the real sequence is not modelled]. From the courtyard the door in the keep's stone base leads to the basement and the stairs to the top floor; every floor is checked as reachable.

**The Hishi gate (built).** The largest of the 21 surviving gates, the turret gate (yagura-mon) at the entrance of the Ninomaru, reached after the Ōtemon and the Sannomaru; white plaster over all, leaving the posts, tie beams and lintels bare; a guard room on the west; the upper storey of three rooms for the garrison; on its front a row of warrior windows (musha-mado) flanked by bell-shaped windows (katōmado), from Zen architecture, finished in black lacquer with metal fittings; the name from the carved wooden diamond (hishi) crest on the head beam (kabuki) over the two posts [stated: tabi-mag "Hishi-no-mon"]; the posts and doors of zelkova (keyaki), the back with a double row of warrior windows, the doors studded with iron strips and boss fittings (suji-gane, manjū-kanamono) [stated: kojodan]; a two-storied turret gate with a hip-and-gable roof, heavy timber doors, guard stations inside [stated: JAANUS "hishi-no-mon"]. Ours: 20 × 8 m, the passage 5 m wide through the middle, standing at the head of the way up into the Ninomaru [inferred: its place in our smaller layout — the real gate stands lower, at the foot of the climb]; the ground storey 4.4 m on stone footings, the guard room on the west with a barred window onto the passage; the dark frame of posts, tie beams and a head beam carrying two diamond crests (a gilt diamond on a wooden one) over the main posts; the leaves open inward, banded with iron strips and bosses; the upper storey 3.8 m on a tiled band, four warrior windows between two bell-shaped windows on the south, two rows of six on the north; a hip-and-gable roof with shachi [inferred: sizes, the gilt of the crests].

**Turrets (yagura, built).** Turrets, gate buildings and plastered earthen walls surround the keeps "positioned according to strategic defensive planning" [stated: Agency for Cultural Affairs]; more than sixteen survive [stated: ermakvagus]. Ours: each turret's ground storey has one stone-drop bay (ishi-otoshi) on every face, set between its first and second windows; the lower roofs carry a triangular gable (chidori-hafu) on their north and south faces against the storey above; the top ridge has a pair of shachi [inferred: from the keep's own vocabulary as it stands].

### 4.1 The arrival yard (deepening pass, 2026-11-12)

- **Ramparts:** the walls are laid in courses (ishigaki of fitted granite, the corners in long-and-short sangi-zumi), the faces curving steeply at the top and spreading at the foot (ōgi-no-kōbai, "fan slope") [stated: Wikipedia "Ishigaki"; MLIT]. Ours: the Honmaru's and the keep base's ramparts coursed 0.6 m in three tones (grey, lighter and darker granite) with dressed blocks standing a hand out of the face so the courses read from 30 m; the Ninomaru's and Nishinomaru's the same in their upper 9 m [inferred: the tones and sizes].
- **The wall on the keep base:** the keep cluster is ringed by plastered earthen walls with loopholes on the edge of its stone base [stated: Agency for Cultural Affairs, nomination text: turrets, gates and walls surround the keeps]. Ours: a white dobei with its loopholes round the base's edge, broken on the south by a plain kabuki gate — two stout posts, a head beam with a gilt crest, banded leaves open inward, a tiled gable [inferred: the gate's form] — at the head of the stair from the Honmaru.
- **The Honmaru's south yard:** raked pale gravel with flagstone paths, pines, stone lanterns, a notice board, a hall for the guards and the lord's reception (west of the stair) and two storehouses; guards at the stair's foot, a servant sweeping, two retainers in talk, a gardener pruning, a porter with pails, a groom with his horse; kites over the keep, swallows and sparrows in the yard. [inferred: the people and the furnishing, from the castle's functions]
- **The climate:** drier weights (clear 4, cloudy 2, drizzle 0.7) and damp 0.2, the whitewash being renewed (the castle was replastered as needed) [inferred].

## 5. The town

Samurai residences: walled compounds with a gate-house (nagaya-mon), a main house under an irimoya roof, a storehouse. Merchants' machiya: two storeys, narrow fronts of wooden lattice, a pent roof between the storeys, tiled gable roofs with the ridge along the street. [inferred from Edo practice; All About Japan for proportions]


### 5.1 Houses open inside (L3, 2026-11-07; `src/styles/edo/rooms.js`)

- **The machiya as a shop:** the shop (mise) usually occupied the front part of the house; the earth-floored area (doma), commonly called the tōri-niwa, was the through-passage to the area behind the house; behind it one or more rooms with raised timber floors; the earliest surviving machiya (Kuriyama house, Gojō) dates from 1607 [stated: JAANUS "machiya"]. The tōri-niwa ran from the street front to the rear of the plot, could be divided by intermediate doors, its front part the retail zone (miseniwa), its rear the domestic service zone (okuniwa) usually open to the rafters, the smoke of the cooking range escaping by a louvre [stated: JAANUS "tooriniwa"]. Ours (one machiya in three, `open`): the front open between its posts, a long indigo noren of three panels over the passage's mouth and a short one along the shop front, each panel with a white crest; the sign board (kanban) on its post at the corner; the earth-floored passage from the street to the back; the shop on its raised floor of mats with their dark borders, the sliding doors to the room behind (two pushed aside); the trade — bolts of cloth on tall shelves and unrolled before a customer, ceramics on stepped stands, or rice in straw bales (tawara) with a measure; the counter's corner closed by a low lattice screen (chōba-gōshi), the desk with a ledger and an abacus, a brazier; at the rear of the passage the clay cooking range (kamado) with its iron kettle and lidded rice pot, the great water jar, shelves of bowls; the ceiling of joists and the boards of the floor above; the shopkeeper kneeling at the counter, a customer sat on the shop's edge in some. [inferred: sizes, the trades and their goods, the furniture]
- **The samurai house's reception room** (one compound in six): ours, in the main house facing the gate, behind its paper doors slid away from the veranda, with a stepping stone before it: a room of mats under a board ceiling, the alcove (tokonoma) with its raised floor, lacquered edge and post of natural wood, a hanging scroll and a bronze vase with a branch; the staggered shelves beside it; plastered walls; the armour on its lacquered chest (the cuirass laced in red, the skirt of plates, the helmet with its gilt crest); the sword rack with the long and the short sword; a standing paper lamp (andon), a cushion, an armrest and a tray. [inferred: the room after the shoin style as generally known; the rest of the house kept closed]

### 5.2 The street and the avenue (L3, 2026-11-07)

- **The kago:** about 0.9 m long, hung from bamboo uprights under one large overhead pole, roofed, with screens for shade or rain; carried by men at each end of the pole, four taking turns; not to be confused with the more elaborate norimono of the samurai and the wealthy [stated: Wikipedia "Kago"]. Ours: on the avenue from the main gate, the basket seat, its bamboo uprights, the black pole, a light roof of reed mat and the blind rolled up; a bearer under each end, the passenger sat in it, a samurai walking after.
- **Samurai:** a 1629 edict required the daishō to be worn on official duty; it was limited to the samurai class in 1683 [stated: Wikipedia "Daishō"]. Ours: samurai in dark robes with their topknots on the avenue and in the merchants' street, the long and the short sword thrust through the sash at the left hip, black scabbards, the hilts bound in white, gilt guards. [inferred: the way of wearing]
- **Pedlars:** men with carrying poles in the merchants' street — pails of water or soy, baskets of greens and fish — some under straw hats. [inferred]
- **Weathering:** the climate set damp (0.6). [inferred]

### 5.3 The merchants' fronts and the town's detail (batch pass, 2026-11-11; `src/styles/edo/town.js`, `layout.js`)

- **Sake brewer:** a ball of cedar (sugi) leaves, the sugidama or sakabayashi, hung from the eaves to signal that new sake is ready; green when new (February to June), light green in early summer, brown and withered in autumn [stated: Wikipedia "Sugidama"]. Ours: one brewer on the deep north row of the merchants' street, a green ball on a cord before the entrance, komodaru (barrels in straw mats and rope) stacked by the door; the shop open inside with casks on a rack and bottles on a shelf. The brewery's tall chimney is left out as a later feature [inferred].
- **Teahouses:** two on the street (one on each side of the middle): a bench with red felt cover, the big red parasol, a kettle on a brazier before the front; inside, a raised bench with red felt, a low table, cups on it, the kettle, a menu board [inferred: Edo-period roadside teahouse types as generally known].
- **Fronts by trade:** closed machiya fronts have a plinth, posts, close-set lattice (kōshi) over a dark recess, the entrance bay with an indigo, brown or black noren swaying, and the trade's sign and goods — dyed cloth hung from a pole, pots, rice bales, oil barrels, straw sandals, a gilt medicine board — a water tub, a paper lantern, a projecting sign board [inferred: types of Edo townsmen's trades; machiya form after JAANUS "machiya", §5.1].
- **Upper storey:** plastered windows with dark slats set in a thick plaster frame (mushiko-mado) over the pent roof; the ridge with a capped course and end tiles; a dark eave band on the pent roof [inferred: the machiya as it survives].
- **Storehouses (kura):** thick earthen walls, plastered, fireproof; spread in Edo after the great fires [stated: web-japan "Kura"]; namako-kabe — square tiles set in the wall with raised plaster in the joints [stated: web-japan "Kura"]. Ours: dark tile base with white seams on the lower 1.35 m, white plaster above, small barred windows high on the gables, a capped ridge [inferred: heights].
- **Samurai gate-house (nagaya-mon):** the front with two posts and a head beam over leaves of planks banded with iron and bossed, a crest on the beam, barred windows either side above a tile-and-plaster base [inferred, from the Hishi gate's vocabulary §4 and the nagaya-mon form].
- **Gardens:** a pruned pine (niwaki), a stone lantern (ishidōrō) and a well with its tiled roof and bucket in the yard before the main house [inferred: Edo samurai garden furniture].
- **Wells and moat:** wells at intervals in the merchants' street; stone landing steps down to the middle moat with moored flat-bottomed boats beside them [inferred]. The moats stand in for the town's canals: Himeji's were its moats, which averaged 20 m wide [stated: ermakvagus; §2].
- **Ramparts:** corners laid in sangi-zumi — more precisely cut stones stacked in an alternating, brickwork-like formation; the uchikomi-hagi wall rises in a gentle fan-like curve [stated: MLIT "Castle Craftsmanship: Stone Walls"]. Ours: long-and-short blocks of lighter granite on every corner, a course to about a metre, the curve in up to 16 courses [inferred: the block sizes].

### 5.4 The moving life (`movers.js`)

Townsmen in kimono and obi with the topknot, samurai in dark robes, women in kimono with a wide obi, pedlars and porters with carrying poles and straw hats, labourers, children; in the rain the straw cape (mino) and hat. The avenue from the main gate, the lanes between the samurai blocks and the merchants' street are the ways; the street is the busiest [inferred; the daishō and the pedlars as in §5.2].

### 5.5 Animals (`fauna.js`)

Carp in the moats, house swallows over the moats and avenue, sparrows on paving and the wall copings, crows over the keep at twilight, a heron on the inner moat's bank and an egret at the middle moat [inferred].

### 5.6 Smoke and steam (`smoke.js`)

Kitchen smoke from a share of the machiya and samurai houses at the meals, steam from the teahouses' kettles, the brewer's steam, mist on the moats at dawn [inferred].

### 5.7 Deepening pass (2026-11-11): enterable compounds, civic furniture, life at eye level
