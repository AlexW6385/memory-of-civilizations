# Joseon Hanyang (Gyeongbokgung), c. 1888 — dossier

Settlement id: `joseon-seoul` · Style: `joseon` · Region: Korea (Hanyang, under Bugaksan) · Code: `src/settlements/joseon-seoul/`
Status of this dossier: L2–L3. **Moment: the 1880s, after Gyeongbokgung's reconstruction of 1865–68** (the owner's decision, 2026-11-07; §12). Gyeongbokgung on its north–south axis: Gwanghwamun on its stone base, Heungnyemun, the Yeongjegyo bridge over the palace stream, Geunjeongmun, Geunjeongjeon on its two-tier platform within its cloister, Sajeongjeon, Gangnyeongjeon, Gyotaejeon, Amisan's terraces and chimneys; Gyeonghoeru of 1867 on its 48 square and round pillars in its walled pond; Hyangwonjeong on its island in Hyangwonji with Chwihyanggyo; Geoncheonggung; the palace wall and corner gates; the offices along the avenue south of the gate; Jongno's shops and bell pavilion; hanok quarters round the palace with their yards, verandas and crock terraces; the street life of late-Joseon Hanyang; ground, planting, weather, music. L2–L3 detail in §10, the throne hall and court in §11, the 1880s in §12. Recorded for later: the bronze dragons in Gyeonghoeru's pond, Jagyeongjeon and its flower wall, the royal kitchens and the eastern palace, Okhoru's upper storey, the electric lamps of 1887.

## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** Gyeongbokgung, built 1395 by Taejo, enlarged under Taejong and Sejong [stated: historyinfograph; LLMpedia]; the gates Gwanghwamun, Heungnyemun, Geunjeongmun, Sinmumun, Geonchunmun [stated: Wikipedia]; walls of 2,404 m [stated: theseoulguide].
- **Moment shown:** the 1880s, under Gojong (about 1888): the palace as Heungseon Daewongun had it rebuilt in 1865–68, with Hyangwonjeong and Geoncheonggung added in the 1870s–80s (§12). (Until 2026-11 the settlement showed about 1430 under Sejong, with Gyeonghoeru as enlarged in 1412 [stated: JoongAng Daily]; that version is described in §11's notes.)

## 3. Orientation

South-facing on its north–south axis, Bugaksan behind. [stated: axial planning, LLMpedia; inferred: the mountain]

## 4. The style

Timber halls on granite platforms; roofs of grey tile with gently curved ridges and upturned eaves, hip-and-gable; columns red, brackets and eaves painted in dancheong (greens, blues and reds) [stated: nomadotravel]; walls of plaster and stone. Hanok: L- and U-shaped wings round yards, tiled roofs for the better houses, thatch for the poorer, walls of stone and plaster on a stone base. [inferred] Geoncheonggung (1873) was built in the manner of a scholar-official's house, its timber left unpainted (§12).

## 5. The town

Hanok in walled yards along lanes; the six ministries' compounds along the avenue south of Gwanghwamun, which ends at Jongno, the bell street, with the licensed shops along it. [inferred] The 1880s town in §12.

## 6. Ground and water

The valley at the foot of Bugaksan, rising to the north; the palace stream and the pond. (`ground.js`) [inferred]

## 7. Planting

Pines and willows in the palace (the pond's edges), flowering trees on Amisan; pines and maples round Hyangwonji, lotus in it, grass on its island; pines on the slopes to the north; a few trees in the yards. [inferred]

## 8. Sky and weather

Clear autumn, misty spring, snow in winter. Latitude 37.6° N. [inferred]

## 9. Sound

Court music (aak): bells and stones, the haegeum's bowed voice, the daegeum flute (a plucked string, the ney and a reed organ stand in). [inferred]

## 10. Carved and glazed detail (L2–L3)

- **Japsang:** small figures along the hips, believed to guard the wooden buildings; "the number of Japsang is always an odd number, ranging from 3 to 11"; the first named after characters of *Journey to the West*, Tang Sanzang leading [stated: Asia Society]. "Generally five, but seven at Geunjeongjeon, nine at Sungnyemun, and eleven at Gyeonghoeru — the most of any building in Korea" [stated: Seoul Mediahub]. Ours: Geunjeongjeon 7 on each hip of both eaves, Gyeonghoeru 11, Heungnyemun and Geunjeongmun 5, Gwanghwamun 7 [inferred], the monk leading, in grey fired clay.
- **Ridges:** the ridge sides plastered white (yangseong) with the dark tile cap above, and the chwidu at the ridge's ends [inferred: photographs of Gyeongbokgung's roofs].
- **Haetae:** the haechi, one-horned beasts at Gwanghwamun, said to judge right from wrong [stated: Google Arts & Culture]. Ours sit in granite on pedestals before the gate, facing south.
- **Cheonrok:** "four animal-shaped statues made of stone" overlook the water beside Yeongjegyo, watching for evil coming in by the stream [stated: Dragon Travel Mag]. Ours crouch on the banks at the bridge's corners, horned and scaled along the spine [inferred: form]. The bridge carries a granite balustrade with lotus-bud posts [inferred].
- **The platform (woldae):** its two tiers carry "carved animal ornaments, including 12 Chinese zodiac signs" [stated: JoongAng Daily]; the animals of the Twelve Earthly Branches and the Four Guardians appear at Geunjeongjeon [stated: Google Arts & Culture]. Ours: granite balustrades with lotus-bud posts, small seated beasts on the posts at the corners and beside the stairs (generic, not each of the sixteen) [inferred: placement], the platform itself now in granite rather than marble. Bronze tripod burners (jeong) on the upper tier and fire-scaring basins (deumu) on the lower [inferred: photographs].

- **The upper storey of the double-eaved halls** (Geunjeongjeon, Gwanghwamun): white plaster between dancheong rails — a sill, rails above and below the band of lattice windows, the painted head beam — on red posts, no longer the Qing palace's red wall [inferred: photographs of Geunjeongjeon; the owner's survey note].
- **Moru dancheong:** Joseon palace exteriors and throne halls are painted in moru dancheong, "meoricho patterns at each end of a beam", the middle of the beam left "with lines only" [stated: The Soul of Seoul]; the plainest grade a single base colour, green or reddish-brown [stated: ibid.]. Ours: at both ends of every bay of the beams under the eaves (lower and upper storeys), bands of vermilion, white, blue, white and ochre with a white-edged red flower, a white line along the green middle [inferred: the order of the bands].
- **Dapo bracket sets:** under the eaves of Geunjeongjeon (three tiers, two sets between each pair of posts), Sajeongjeon (three tiers), Gwanghwamun, Heungnyemun and Geunjeongmun (two tiers), each a bearing block and tiers of crossed arms — lateral arms along the wall, tongues stepping out tier by tier with a little block on each tip — on a plate over the beam, under an outer purlin, with painted boards between the sets; the eaves raised by the bracket band (about 1.2 m) [inferred: the dapo system of Joseon palace halls as photographed; colours — green arms, blue tongue tips, vermilion blocks — inferred]. Gangnyeongjeon and Gyotaejeon have brackets on the posts only (two tiers) [inferred].
- **No main ridge (muryang):** Gangnyeongjeon and Gyotaejeon are "the only two main halls in the palace that lack a top roof ridge, known as a yongmaru (dragon ridge)", left off because the king himself was the dragon [stated: Imperial Tours]. Ours: hip-and-gable roofs whose two slopes round over into each other at the top, the gable boards plastered, the hips capped dark over white-plastered sides, five japsang on each hip, no chwidu.

## 11. Inside the throne hall, Gyeonghoeru, the court (L3)

- **Geunjeongjeon, inside.** Behind the throne "a picture showing a red sun and a white moon, above five mountains", with a waterfall, the ocean and pine trees; "on the ceiling is a watchful gold dragon" [stated: ibike]. The ceilings of Geunjeongjeon (and Junghwajeon) show "two yellow dragons flying in the sky", and "yellow dragons are depicted on both the ceiling and the canopy over the king's throne" [stated: Google Arts & Culture]. The screen (Irworobongdo): five peaks, a red sun and a white moon, two waterfalls, a pair of pines; it stood behind the throne in the throne halls [stated: Wikipedia]. Ours: the throne's platform 5.4 × 4.2 m and 1.2 m high, red with a painted moulding and railing, one stair in front [inferred]; the throne in red lacquer with gilt dragons along its rail, a footstool [inferred]; the screen in three leaves (the side ones turned forward) — blue sky, five green peaks outlined darker, two waterfalls, the red sun on the east (the viewer's right) and the white moon on the west, a red-trunked pine on each side leaf, waves with white crests at the foot [inferred: the sun's side, the leaves]; the canopy (dangga) hung under the ceiling over the throne: tiers of little red bracket arms on dancheong frames, a blue inner ceiling with a gold dragon, the eave frame and a gilt valance of pendants [inferred: form]; the coffered ceiling in dancheong 6.5 m over the floor, raised in the middle of the hall in three steps to a blue field with two gold dragons flying round a pearl [inferred: form]; the inner columns of the two middle lines left out before the throne [inferred].
  - **The moment.** The earliest written evidence of such screens in the palace dates from the mid-17th century, and a 16th-century painting shows no screen behind the throne [stated: Wikipedia, after the National Museum of Korea and Yi Song-mi]. The screen was anachronistic for the earlier moment (1430); with the moment now in the 1880s (§12) it, the two-tiered platform with its zodiac animals and the present hall (all of Gojong's rebuilding) are consistent.
- **Gyeonghoeru as in 1430 (superseded, §12).** "During the reign of King Taejong, there were 48 stone pillars carved with wriggling dragons"; "under the reconstruction [1867], square pillars were erected outside, while circular pillars were erected inside" [stated: JoongAng Daily]. "It was first completed in the 4th month of 1412"; "the original form was smaller than the current, although it had a two-story roof rather than the current single-story roof"; repaired in 1429; destroyed in 1592; rebuilt in 1867; the upper floor's "three levels of differing heights" are those of the rebuilt pavilion [stated: Wikipedia]. Ours, for 1430 (built until 2026-11, now replaced by the 1867 pavilion of §12): 48 round granite pillars on square plinths under capital blocks, each with a dragon coiled up it in relief, its head reaching out near the top [inferred: the carving's form]; the upper floor flat, open on all sides, with a red railing of posts, sill, balusters and a round rail; the roof in two tiers — a skirt of eaves round the upper floor, then a raised storey of white plaster between dancheong rails with a band of lattice over the inner bays, under the hip-and-gable roof [inferred: the form of the "two-story roof", on the model of Geunjeongjeon's upper storey]; eleven japsang kept on the hips (that number is recorded for the 1867 pavilion) [inferred]. The size is kept (the 1412 pavilion was smaller, by how much unknown).
- **The court in session.** "The posts indicate where each rank is to stand" [stated: ibike]. Court robes: the red jobok "worn at large national events", with the gilt coronet (geumgwan) whose gold lines mark the rank; by a rule of 1744 "1st~4th ranks wear Jobok and 5th~9th ranks wear black Dallyeong" [stated: Google Arts & Culture]. Ours: the civil officials on the east, the military on the west of the royal path (as the rank stones in plan.js), two officials at each of the first four rank stones in red with gilt caps, up to three at each lower stone in black with black hats, all facing the throne [inferred: the 1744 rule, in force in the 1880s; the figure library's soft cap stands in for the coronet and the gauze hat]; guards in indigo with black felt hats and grounded spears, two by two at Geunjeongmun and Gwanghwamun [inferred: dress].

## 12. The 1880s: Gojong's palace and Hanyang (L3)

**The moment.** Gyeongbokgung, burnt in 1592, lay in ruins until the regent Heungseon Daewongun had it rebuilt for his son King Gojong in 1865–68; Gyeonghoeru was rebuilt in 1867 [stated: Wikipedia, Gyeonghoeru; JoongAng Daily]. Hyangwonjeong was long dated 1867–73; timber cut in 1881 and 1884 now dates it to about 1885, and its name first appears in the *Seungjeongwon ilgi* in 1887 [stated: Aju News 2021; Korea Times 2021]. Chwihyanggyo is given as 1873 [stated: Wikipedia, Hyangwonjeong]; a study judges the bridge and pavilion to belong to the time Geoncheonggung was founded [stated: Koreascience, the Chwihyanggyo study]. Ours: **about 1888** — after the pavilion and the first electric lamps (1887), before Jibokjae was moved into the palace (1891) and the queen's murder (1895) [inferred].

**Gyeonghoeru of 1867.** "Seven rooms in the front and five on the side", two storeys; "square pillars on the outside and round pillars on the inside"; the first floor paved with square stones, the second with wooden floors, its heights varying so that officials sat by rank; on a square island of long smooth stones joined to land by three stone bridges [stated: JoongAng Daily]. 48 stone pillars; 35 rooms; the upper floor in three levels of differing heights, the highest, in the middle, the king's; a single roof where the 1412 pavilion had a two-storey roof; the rebuilt pavilion had a wall round its pond (torn down in the colonial period, rebuilt 2004–05); two bronze dragons were set in the pond against fire [stated: Wikipedia, Gyeonghoeru]. Ours (`nu` in `src/styles/joseon/gojong.js`): 34.4 × 28.5 m [inferred: the commonly published size], all 48 grid points carrying a granite pillar — the 24 of the outer ring square and tapering a little under a moulded cap, the 24 inside round, plain (no dragons) [inferred: plain, from photographs]; painted girders both ways over them; the upper floor's three levels a step (0.24 m) apart, the second ring and the 3 × 1 bays of the middle; red posts at every grid point; the lattice doors (bunhap) of the second ring hooked up under the ceiling, as in summer [inferred]; the railing (posts, sill, balusters, bracket arms, round rail) round the edge; ikgong brackets on the posts only, moru dancheong on the head beams [inferred: the bracket type]; one great hip-and-gable roof, white ridges, chwidu, eleven japsang. The island's granite balustrade with lotus-bud posts and beasts at the ends, open where the bridges land; the three bridges as granite decks on piers with balustrades [inferred: from photographs]; the wall round the pond with three gates on the east facing the bridges and one on the north [inferred: the gates' places]. The bronze dragons are left for later.

**Hyangwonjeong and Hyangwonji.** "A hexagonal, two-story pavilion" on an island in Hyangwonji, a square pond, where the king and queen rested; six columns; stone plinths and base blocks; restored 2018–21 with 799 wooden piles under it; the finial (jeolbyeongtong) restored from old photographs; ceilings once papered blue; exterior railings [stated: Aju News 2021]. The pond covers 4,605 m²; the floor was heated by ondol [stated: Korea Times 2021]; the flue ran round the outside edge of the hexagon like a doughnut, its fire-hole on the north-west, the chimney low by the island's bank [stated: History Library, after Yonhap 2019]. Ours (`hyangwonjeong`): the hexagon 2.9 m to a corner [inferred], on a hexagonal granite base 0.75 m high with steps north and south; six red posts through both storeys on round plinth stones; below, lattice windows over a plastered dado, a lattice door toward the bridge, an ochre oiled-paper floor; the upper floor carried out 1 m on brackets from the posts, with a railing all round and lattice doors; dancheong on the beams with vermilion and blue bands at their ends; the hexagonal roof swept at the corners and its finial [inferred: proportions from photographs]. The island round, 8 m in radius, its granite bank battered, grass on it [inferred]; the pond ours ~4,350 m² [inferred: to fit].

**Chwihyanggyo.** "The longest wooden bridge built over a pond in the Joseon period", originally north of the pavilion, toward Geoncheonggung; destroyed in the Korean War, rebuilt on the south in 1953, restored to the north in 2021 [stated: Wikipedia, Hyangwonjeong]. The first bridge had a deck with a slight arch on three rows of piers; later it became flat on more rows [stated: Koreascience study]. Before 2021 a flat bridge on stone piers with a wooden railing; now an arched wooden bridge [stated: Aju News]. Ours (`plank_bridge`): 1.8 m wide, a slight arch (0.6 m) on three rows of granite posts with wooden caps, planks, a plain wooden railing, unpainted [inferred: the finish].

**Geoncheonggung.** Built in 1873 in the manner of a yangban residence; Jangandang was King Gojong's quarters, Gonnyeonghap the queen's (Empress Myeongseong, who was assassinated here); Korea first generated electricity here in 1887, lighting electric lamps [stated: Asiae 2023]. Its timber unpainted, Okhoru at the queen's side, the generator fed from Hyangwonji, the murder in 1895 [inferred: general histories, not checked against a source here]. Ours: a walled compound with two south gates and an east gate, a wall dividing the king's side (west) from the queen's (east); Jangandang and Gonnyeonghap with their wings and two service ranges, their timber left unpainted, paper-lattice doors [inferred: plan simplified].

**Amisan's chimneys.** Gyotaejeon's ondol smoke was led out through a network of hexagonal chimneys of orange brick in the Amisan garden, "a different design on each side" (Treasure 811) [stated: David Kennard Photography]. Ours (`chimney`): four, 2.7 m high, on Amisan's second and third terraces: a granite plinth, a hexagonal brick shaft with a moulded footing and a frieze near the top, each face framed in brick round a recessed panel with a design in pale clay relief (roundels, lozenges, a central stem), a beast panel at the foot, a tiled hexagonal cap and a little smoke house with its own roof [inferred: from photographs; the motifs are generic, not each of the real designs]. The terraces (hwagye): four, 0.9 m each, walled in dressed granite with copings and walled at their ends, planted with flowering trees and pines; two stone basins on the first [inferred: from photographs of Amisan].

**Jongno and its shops.** The licensed market of Jongno (yukuijeon) sold six goods — silk, raw silk, cotton, paper, ramie and fish — in government-owned shops by approved merchants; a *haengrang* room behind a shop served as store or dwelling [stated: Korea Times 2012]. The bell of 1468 tolled at the gates' opening and closing, 33 times at 4 a.m. and 10 p.m.; it gives Jongno ("bell street") its name [stated: Wikipedia, Bosingak]. Ours: shop rows (`sijeon`) along the south side of Jongno, east and west of the avenue's end, cut into blocks of eight bays by lanes: a granite footing, a tiled roof along the street, one-bay shops (2.6 m) between plastered party walls, board shutters hooked up under the eave, a raised floor (maru) in front where the merchant sits cross-legged, shelves behind stacked with bolts of silk (coloured), cotton and ramie (white and grey), quires of paper, baskets of dried fish, earthenware [inferred: form from late-19th-century photographs of Jongno]. The bell pavilion (`bell_pavilion`) at the corner: a single-storey hall of 5 × 2 bays, plastered below, lattice above, open to the street on its middle bays, the bronze bell about 3.2 m high hung inside [inferred: form after 1890s photographs; the bell's size from the National Museum's figures].

**The street life of the 1880s.** Women laboured at the white clothes everyone wore ("this wearing of white clothes… entails very severe and incessant labour on the women"); peddlers carried their packs themselves "or employing porters or bulls"; ladies of rank went out by night [stated: Isabella Bird, *Korea and Her Neighbours*, quoted by Textile Ranger]. Bulls hauled heavy loads of brushwood through Seoul's streets, led by farmers and even children; almost every house kept a small dog [stated: Korea Times, "The bane of cyclists in 19th-century Seoul"]. Ours (`town.js` street, `jige`, `ox_load`): men in white coats (durumagi) and black horsehair hats (gat; the figure library's brimmed hat in black lacquer); women in white or indigo under the green cloak (jangot) over the head; children; merchants on their shop floors and buyers before them; porters with the A-frame (jige: two forked uprights, rungs, the prongs that carry the load, straps of straw rope) loaded with pine brush, split wood, a sack or jars, walking with the forked stick in hand or squatting by a frame propped on its stick; oxen under great piles of brush on a pack saddle, each led by a farmer in a straw hat [inferred: dress and loads from photographs; the gat's tall crown is not in the figure library]. The hanok quarters gained (`hanok`): lattice doors papered inside toward the yard, a wooden veranda (toenmaru), the open hall (daecheong) in the main range, damp-greyed foot of the walls, a low chimney, the crock terrace (jangdokdae) with its jars, walls of fieldstone below and plaster above, a roofed gate [inferred: the hanok type].

## 13. A yangban house with walkable rooms (L3)
