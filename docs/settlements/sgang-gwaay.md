Respect. The village is the home of the Kunghit Haida and a World Heritage Site that the Haida Gwaii Watchmen look after; its poles are the memorials and graves of named people. We show a village as it stood before the sickness, and carve nothing that copies a particular crest or a particular pole: the figures on our poles are generic beings of the coast (a bird with a hooked beak, a bear, a whale with a tall fin, a watchman in a ringed hat) in the proportions and the black-and-red colours the art is known by, put in sequences of our own. ## 1. The real example

- **Place:** SG̱ang Gwaay Llnagaay, "Ninstints" in older books, on SG̱ang Gwaay (Anthony Island), at the south end of Haida Gwaii, off British Columbia; 52.0989 N, 131.2175 W [stated: Wikipedia, "Ninstints"]. The planet's table has 52.09 N, 131.22 W. The village lies "in a sheltered bay on the east side of an island" [stated: Parks Canada, "Nan Sdins National Historic Site"], "just west of and facing Kunghit Island" [stated: Wikipedia, "Ninstints"].
- **People:** home of the Kunghit Haida; the southernmost Haida village and the earliest recorded in the south of the archipelago [stated: Wikipedia, "Ninstints"]; "around 300 people when it was inhabited" [stated: worldheritagesites.net, "SG̱ang Gwaay"].
- **Moment shown:** about 1850, before the great smallpox of 1862 that "greatly reduced the population", the village used "mainly as a camp" by 1875, the last people moving to Skidegate about 1885 [stated: Wikipedia, "Ninstints"; archaeology.org, May–June 2026: abandoned in the 1880s after epidemics]. The sea-otter trade of 1787–89 (Dixon, Duncan, Gray, Kendrick) [stated: Wikipedia, "Ninstints"] has left iron and glass beads in the village, a few trade blankets and tools in use; we show a village at the height of its life, houses and poles whole. [inferred: the choice of 1850]
- **What survives, what we rebuild:** "19 cedar-plank longhouses, canoe launches, orchards, a potato patch and ancestral walkways" and "more than 30 ceremonial poles, some over 50 feet tall … mortuary and memorial poles dating to the 18th and 19th centuries" [stated: archaeology.org]; "rows of weathered cedar poles stand on a beach, all facing the ocean. Behind them lie the remains of longhouses, including their outlines and massive corner posts" [stated: worldheritagesites.net]. Early Haida testimony put the longhouses at twenty [stated: Wikipedia, "Ninstints", citing Swanton]. We build seventeen houses in a row, the landmark among them, and some forty poles. [inferred: our count of the surviving 19 pits less the two we leave out for the disc's length]
- **Inscription:** UNESCO World Heritage Site 1981 (ref. 157), National Historic Site "Nan Sdins" [stated: Wikipedia, "Ninstints"].

## 4. The style

### 4a. The house

- **Frame**: "Four main posts are set firmly in the ground. Their backs are hollowed to reduce weight, and their faces are carved. Some larger houses use six posts. The upper ends of the posts are hollowed to hold large horizontal 'plates' … Rafters and frames are beveled to receive wall boards. Roofing is planks or bark slabs, held down by rocks, beams, and cross-pieces. Purlins support the roof planking." [stated: tota.world]. "Haida houses were framed with two or six large beams running lengthwise. The six-beam house was the more common type." [stated: Canadian Encyclopedia]. We build the landmark with six: two heavy beams on the four posts and a lighter purlin each side over the walls. [inferred: choice]
- **Walls and roof**: "walls were made of wide wooden planks, often red cedar in large lengths" [stated: Canadian Encyclopedia]; the roof a low gable of overlapping cedar planks weighted with stones and poles, the ridge about 5 m over the ground at the landmark, the eaves about 2.7 m. [inferred: from the 12–15 m width and the standard low pitch of about 17°; the house is called a "plank house"]
- **The pit**: "most Haida houses are partly excavated into the ground rather than built on raised foundations. The interior has an upper ledge and a lower platform. The fire burns on bare earth or in a box of rocks." [stated: tota.world]; "houses of high-ranking Haida and Tsimshian people had a central fire pit, with steps leading down from the main floor" [stated: Canadian Encyclopedia]. We dig a pit 1.2 m deep with two tiers (0.4 m each): the ledge round the walls at 0.4 m, a lower platform at 0.8 m, the pit floor at 1.2 m. [inferred: the depths; the tiers each within a step]
- **The smoke hole**: "a smokehole sits at the top, with a shutter on a pivot; a rope or chain turns the shutter to face away from the wind" [stated: tota.world]. Ours: a hole about 1.4 × 2.4 m between the two beams, a board shutter on a pole at one side, a rope hanging. [inferred: size]
- **People**: "some houses held 30 to 40 people, including a chief, his family, grandchildren, and relatives' families" [stated: tota.world]; "likely held 30 to 50 people from multiple families … ceremonies, food preparation, meals, weaving, and carving" [stated: archaeology.org]. [inferred: sleeping places for five or six families on the ledge, in compartments divided by plank screens]

### 4b. Interior furnishing (landmark)

- Sleeping platforms along three walls on the ledge, divided into family places by upright plank screens, cedar-bark mats and hides on them; the chief's quarter at the back behind a painted plank house screen with an oval doorway [inferred: Haida practice, from the standard ethnographies (Swanton; Dawson, 1878; MacDonald, *Haida Monumental Art*); not fetched]. Our screen is painted in generic black and red forms, as the poles.
- Bentwood boxes in stacks along the walls: "a kerfed cedar box with a lid" with ridged sides and a raised panel; one in the Haida Gwaii museum measures 28.6 × 39 cm, its lid carved from one slab [stated: Haida Gwaii Museum, "Cedar Bentwood Box"]; we build the large storage kind, about 0.5 × 0.8 m and 0.55 m high, painted [inferred: sizes].
- Dried halibut and salmon on strings from the rafters, cedar-bark capes and spruce-root hats hung on pegs, baskets, a carved dish and ladle, wooden fishing floats, firewood stacked by the hearth. [inferred: the stores of a winter house]
- Found in the house excavated at the village: "bird-bone drinking straws, a carved argillite block, perforated thimbles, glass trade beads, Asian coins and amulets, pigments, grinding stones" [stated: archaeology.org]; we place the pigments (red ochre, black) and a grinding stone by the screen. [inferred: place]

### 4c. Poles

- Types: "carved columns (totemic columns)" in front of each house, "posts with carved faces" (the main house posts), "mortuary and commemorative columns" that "stand in graveyards and scattered through the village, near house corners" [stated: tota.world]. At the village "mortuary and memorial poles", with "eagles, ravens, bears, killer whales, an animal face that may be a wolf, and a human figure" [stated: worldheritagesites.net], "bears, orcas and a thunderbird … signalled lineage, status, or clan crests" [stated: archaeology.org]. Some "over 50 feet tall" [stated: archaeology.org]; ours reach 15 m.
- Our frontal pole (landmark): about 10.5 m, 1.4 m across at the foot where the oval doorway is cut through it, four stacked beings, a watchman in a ringed hat at its head. The others 7–10 m. The mortuary pole: a post 6–8 m with two carved beings and a chest on top (a box with a gabled lid) holding the dead; the memorial pole: a tall freestanding pole, carved its whole length. [inferred: sizes and arrangement; the forms are generic]
- Paint: black (tar_wood) for the formline eyes and outlines, red (kokowai) for the lips, brows and fields, on cedar weathered to silver in the older poles. [inferred: the colours of the known art; the poles at the village have weathered to grey]

### 4d. Canoes

- "The Haida … reputedly made the biggest Pacific Northwest canoes, some 60 feet (18 m) long. Hulls were carved from solid logs, usually red cedar … walls very thin so the sides could be spread" by steaming; bow and stern pieces fitted after spreading; "large travelling and war canoes were often painted with designs linked to the canoe's name or the owner's crest"; "racing canoes carried ten paddlers and a steersman" [stated: Wikipedia, "Pacific Northwest canoes"]. Ours: hulls of 6–14 m with the high carved bow (a separate piece) and a lower stern piece, painted black at the bow with red; the largest 14 m. [inferred: lengths in the village's use]
- Beached on the shore, bow up the beach on logs; a few moored in the shallows; three paddled by day across the bay (the living system). A log being hollowed at the north end of the row. [inferred]

## 5. The town

- The row: seventeen houses, the fronts boarded, weathered by their years (the oldest silver-grey, the newest brown), moss on the roof planks' north edges and on the stones. [inferred: climate; "moss covers the longhouse beams" (worldheritagesites.net) in the ruins]
- At the fronts, in the street between the houses and the water: canoes drawn up, drying racks with halibut and salmon, boxes and baskets, stacks of cedar planks and bark, split firewood, the memorial and mortuary poles between the houses. [stated: tota.world, "the space between the houses and the water works as a street for hauling canoes and drying fish"]
- Behind: a few small sheds and racks, the paths of the "ancestral walkways" [stated: archaeology.org], then the forest. Trails there are lined with white clamshells today [stated: worldheritagesites.net]; in our village the paths are trampled earth. [inferred]
- "Orchards" and "a potato patch" [stated: archaeology.org] belong to the later village (potatoes came with the traders); we leave the clearing behind the houses bare, with salmonberry and fern. [inferred: omission]

## 9. People and sound

- Dress: the cedar-bark cape and skirt, the woven conical spruce-root hat with its painted crest and stacked rings (the potlatch rings), blankets of cedar bark and trade wool for the rich, bare legs and feet; the women with long hair, the men with hair loose or tied. In the rain the hat and the bark cape are all. [inferred: standard ethnography, not fetched; the figure system has 'straw' hat, 'cape', 'wrap']
