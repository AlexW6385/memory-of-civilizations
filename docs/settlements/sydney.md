## 1. The real example

- **Sydney Opera House:** designed by Jørn Utzon and opened on 20 October 1973 on Bennelong Point. Its shells are covered in white and cream tiles and rise to 67 m. It stands on a podium about 183 × 120 m clad in granite, reached by the monumental steps. Two main halls sit side by side (the Concert Hall and the Joan Sutherland Theatre), and there is a restaurant under smaller shells [stated: Wikipedia "Sydney Opera House"].
- **Sydney Harbour Bridge:** opened in 1932. It is a steel through-arch with a 503 m span, 134 m to the top of the arch and 49 m above the water at the deck, with granite-faced pylons at each end. It runs from Dawes Point to Milsons Point [stated: Wikipedia "Sydney Harbour Bridge"].
- **Circular Quay:** the ferry wharves at the head of Sydney Cove, with the Cahill Expressway (1958) above the railway station [stated: Wikipedia "Circular Quay"; "Cahill Expressway"].
- **Australia Square:** a round tower of 1967, 170 m tall [stated: Wikipedia "Australia Square"]. The **AMP Building** at the quay is from 1962, 117 m [stated: Wikipedia "AMP Building, Sydney"].
- **Royal Botanic Garden:** east of the Opera House round Farm Cove [stated: Wikipedia "Royal Botanic Garden, Sydney"].
- **Moment shown:** October 1973, at the opening.

### Detail added (shells, glass, bridge pylons, ferries)

- **Tiles:** the shells carry 1,056,006 tiles in two colours, glossy white and matte cream, made by Höganäs AB. The roof is made of 2,194 precast concrete sections on precast ribs, and each shell is a section of a sphere of 75.2 m radius. The highest point is 67 m above sea level [stated: Wikipedia "Sydney Opera House"]. Built: on each shell the cream joints of the tile lids drawn as chevrons between the ribs, pointing up the shell [inferred: the pattern of the lids; their number is ours].
- **Glass walls:** Utzon planned prefabricated plywood mullions, but "a different system was designed to deal with the glass" [stated: Wikipedia "Sydney Opera House"]. Built: the crude fan of glass that ran to one point on the podium is replaced by a pleated wall hung in each shell's mouth. Its folds run from the shell's edge down to a foot line on the podium that steps in and out every panel; there is a steel mullion on every fold and transoms every 3.2 m [inferred: the folded form from photographs of the walls; sizes ours].
- **Harbour Bridge pylons:** granite-faced pylons at each end [stated: Wikipedia "Sydney Harbour Bridge"]. Built: each pylon a battered shaft in rusticated courses, a cornice, two stepped stages above it, and tall windows high on the faces [inferred: composed from the pylons' Art Deco form; the bridge is at half scale].
- **Ferries:** the Lady class — Lady Cutler (arrived in Sydney 1968), Lady Woodward and Lady McKell (launched 1970; reverse-sloped wheelhouses); Lady Wakehurst and Lady Northcott followed only in 1974–75 [stated: Wikipedia "Lady-class ferry"]. The Manly service kept double-ended ferries and had hydrofoils from the mid-1960s; Baragoola and North Head were retired in 1983 and 1985 [stated: Wikipedia "Manly ferry service"]. Built: a Lady-class ferry (about 38 × 9.6 m) berthed beyond the quay's last wharf and another crossing the harbour, a Manly ferry (about 60 × 11 m, two decks of saloons, a funnel) and a hydrofoil out on the water. The ferries are double-ended hulls with a green hull, a cream strake, white saloons with bands of windows, a wheelhouse at each end and railed upper decks [inferred: dimensions and livery; the green-and-cream is the traditional Sydney ferry colouring].

### 1b. The Harbour Bridge's trusses and deck

- "The arch is made of two 28-panel arch trusses", their depth varying from the centre to the ends by the pylons; "The bridge was designed to carry six lanes of road traffic, flanked on each side by two railway tracks and a footpath"; "In 1958 tram services across the bridge were withdrawn and the tracks replaced by two extra road lanes" — eight lanes, the footway on the east, the cycleway on the west, two tracks of the North Shore line; the hangers carry crossbeams on which the deck is built; "the pylons themselves have no structural purpose" [stated: cavacopedia mirror of Wikipedia "Sydney Harbour Bridge"]. The depths (18 m at the crown, 57 m at the ends) are the commonly published figures; the mirror we could read dropped them [stated elsewhere — to recheck].
- Built (half scale, the arch's span 260 m; true scale cannot fit: the settlement's radius of 460 m is fixed in `src/planet/sites.js`, and the north springing would stand 630 m from the centre): two trusses 15 m apart (true 30 m) of 28 panels, 28.5 m deep at the ends and 9 m at the crown, lower chords springing from pin housings on granite skewbacks, upper chords, diagonals sloping toward the crown, laced verticals, wind bracing at both chords, hangers down to the deck's cross girders and posts up to it near the ends; the deck: eight lanes between the trusses with their lane lines and kerbs, on the west the two railway tracks on ballast and ties under their overhead wire, on the east the footway, cross girders every panel and six stringers under it, railings; the pylons now stand clear of the deck's edges [inferred: sizes halved from the stated ones; the overhead wire's masts ours].

### 1c. Circular Quay's wharves

- The quay "consists of five double-sided wharves at 90 degrees to the shoreline"; from the mid-1960s a pontoon on wharf 2 served the hydrofoils; wharf 3 was rebuilt in the 1980s for the Freshwater class's onboard gangways [stated: cavacopedia mirror of Wikipedia "Circular Quay ferry wharf"]. So in 1973 the gangways were the wharves' own [inferred].
- Built (four wharves still, the cove compressed): each on rows of three timber piles with headstocks and iron braces, fender piles every 2 m and two walings down each side, a plank deck with kerbs, bollards, life buoys; the waiting shed and ticket office at the shore end, an awning on two rows of steel posts with benches; at every berth a gangway on its gallows (two posts, a beam, chains, a counterweight), lowered onto the Lady-class ferry's foredeck at the east wharf and raised at the others [inferred: the form of the wharves and gangways]. The hydrofoil pontoon is not built.
- Built (2026-11-06): the benches under the awnings rebuilt from flat slabs as back-to-back park benches: four timber seat slats a side, a shared back of three slats on a central standard, cast-iron end frames with front legs and arms [inferred: the common public bench of the period; sizes ours]. The people of the quay on a weekday: about 34 groups along the promenade, alone, walking along it, or talking in twos and threes; on each wharf passengers sitting on the benches and a few standing about under the awning. Men in suits or shirt and trousers in grey, black, navy and brown, a few in felt hats and some bearded; women in dresses and skirts in colours [inferred: the city's dress of the early 1970s; Circular Quay as Sydney's main commuter ferry hub, stated in Wikipedia "Circular Quay"].

### 5a. The Rocks' terraces and stores

- Argyle Terrace (1883–84): "two storey houses of five rooms constructed with brick and cement walls and iron roof", stuccoed and painted, a parapet, string courses, "decorative moulded brackets to sills and arched window heads"; 15-foot frontages [stated: NSW State Heritage Inventory, Argyle Terrace]. Playfair Street Terraces (1875–77): "face brick with no extraneous detailing" on sandstone foundations, "cantilevered balconies overhanging a narrow footpath", shingle roofs replaced by 1902 [stated: NSW State Heritage Inventory, Playfair Street Terraces].
- Built: in the Rocks each lot is a short terrace (`rocks_terrace`) of two-storey houses about 4.6 m wide on sandstone footings stepping with the slope; walls of face brick, stucco or dressed sandstone; a panelled door under a fanlight with a sandstone step, sash windows with sandstone sills and lintels, shutters at some; at about half the rows a balcony on iron brackets over the footpath with a cast-iron railing and rosettes under a skillion roof; corrugated-iron roofs with the ridge along the row, gutters and ridge capping, the party walls carried up as fire walls with brick chimneys and terracotta pots; near the harbour, sandstone bond stores of three storeys under a gable with stacked loading doors, barred windows and a hoist beam [inferred: the stores from the Argyle and Campbell's Stores type; sizes ours]. The city's other buildings no longer carry New York's roof water tanks, and their shop fronts face only streets and lanes wider than 3 m.

### 1d. The Concert Hall (2026-11-07)

- The Concert Hall has 2,679 seats and is the home of the Sydney Symphony Orchestra; "Perspex rings (the "acoustic clouds") hanging over the stage were added shortly before opening"; the interiors use "Australian white birch plywood supplied from Wauchope in northern New South Wales" [stated: Wikipedia "Sydney Opera House"]. "The walls, stage and auditorium floor are made of Australian brush box timber"; the ceiling is lined with white birch; each seat has a shell of white birch veneer upholstered in magenta wool; "a set of 21 acoustic clouds hangs above the stage"; the Grand Organ stands above the stage; designed by Peter Hall [stated: Google Arts & Culture, "The Concert Hall" — of the hall today]. The organ's contract was awarded in 1969 and the organ "was completed in 1979, six years after the opening of the building"; Ronald Sharp, 10,244 pipes [stated: Wikipedia "Sydney Opera House Grand Organ"].
- Built: the west hall's enclosure hollow (its south 16 m left solid, where the restaurant's shells come down): side walls and a stepped roof of brush box following the shells' envelope, a ceiling of birch on ribs 1.3 m under it; the floor of brush box; the stage (17 × 13 m, 0.9 m high) near the north end with steps up from the aisle, the orchestra's chairs and stands in arcs round the podium; behind it five choir terraces with seats; on the north wall the organ's case of brush box — seven towers and flats with their openings still empty and only the centre's pipes in, scaffolding before it (the organ being built until 1979); 21 perspex rings hung over the stage; the stalls rising south from the stage in rows of seats (birch shells, wine-red upholstery) either side of a central aisle with stepped birch rails; a door in the east wall from the podium between the halls; ceiling lights [stated: the materials, the rings, the organ's dates; inferred: the plan, compressed to the enclosure's 21 m width (the real hall is wider and seats people round the stage), the case's form, the scaffolding].
