## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** Florence Cathedral, Santa Maria del Fiore. Brunelleschi's dome, built 1420–1436, is about 45.5 m across inside, and the lantern's top stands about 114 m up [stated: Wikipedia "Florence Cathedral"].
- **Giotto's Campanile:** about 84.7 m high and 14.45 m square, in white, green and red marble [stated: Wikipedia "Giotto's Campanile"].
- **The Baptistery of San Giovanni:** octagonal, before the cathedral's west front [stated: Wikipedia "Florence Baptistery"].
- **The Signoria:** the Palazzo della Signoria (Palazzo Vecchio), its tower 94 m high, stands on the Piazza della Signoria with the Loggia dei Lanzi (1376–1382) [stated: Wikipedia "Palazzo Vecchio"; "Loggia dei Lanzi"].
- **The Ponte Vecchio:** across the Arno, lined with shops [stated: Wikipedia "Ponte Vecchio"].
- **Moment shown:** about 1480, under Lorenzo de' Medici.

## 4. The style

White, green and pink marble on the cathedral, campanile and Baptistery; red tile on the dome and roofs; rusticated stone on the Palazzo; plastered houses under tiled roofs. [stated]

**Santa Maria del Fiore inside (L3, built).** A basilica with a wide nave of four square bays and an aisle either side, separated by wide pointed arches on composite piers [stated: Wikipedia]; the clerestory windows are round [stated: Wikipedia]; the interior cold and grey with pietra serena [stated: Horner 1877]. The polychrome marble floor dates from the 16th–17th centuries [stated: buffaloah], so in 1480 a brick floor is shown [inferred]. On the inner façade, Paolo Uccello's painted 24-hour clock of 1443, between the mosaic of the Coronation and the rose window [stated: Opera del Duomo]. The eight round windows of the drum were glazed 1434–45 to designs by Donatello, Ghiberti, Andrea del Castagno and Uccello [stated: Opera del Duomo], and Brunelleschi's choir enclosure stood under the dome [stated: Opera del Duomo]; the Last Judgment in the dome was painted only in 1572–79 [inferred: after our date], so the shell is shown white between its ribs. Ours: the four bays (oblong in our smaller building), piers of pietra serena with half-columns and leaf capitals, the arcades, a walkway on corbels at the springing [inferred from the ballatoio inside], rib vaults, lancets in the aisles where the outer skin has windows; the central west door open; under the dome an octagonal wooden choir round the high altar; the three tribunes with five chapels each, lit by lancets, under ribbed half-domes. Recorded for later: Uccello's Hawkwood (1436) and Castagno's Niccolò da Tolentino (1456) on the north aisle wall, the cantorie of Luca della Robbia and Donatello, the sacristies' doors.

**The Baptistery (L3, built).** An octagon about 25.6 m across, faced with white Carrara and green Prato marble in three horizontal bands: green pilasters below; in the middle three arches on each side with windows under tympana, pointed and round by turns, a stylised arch under each window; the upper band with small windows in the middle of three panels [stated: ermakvagus; Wikipedia via ipfs mirror]. The rectangular scarsella on the west, opposite the entrance, made rectangular in 1202 [stated]. The three bronze doors: on the east, toward the cathedral, Ghiberti's Gate of Paradise (1425–52), ten rectangular panels of Old Testament scenes in five rows, entirely gilt, in a gilt frame of foliage and fruit with prophet statuettes and 24 heads [stated: ermakvagus; Wikipedia]; ours each leaf with five panels, twenty statuettes in niches on the frame and twenty-four heads at its crossings, every panel a scene in low relief of figures before a building or hills [inferred: the scenes generalised]. **The Mercato Vecchio (L2–L3, built).** The market stood on the Roman forum, where the cardo crossed the decumanus [stated: Wikipedia "Piazza della Repubblica"; "Colonna dell'Abbondanza"]. Ours: the column on three steps and a pedestal, a moulded base, a shaft 7.2 m with entasis, a leaf capital and abacus of marble, about 11 m to the statue [inferred: proportions]; the Dovizia about 2.1 m, facing the cathedral, her right hand steadying the basket, the horn of plenty on her left arm [inferred: the pose]; the chains on iron staples on two faces of the pedestal, hanging in swags [inferred]; the market hall (34 × 7 m) of timber posts on stone bases with knee braces, a tiled roof open at the gable ends, a spine of boards with shelves of jars down the middle, and on both sides counters on trestles: butchers' (meat on hooks, a block), fishmongers' (fish laid in rows) and greengrocers' (baskets of fruit and greens) [inferred: from the stated stalls along both sides and the market's trades]; the square itself is a market room, where the life of the streets sets out stalls and people. **A bottega (L3, built: `bottega` in `src/styles/renaissance/craft.js`, `src/settlements/florence/crafts.js`).** Each workshop was a family business run by a master of a guild — painters first under the Arte dei Medici e Speziali; the panel bought from a carpenter, its holes filled, sized, covered with canvas and a thick then a thin layer of gesso, scraped flat; apprentices from about 15 for some seven years, learning to grind, size, gesso, scrape, gild, burnish and temper; grinding pigments took hours or days, colours tempered with egg; Cennini's *Libro dell'Arte* the manual [stated: shafe.co.uk "The panel painter's workshop"]; Leonardo was apprenticed to Verrocchio at 14 or 15 [stated: same]. Ours, after the workshops of Verrocchio's kind that made paintings and sculpture alike [inferred]: a shop 12 by 10 m on the street south of the cathedral's square, open through two round arches of pietraforte with voussoirs, one with the counter on a stone bench outside, the shutters open, a painted sign on an iron arm; plastered walls, a cotto floor, the ceiling's beams; a living storey of stucco with windows and green shutters under a hipped tile roof on rafters. Inside: the altarpiece on its easel (the gesso laid, the gold ground of the upper half, the Virgin's blue mantle and red dress blocked in, her halo); a panel on trestles half gessoed; the grinding table with its porphyry slab and muller, five pigments in dishes and a bowl of egg; a cassone being painted; the sculptor's corner with a marble block roughed out on its banker and a terracotta bust on a turned stand; the long bench with tools under shelves of jars; drawings pinned up; a brazier warming the glue pot; stools. The master at the easel, a journeyman at the grinding table, a boy by the gessoed panel, a client at the door [inferred: the furnishing from the stated practice].

**The dyers and the drying sheds (L3, built: `tintoria`, `tiratoio`).** Huge dyeing vats stood in workshops along the Corso dei Tintori; the wet cloth was washed and hung in drying sheds built along the Arno's banks; dyers and wool workers made up about a quarter of the population; undyed cloth came from as far as France and Flanders [stated: voicemap "Corso dei Tintori"]. Dyes included kermes (grana), madder, brazilwood and woad, and dyed cloth had to be registered with the Arte della Lana [stated: refashioningrenaissance.eu "Tracking down tintori" (on 1550–1650)]. Ours, on the north bank east of the Ponte Vecchio: an open dyers' shed of posts and braces under tile, four copper vats over brick furnaces with their fires, dye in blue, red and yellow, stirring poles, rinsing tubs, dyed cloth over poles; two drying sheds two storeys high (36 by 6 m), open, cloth hanging from their rails in the dyes' colours; bales and crates of cloth and wool, a donkey cart and a wagon; dyers at the vats, porters with bundles, a dyer rinsing at the river with a pole [inferred: forms; the sheds' size].

- **Houses entered.** About a dozen ordinary lots can be entered: an osteria nearest the arrival, homes, and one workshop of each of the lots' nine trades (cloth, wine, bread, spice, leather, smith, goldsmith, woodwork, candles) [inferred: the trades of the quattrocento shop-houses; fittings generalised from guild-trade practice: loom and shelved bolts, oven and kneading trough, forge with bellows and anvil, tallow pot and taper racks]. A stair on the side wall climbs to a bedroom with the great bed with curtains, a cassone, table and prie-dieu [inferred: Florentine domestic inventories name bed, cassone and prie-dieu as the usual furniture].
- **Fronts.** Three manners of window: pointed Gothic for the older houses, round arches with a bifora on the piano nobile for the quattrocento ones, rectangular architraves for plain houses [inferred]; corner quoins and a painted frieze under the eave on some plastered fronts; the family arms (stemma) over doors; iron lantern brackets (fanali) [stated: palace fronts carried torch-holders and rings]; street-corner shrines (tabernacoli) of the Madonna [stated: a Florentine street custom, many survive]. Shop signs carry the trade's emblem on both faces [inferred].
- **Palazzo Medici rooms.** On the ground floor off the court's portico, a sala with a long table, chairs, a hearth, cassoni and hangings, and a studio with lecterns, book chests and a globe [inferred: the family's famous library and collections were in the palace; the ground rooms' exact use is not recorded in our sources]. The chapel with Gozzoli's Magi on the piano nobile stays unbuilt.
- **Palazzo della Signoria.** The great door stands open on a passage to the court Michelozzo remodelled in 1453, with an arcade of slender columns round a paved court and a stone basin [stated: Michelozzo's court of 1453; inferred: proportions]. Verrocchio's putto with the dolphin is NOT shown: it was made for the Medici villa at Careggi and brought to the palace only in the sixteenth century [stated from general art-historical knowledge; not re-verified against a fetched page].
- **The Arno.** Flat-bottomed barges carried goods on the river; washing and fishing from the banks, weirs (pescaie) crossed the stream [stated: Museo Galileo itineraries list the San Niccolo and Santa Rosa weirs; the barge, fisher and washerwoman scenes are [inferred]]. Weirs themselves are not modelled.
- **Planting.** River banks: reeds, willow, poplar; garden walls: fig and olive; cypress and umbrella pine for villas; olive groves and holm oak on the outer ring [inferred from Tuscan landscape painting of the century].
