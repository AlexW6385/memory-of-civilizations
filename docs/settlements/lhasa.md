## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Potala on Marpo Ri, about 3,700 m, 400 m east–west and 350 m north–south, sloping stone walls 3 m thick on average and 5 m at the base [stated: Wikipedia]; thirteen storeys rising 117–119 m on the hill [stated: Wikipedia]; the White Palace lived in from 1649, the Red Palace added 1690–1694 [stated: Wikipedia]; the yellow Deyang Shar court between them [stated: famous-historic-buildings]; colours of red, white and yellow from limewash [stated: cavac (Wikipedia text)]; a principal stair rising from the east, dividing halfway to the White and the Red Palaces; sun and moon towers east and west [stated: ancient-history-sites].
- **Moment shown:** 1694, the Red Palace just finished.

### 1.1 The detail as built (2026-11 pass)

- **Massing.** The palace is not two boxes but many masses at different heights, flat roofs at various levels [stated: NWE "its flat roofs at various levels"; Wikipedia]. Ours: four staggered tiers (frontage z 8–30, then −26, −62, −98), each broken into four to seven masses whose fronts and heights differ by a few metres; the west wing steps down westward, the White Palace east steps down eastward, the Red Palace highest in the middle [inferred from the palace's silhouette].
- **Batter.** Walls slope inward, about 10° in Tibetan building generally [stated: tibettour; Tibetan Buddhist architecture — Wikipedia]; the Potala's walls 5 m thick at the base, 3 m on average [stated: Wikipedia]. Ours: 8 % of the wall height on the palace (≈ 4.6°), less than 10° because our walls stand on the block's whole height, not on the rock [inferred].
- **Red Palace on a white base.** The crimson central member [stated: Wikipedia]; ours red above 50–52 m, white below, in two stages (104 m and 117 m) [inferred from photographs of the palace].
- **Windows.** Straight rows of many windows in the upper parts of the walls, the lower walls blind [stated: NWE, Wikipedia]; small windows, typically 60–80 cm wide and 120–150 cm tall [stated: tibettour]; framed in black [stated: Tibetan architecture (chinese-architecture-jycsd); Tibetan Buddhist architecture — Wikipedia]; formerly lattice with paper, not glass [stated: buffaloah quoting Wikipedia]; the wide central windows of the façade hung with bright valances [stated: Richardson, Tibet Album, Pitt Rivers 1999.23.2.19]; drapes on temple exteriors animated by the wind [stated: Tibetan Buddhist architecture — Wikipedia]. Ours: the black surround a trapezoid wider at the foot and reaching below the sill [inferred: the familiar Tibetan form]; a red frame with lattice bars and a transom; a cornice of a red board and a row of painted beam ends under a dark cap; a pleated cloth valance (shambu) with a coloured band and hem in five colourings; windows growing larger storey by storey, grouped into lit bays and blank stretches, the odd two-light window, three-light windows stacked in the middle of the White Palace east's upper storeys (the Dalai Lama's quarters) [inferred].
- **Pembe frieze.** A dark red band of dyed tamarisk at the top of the wall, wider on important buildings (over 2 m on assembly halls), narrow or absent on village houses [stated: Tibetan architecture (chinese-architecture-jycsd)]; heavy dark friezes round the roof's edge recalling the firewood stacked on peasant roofs [stated: Tibetan Buddhist architecture — Wikipedia]. Ours: 1.3–2.0 m on the White Palace, 2.0–2.8 m on the Red Palace, doubled there with a white moulding between and set with gilt roundels (melong) [inferred from photographs]; 0.6 m on the houses; a white ledge under it and a white coping over it [inferred].
- **Gilded roofs and banners.** The central part terminates in gilt canopies like the Jokhang's [stated: Wikipedia]; gilded roofs on important monasteries [stated: tibettour]; victory banners are cylinders of beaten copper with a parasol on top, set at the four corners of monastery and temple roofs; on the Potala also a trident-topped banner hung with black silk [stated: Dhvaja — Wikipedia]. Ours: five gilded hip-and-gable roofs on the Red Palace on a red drum under a pembe band, a gilt vase finial (ganjira) on each ridge [inferred]; gilt gyaltsen at the drum corners and the Red Palace's roof corners; black cloth banners with gilt trident-flames at the White Palace's roof corners.
- **Stairs.** A series of staircases broken by intervals of gentle ascent leads up [stated: NWE, Wikipedia]; the eastern court (Deyang Shar) is entered by a long, steep triple stair [stated: Richardson, Pitt Rivers]. Ours: two zigzag stairs of four flights each on the frontage's face (west x −140…−100, east x 46…86), the lowest flight outermost, each landing turning back onto the next, flagstone treads between white parapets, 22 m up onto the frontage roof; walkable. The triple stair into the Deyang Shar: §10.
- **Round bastions and walls on the rock.** Sun and moon towers east and west [stated: ancient-history-sites]; ours round, battered, 10 m in radius, with walls stepping up Marpo Ri behind them [inferred].
- **Yellow building.** The yellow building beside the White Palace in the court between the palaces housed the giant banners [stated: NWE]; ours a narrow yellow mass east of the Red Palace to 86 m.

## 3. Orientation

Facing south over the valley, along the east–west ridge. [stated]

## 4. The style

Battered stone walls, whitewashed or limewashed red; rows of windows with black trapezoid frames; parapets with a dark red-brown frieze of tamarisk twigs (pembe); gilded roofs with upturned eaves and victory banners on the Red Palace. [stated: colours; inferred: details]

## 5. The town

Shöl: two- and three-storey whitewashed houses with flat roofs, black-framed windows, prayer-flag poles, inside a wall with corner towers. [inferred] Traditional houses two or three storeys [stated: tibettour]. Shöl held the residences of dignitaries, government offices, courts, the city prison, the revenue bureau, the mint, the western and eastern printing houses, stables, dairies and a granary [stated: Zhol Village]; an "outer Zhol" of low houses lay outside the south wall [stated: Zhol Village].

- **Walls and gates.** Ours: the walls run from the round bastions at the hill's foot south and round; gatehouses (two battered piers and a storey over the passage, windows both ways, a timber lintel) in the middle of the south wall on the street up to the palace, and in the east and west walls on a lane across the village (z 142); square towers at the south corners [stated: three walled sides with three gates; inferred: the gatehouses' form].
- **The open ground below the palace** (z 47–62, between the stairs and the houses): people about in it (the life pass), and the **inner pillar (doring nangma)**, uninscribed, on the village's north border directly beneath the stairs [stated: Zhol Village]; ours at (−4, 54), 6.5 m [inferred height].
- **The outer pillar (doring chima)**, c. 764, bearing the oldest known Tibetan inscriptions, stood outside the south entrance in a small enclosure [stated: Zhol Village; Lhasa Zhol Pillar — Wikipedia], on the south side of the road past the village [stated: Pitt Rivers 2001.59.2.76.1]; a tall pillar with steps at the base and a further base laid in bricks, a stone canopy topped by a carved finial of the flaming wish-granting jewel [stated: Pitt Rivers 2001.59.2.76.1, 2001.59.2.74.2]; a heap of stones and an incense burner beside it [stated: Pitt Rivers 2001.59.2.74.2]. Ours at (−1, 238): a brick base, three stone steps, a 7.5 m tapering square shaft, a collar, a canopy slab and low hipped cap, the jewel finial; the heap of stones and a whitewashed incense burner (sangkhang) to its west [inferred: dimensions, the burner's form].

## 10. Detail added (2026-11 third pass)

- **The Deyang Shar.** "The great eastern courtyard of the Potala, the Deyang Shar. It is entered by a long, steep, triple stair, below which is a well-proportioned flight of stone steps"; the wide central windows of the façade above hung with bright valances; on the north side a long canopy over the monk orchestra's seats with an open parapet above for spectators, a similar parapet on the south with galleries for ladies and guests beneath it; "towering over the east end is a massive three-storied building reached by a long stone stairway from the Sho" [stated: Richardson, *Ceremonies of the Lhasa Year*, quoted by the Tibet Album, Pitt Rivers 1999.23.2.19]; the courtyard painted yellow, dividing the living quarters from the temple [stated: Deyangshar — Wikipedia]. Ours: the court raised on its platform at 70 m east of the White Palace (about 46 × 44 m), a range of galleries along its south side and a long range on the north (both to 78 m), the east building towering to 86 m with windows on both faces and black banners; the White Palace's east faces stand along its west side [inferred: plan and heights]. The two upper masses that stood here before (to 92 and 80 m) are gone.
- **The triple stair.** Three flights side by side, 2.2 m each, rising 14 m over 15.5 m (about 42°) from the court up the White Palace's east face, between four whitewashed walls with dark copings on a solid whitewashed base; at the top a landing running into the wall, a porch of two red pillars under a beam with painted ends, a band of pembe and a white coping, the door hung with a black curtain bordered in white [stated: the triple stair; inferred: its dimensions, the porch and the curtain (as at Tibetan temple doors)]. The middle flight is commonly said to have been the Dalai Lama's [inferred].
- **The great banner (koku).** On the 30th day of the second month, the day of the Golden Procession (Tshogcho Sertreng), "a great appliqué banner which covers the lower face of the Potala for a space of some 75 by 40 feet" was hauled up after the procession; "it consists of two panels, one rather larger than the other", each with "a huge figure surrounded by many deities and bodhisattvas" [stated: Richardson, quoted by the Tibet Album, Pitt Rivers 1999.23.1.14.2 and 2001.59.8.94.1]. Ours: shown hung, as on that day: two panels 7 and 5 m wide, 20.9 m high, on the frontage's blank middle (the windows behind it left out), lying on the battered face; each an indigo field in red and ochre borders, a pleated red-and-white valance and an ochre band under the pole at the top, a green ground at the foot; the seated Buddha in his aureole with a green halo, gilt face and hands, on a lotus throne of white petals, two attendants at his feet, rows of small seated figures in red, white, ochre and green above him [inferred: the composition, colours and proportions; the choice to show it hung].
- **The pond behind the palace.** The lake behind the Potala was "the excavation of mortar for the construction of Potala Palace in the 17th century", which "became the lake"; the Lukhang temple on its small island was built in 1700 by the sixth Dalai Lama [stated: Windhorse Tours]. Ours: an oval pond 136 × 64 m north of Marpo Ri at (0, −292), its water 1 m below the plain over a bed at 2.4 m, the little island left in it without its temple (not yet built in 1694), willows round its banks and on the island [inferred: size, the island in 1694, planting].

## 11. The way up and the Great East Hall (2026-11 fourth pass)

The court at 70 m and its triple stair had no way up to them; now the whole climb is walkable, from Shöl to the roof of the Red Palace's chapels.

- **The ramps up to the court.** "Entry to the Potala is up two steep access ramps"; "the stairs lead past the ticket office to the large Deyang Shar, the external courtyard of the White Palace" [stated: Lonely Planet, Deyang Shar]; outside the White Palace "a 'zigzag' type of mountain road" [stated: slideshare, Potala Palace (machine-translated Chinese guide); bendibao: 白宫外部有"之"字型的上山蹬道]. Ours: from the eastern zigzag stairs' head on the frontage roof (22 m) a landing, then the first ramp — a straight stone stair 42 m long climbing east along the second tier's face to a landing at 44 m; the second ramp in two flights on the second tier's roof turning at a landing (57 m), up to a stair tower before the south galleries at 70 m. Flagstone treads of about 17 cm on solid whitewashed masses between parapets a metre high with dark copings [inferred: routing, dimensions, the parapets as on the zigzag stairs].
- **The gate passage into the Deyang Shar.** "Stairs leading into the Eastern Courtyard" [stated: Tibet Album, Pitt Rivers BMR.86.1.12.1]; a south-eastern entrance of the Potala "the doorway covered with yak hair curtains", monks watching from a balcony above it [stated: Tibet Album, Pitt Rivers 1998.286.30]. Ours: a passage 4 m wide and 3.6 m high straight through the south galleries (now running from the White Palace's face to the east building, closing the gap that stood at the court's south-west corner), framed on both faces by a Tibetan doorway — the black trapezoid surround, red jambs and lintel, a cornice of painted beam ends under a dark cap, a pleated valance, the black yak-hair curtain rolled up under the lintel [inferred: the frame after the windows and temple doors; placement]. The court's south edge now meets the galleries' battered face (a slot ran along it before).
- **The triple stair's head.** "At the top of the triple stairs leading up to the White Palace" [stated: Lonely Planet]; the passage from the court reaches "the East Palace, the largest palace in the White House" [stated: slideshare guide, "through the Dasongge Corridor"]. Ours: the stair's landing now ends at the wall's face, where a doorway opens into a passage 2.4 m wide through the wall to the hall [inferred]. The door that was drawn there before lay inside the battered wall and could not be seen.
- **The Great East Hall (Tshomchen Shar).** The largest hall of the White Palace, on its fourth floor, 717 m² [stated: Travel China Guide; CCTV], 27.8 × 25.8 m [stated: bendibao; slideshare guide]; the Dalai Lamas were enthroned and great ceremonies held in it [stated: Travel China Guide; CCTV]; "inside is the Dalai Lama's throne" [stated: bendibao] (the Tongzhi emperor's plaque over it is of the 1860s, after our moment, and left out); the columns of the Potala's great halls wrapped in cloth [stated: Architecture of Lhasa — Wikipedia, of the Great West Hall]. Ours: the hall hollowed out of the White Palace east at 84 m, 28 × 25.8 m, 5.6 m high; 36 pillars on a 7 × 7 bay grid (count not found in the sources) — red lacquer on stone bases with brocade bands, square painted capitals, short and long splayed brackets carrying red beams with a painted band, painted joists and a coffered board ceiling; a thangka in each bay round the walls, each on its indigo brocade mount with red and ochre "rainbow" borders, the brocade "door" below, a deity on a lotus in a ring of light on green hills under a blue sky, the yellow silk veil rolled at the top with its red ribbons, the stick and the roller with gilt knobs; the throne on the west wall facing the hall — a lacquered dais, stacked cushions of yellow and red, a painted and gilt back, a canopy with a pleated valance; a table of butter lamps before it; the monks' long low red seats in rows between the pillar rows, the middle aisle clear [inferred: everything but size, use, the throne and wrapped columns].
- **On to the roof.** Visitors reach upper floors "by climbing a steep ladder"; one follows "the rooftop corridors from the White Palace to reach the Red Palace" [stated: tibettravel.org]; the Sunlight Hall on the White Palace's top floor, part of its roof open to the sun [stated: bendibao] (not built here). Ours: from the hall's north-west corner a steep wooden stair (about 57°) with heavy stringers, plank treads and red handrails climbs a stairwell to the roof (100 m), where low walls guard the opening; a link building behind the yellow banner store carries one west to a short flight up onto the Red Palace's lower stage (104 m), and a flight against the upper stage's east face climbs to a landing at the roof of the chapels and tombs (117 m) under the gilded roofs [inferred: routing].
- **The Jokhang** lies about 2 km east of the Potala, beyond the settlement's 420 m disc, and is not built.

## 12. One stepped body; Shöl's courtyard houses, the printing house, the people (2026-11 fifth pass)

- **The joints between the masses.** Every mass is battered on all four faces, so where a lower mass met a higher one behind it or beside it the two sloping faces parted into V-shaped slots, from the frontage roof down to the rock (up to 7 m wide between the third tier and the White Palace above it). The palace's flat roofs at various levels [stated: NWE] meet the walls of the higher parts; ours: wherever a lower mass stands against a higher one (behind it, or beside it up to 7 m off), its roof runs on — frieze, ledge and coping with it — until it meets the higher wall: a joint block of the lower mass's height and colours, its sides following the faces of the two masses 0.15 m inside them, its ends buried in both (46 joints, made by the plan from the masses themselves) [inferred]. No window is drawn where a mass, a joint or a stair stands against the face (it lay inside the stair or the neighbour before).
- **The zigzag stairs** stood off the battered frontage with a wedge of air behind the top flight (the face leans back 2 m over the 22 m); the top flight's solid now runs 2.4 m into the wall, the flights overlap each other by 2 cm instead of touching face to face, and their solid masses start 0.8 m below the ground (they began at 0, where the ground under them stands up to 0.2 m) [inferred].
- **Shöl's houses.** Lhasa's house types — the noble house, the residence yard and the merchant's or commoner's house — "generally have at least one courtyard"; the commoner's house "usually two stories high", serving as residence, shop and storage; ground floors "store-rooms and stables"; round the courtyard "a gallery giving access to the rooms on the upper floor", projecting upper-floor verandahs shading the stables; flat roofs, the outer walls sloping inward, "the corners of the roof are elevated"; doors and windows with "thick black frames and little slate roofs" over them; walls more than a metre thick below; adjacent buildings of one, two and three storeys; the third floor of residence yards "always built from mudbricks"; on the roofs "twigs decorated with prayer-flags in five colours"; the Lhasa style refined "from the 17th century onwards" [stated: Tibet Heritage Fund / Lhasa Archive Project, *Lhasa's Historic Urban Fabric*]. Incense is burnt on "a hill or the top of a house" in "a large urn-shaped burner" (sang-khun), juniper and other fragrant wood [stated: Tibetan Buddhist Encyclopedia, Sang-khun]. Ours: each lot a house round its courtyard — the main range on the north facing the sun (3.6 m deep, 4.3 m with a third storey), two side ranges (2.9–3.2 m), all two storeys (6 m) under a narrow pembe band; on the street side a lower wall (3.3 m) with its gate, black-framed doorway and valance, the two leaves standing open; the court pebbled; store-room doors in black frames below and windows above on the court's faces; along the main range's court face a gallery 1.25 m deep at the upper floor on timber posts with painted capitals, a plank floor, a railing of balusters, reached by a steep ladder at its east end; the outer ground floors nearly blind, the upper floors lit, few windows to the north; on the roof the four corners raised as little whitewashed blocks banded dark, each with a bundle of twigs hung with flags in the five colours, the flag pole with its strings, the whitewashed urn of the incense burner over the street, its mouth sooted; in about a third of the wider houses a third storey set back on the main range [inferred: dimensions, proportions, the mix].
- **The printing house (Shöl Parkhang).** "Established around 1690 on the orders of the Fifth Dalai Lama, at around the same time as the construction of the White Palace … a two-story building on the southeast corner of the Shol", for two centuries the main printery of Lhasa, housing woodblocks of the 17th and 18th centuries [stated: Treasury of Lives, Shol Printery]. Of how such a house worked, at Dergé: "racks of thousands of blocks throughout the building" [stated: Rubin Museum, Derge Parkhang]; the printers "sat in pairs in low chairs, facing each other" in a room on the upper floor open to a courtyard, working by sunlight; one coats the block with red or black ink, the other lays a sheet of paper on it and runs a roller over it, then whips off the sheet and puts it aside [stated: Toronto Star, 2017, on Dergé]. Ours: at the south-east corner of Shöl, 42 × 34 m, two storeys (7.6 m) battered, a pembe band of 0.8 m, windows round the outside; the gate through the south range (a passage of 2.8 m framed by Tibetan doorways on both faces) into a pebbled court; the north range open to the court on both floors behind red pillars with painted capitals and brackets: below, the block store with racks along its back and end walls, the blocks lying end-on on the shelves; above, the printing room behind a parapet, racks behind, seven benches each with the block on its sloping board, its inked face red or black, the ink pot, the blank and the printed sheets, the two low seats and two printers facing each other; a steep ladder up from the court; flags, raised corners and an incense burner on the roof [inferred: plan, dimensions, the arrangement after Dergé].
- **The stupa-gate (Pargo Kaling)** — the great chorten straddling the road between Marpo Ri and Chakpori, the western gate leading into Shöl, chained by bells to two smaller chortens on the ridges — is shown on an early 19th-century map and photographed from 1904; its building date is not found [stated: Pargo Kaling — Wikipedia]. Not sourced for 1694; not built.
- **The people.** Monks: the maroon skirt (shemdap), the shirt (dhonka), the maroon shawl (zhen) for everyday and the yellow outer robe (chögu) for formal occasions, the right shoulder bare [stated: Great Tibet Tour, Tibetan Kasaya]; the robe red since Buddhism reached Tibet [same]. Lay people: the chuba, an ankle-length robe of wool or sheepskin, wrapped left over right and bound with a long sash, the excess pulled over the belt as a pocket; married women's striped apron (pangden); otter-trimmed hats from the 1600s [stated: Chuba — Wikipedia]. Pilgrims walk clockwise round the Potala on a circuit lined with prayer wheels and chortens, carrying prayer wheels, beads and incense, some prostrating the whole way [stated: tibettravel.org, Lhasa Pilgrim Circuits]; the hand prayer wheel (mani lag 'khor): a sheet-metal drum, often embossed, on a pin in a wooden handle, a weight on a chain keeping it spinning, turned clockwise with the wrist [stated: Prayer wheel — Wikipedia]. Ours (as still figures, §5): some fifty pilgrims in twos and threes on the circuit outside Shöl's walls and behind the hill — west along the south road, north up the west side, east behind the hill, south down the east side — in chubas of brown, black, grey or sheepskin with coloured sashes, women with aprons, fur and felt hats, most with the prayer wheel raised in the right hand (a copper drum with a silver band on its wooden handle, the weight standing out on its chain); others looking up at the palace below the stairs and at the outer pillar; monks in red with the shawl or the sash, heads shaven, the odd one with the yellow robe, in knots in the Deyang Shar, on the frontage roof looking out, and walking below the palace; fourteen printers at their benches and a monk among the racks [inferred: numbers and placing; the dress as near as the shared figures allow — no maroon cloth, the red stands in].

## 13. Murals, the chapel of the great prayer wheel, the rows of prayer wheels (2026-11 sixth pass, L3)

- **Murals.** The Great West Hall "is noted for its fine murals reminiscent of Persian miniatures, depicting events in the fifth Dalai Lama's life"; the Red Palace holds "numerous chapels and shrines" [stated: Architecture of Lhasa — Wikipedia]. Ours (`mural`, `src/styles/tibetan/index.js`): a wall painted from a red dado with an ochre line up to a band of alternating blue and green blocks under a red frieze; between them rows of scenes about 1.1 m wide, each a deity in a ring of light on a lotus among green hills under a blue sky, small figures above (the composition of our thangkas, painted straight on the wall, without mounts) [inferred: the arrangement of Tibetan chapel walls; the narrative murals of the Great West Hall are not modelled]. Laid on the north and south walls of the Great East Hall behind its thangkas, and in the chapel below.
- **The circuit's prayer wheels.** Pilgrims walk clockwise round the Potala on a circuit lined with prayer wheels and chortens (§12) [stated: tibettravel.org]. - **The chapel of the great prayer wheel (mani lhakhang).** Butter lamps traditionally burn clarified yak butter; pilgrims supply the butter to earn merit [stated: Butter lamp — Wikipedia]; the prayer wheel's drum carries the mantra and is turned clockwise (§12) [stated: Prayer wheel — Wikipedia]. ## 15. Deepening pass (2026-11, Suzhou standard)

Gaps found against Ming Suzhou: Shöl read as detached houses on a grid with 3–7 m of bare ground between them and an empty 20 m band across the middle; the houses' outer walls bare; no goods at the shop fronts; the lanes without life; roofs bare.

- **Shöl's blocks.** Houses stand shoulder to shoulder in rows with lanes of 2.6–3.3 m between them, the one wide lane (7 m, z 138.5–145.5) between the east and west gates, where the gates already stood; rows cut to fit the street's shops and the printing house; about 4.5% of plots left as yards [inferred from the closeness of Lhasa's built fabric: "adjacent buildings of one, two and three storeys", the Tibet Heritage Fund text quoted in §12; the lane widths are ours]. Houses within the farmsteads' ring unchanged (about 90 scattered farmsteads, spacing 26 m).
- **Dung cakes on the walls.** Cakes of yak dung are slapped on whitewashed walls to dry and stacked as fuel [inferred: familiar plateau practice; the plateau has no timber to burn — our reading, not a cited source]: ours a patch of rough brown courses 2.4–4.2 m wide and 0.8–1.4 m high on one outer wall of 80% of the houses, the top row set on edge, a few fallen at the foot.
- **The roof as store.** Fodder, dung cakes and firewood are kept on the flat roofs [inferred]: ours a heap of barley straw tied with rope, a stack of dung cakes against the parapet, a bundle of firewood on the roofs of the houses without a third storey.
- **Foot-of-wall goods.** Firewood stacks, water jars, baskets, sacks and churn barrels at the houses' feet; a second item further along on four in ten fronts [inferred].
- **Flags over the lanes.** The five-colour prayer flags (§12) now also strung across the lanes from roof corner to roof corner between neighbouring houses (half the gaps) [inferred: the flags' practice; heights 6.35 m = the roofs' raised corners].
- **Life in the lanes.** A dog asleep, a goat or a donkey tethered at a wall, an old woman on her stool at the gate in the sun [inferred].
- **The street's fronts.** Each trading house and tea house: two paper lanterns hung at the door, sacks, baskets and a blue-glazed jar before it, a goods mat; the tea houses a stack of fuel and the kettles' pots [inferred].
- **Planting.** Willows and poplars on the open ground below the palace (z 35–46) at the village's north edge [inferred: the valley's willow and poplar, §8].
- **Sparrows** moved from the lots to the wide lane and the lane's end.

Still below Suzhou: the houses' fronts are one design in many sizes (no merchant house with a painted gate or noble residence with a carved porch); the Red Palace's chapels are not furnished; the lanes have no steps, drains or paving detail; walkers fewer than Suzhou's near the visitor.
