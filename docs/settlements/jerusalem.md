## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Dome of the Rock, completed 691/92 [stated: `docs/buildings/dome-of-the-rock.md` and its sources].
- **The Haram al-Sharif:** the esplanade on Herod's retaining walls, about 480 m north–south by 300 m east–west [stated: Wikipedia "Temple Mount"].
- **The al-Aqsa mosque:** at its south end. The first mosque was built under the early caliphs; it was rebuilt under ʿAbd al-Malik and al-Walid about 705 as a hypostyle hall with a dome before the mihrab [stated: Wikipedia "Al-Aqsa Mosque"].
- **The Kidron valley:** east, below the Mount of Olives [stated: Wikipedia "Kidron Valley"].
- **The Tyropoeon valley:** west, between the Haram and the upper city [stated: Wikipedia "Tyropoeon Valley"].
- **The Church of the Holy Sepulchre:** north-west, Constantine's rotunda and basilica, restored after 614 [stated: Wikipedia "Church of the Holy Sepulchre"].
- **Moment shown:** about 700, the Dome finished and the Aqsa about to be rebuilt (shown in its rebuilt form).

## 1a. The Aqsa mosque in detail

- Rebuilt under ʿAbd al-Malik and completed under al-Walid I; the Umayyad building "most likely measured 112 x 39 meters"; damaged by earthquakes in 713–14 (the eastern part rebuilt by al-Walid) and in 746; nothing of ʿAbd al-Malik's dome survives; the dome in front of the mihrab is "one of the few domes to be built in front of the mihrab" of the period; the present seven aisles and arcades are Fatimid (1034–36) [stated: Wikipedia "Al-Aqsa Mosque"]. Earlier, Arculf (679–82) saw a simple quadrangular building for about 3,000 worshippers [stated: same].
- Because the Umayyad plan is uncertain, the building keeps the frame of the earlier dossier (80 × 76 m on the south end of the esplanade) and the type of the surviving mosque: aisles perpendicular to the qibla, a wider and higher central nave, a dome over the bay before the mihrab [stated: type; inferred: the dimensions].
- As built: 11 aisles (the nave 9.8 m, the others 6.7 m) on a bay of 4.9 m; arcades of round arches springing at 5.9 m on reused columns (Attic base, monolithic shafts of mixed marbles and granite, Corinthian capitals, impost blocks), tied by timber beams at the springing as in the Dome of the Rock [inferred: by analogy with the Dome of the Rock and Umayyad practice]; side arcades to 9.7 m, the nave to 13.2 m with clerestory windows; a gabled timber roof sheathed in lead over each aisle and painted timber ceilings beneath [stated: lead-sheathed timber roofs of the later building; inferred: Umayyad]; a door into every aisle in the north façade, the nave's the largest, a window over each; arched windows high in the side and qibla walls; a wide arch from the nave into the square dome bay, a ring over its corners, a drum of 12 windows, an inner dome of gold mosaic and an outer of lead with a gilt finial [inferred]; the mihrab in the middle of the qibla wall; reed mats down the aisles; lamps hung in the nave [inferred].

## 1b. The Church of the Holy Sepulchre

- From the Cardo one passed through an eastern atrium into the basilica (the Martyrion) of four rows of columns, with a western apse ringed by twelve columns (Eusebius); beyond it an inner court, the Holy Garden, with the rock of Golgotha; the westernmost building, the Anastasis rotunda, about 35 m across, its 4th-century outer wall about 10 m high, twelve columns in groups of three between four pairs of square piers round the Tomb; burned by the Persians in 614, partly rebuilt by Modestus, destroyed in 1009 [stated: Jewish Virtual Library "The Church of the Holy Sepulcher"].
- As built (compressed to 119 m from the Cardo, 60 m wide): a stair of 17 m up from the Cardo to the church's terrace between two terraced blocks, six granite columns of the propylaea on the Cardo; an enclosure wall with three doors into the atrium; the basilica 40 × 40 m, five aisles (nave 14 m, arcades on columns to 15 m with clerestory, inner aisle arcades to 9.5 m), a floor of opus sectile, a gabled tile roof and lean-to roofs over the double aisles, the west apse with a half-dome of gold mosaic and twelve columns round it; the court with the rock of Golgotha and a great silver cross on it; the rotunda (inner diameter 33.7 m) with its doors to the east, 20 supports on a ring of 11 m radius (per quarter two piers and three columns), arcades and a drum of 16 windows to 20 m, a ceiling over the ambulatory under a tiled roof, a conical timber roof sheathed in lead open at the top; the Edicule of the Tomb in the middle, round, of marble, under a conical silver roof with a porch of two columns [stated: the sequence, the supports, the size of the rotunda; inferred: heights, roofs, the cross, the Edicule's form].


## 1c. The Dome of the Rock at L3: mosaics, the Rock and its cave (2026-11-07)

- **Mosaics.** The interior mosaics are "largely seventh century"; there are about 1,280 m² of mosaic; "Blues and greens dominate on the gold background", with red, silver and mother-of-pearl as highlights, mother-of-pearl often set on surfaces facing away from the light; the motifs are trees, fruits and garlands, leaves, shells, vases, baskets, crescents, stars and scrolls, "acanthus designs" from Late Antique art, Sassanian-style "stylised trees with their tulip-shaped flowers", and jewels "in the form of actual crowns, necklaces, bracelets"; the inscription band runs round both the outer and the inner faces of the octagon, "gold letters on a turquoise background", about 240 m long; "The inner face of the octagon … is more colourful than the outer" [stated: Medieval Mosaics, "Jerusalem, the Dome of the Rock"]. The drum carries a "vessel with floral motif" [stated: cavacopedia mirror of Wikipedia "Dome of the Rock", a gallery caption].
- Built: on both faces of every side of the octagonal arcade, a tree in each spandrel — a vase of blue with a pearl rim and a red stone, the stem rising from it, scrolls of acanthus leaves curling out to alternate sides in greens with blue turned leaves and a fringe of lobes, a jewel (a red stone in a ring of pearls) at the heart of each scroll, a crown of pearls with three points and a stone at the top; along the top of the arcade on both faces the inscription band (8.5–9.1 m): a turquoise ground between gold rules, gold letters in the manner of early Kufic — tall shafts, low letters with their heads, bowls, tails below the line — grouped in words on a joining baseline; a tree over each column of the circular arcade on both faces; inside the drum a tall tree between each two windows and, in the zone below, a tree under each window and between [inferred: the composition, the sizes and the placing; the text is not legible and not meant to be — no real text is reproduced]. Materials: the gold mosaic ground, the green mosaic, lapis, carnelian and a white glaze standing for mother-of-pearl, a turquoise glass for the band.
- **The Rock.** About 18 × 13 m, rising about 1.5 m, with a cave below about 4.5 m square [stated: `docs/buildings/dome-of-the-rock.md`, after Creswell]. On its west and north edges a "low clean-cut scarp"; "sections of the rock cut completely flat"; "a small hole on the southeastern corner that enters a cavern beneath the rock"; the southern side forms a ledge with a gap, and "a set of steps currently uses this gap to provide access … to the Well of Souls beneath it" [stated: Wikipedia "Foundation Stone"]. Built: the Rock re-made as a pitted outcrop highest toward the north-west and falling to the south-east, a scarp along the west and north, a flat-cut stretch; the cave under its south-east (4.5 m square, 2 m high) with walls and roof of the rock, a clay lamp burning in a niche; a flight of nine steps cut down into it from the south through the gap under the ledge, the platform's floor and the marble opened round them, the wooden screen left open on that side so the cave can be walked into [inferred: that the steps were there about 700 — the source describes them as current; the cave's height and the lamp ours]. The Crusader marble slab and cuttings are later and not shown [stated: same, the cuts "generally attributed to the Crusaders"].

## 4a. The city: walls, gates, the Cardo and its shops

- The Damascus Gate's lower structure is Hadrian's 2nd-century free-standing triumphal gate, joined by walls at the end of the 3rd or start of the 4th century and worked on under Justinian; the Roman gate stayed in use in the Early Islamic period; the name Bab al-Amud refers to the Roman column with a statue of Hadrian shown on the 6th-century Madaba map in the square behind the gate [stated: Wikipedia "Damascus Gate"].
- The Byzantine Cardo ran from the Damascus Gate to the Zion Gate: 22.5 m wide, a central roadway between two rows of monolithic limestone columns about 5 m high with Attic bases and Corinthian capitals, covered passages about 5 m wide each side under a timber-beamed roof (probably tiled), shops along it, paving in parallel rows; the Madaba map shows it with the Holy Sepulchre on it [stated: Jewish Virtual Library "The Nea Church & the Cardo"].
- As built: the wall (2.8 m thick, 9 m above the ground, battlemented, an inner parapet, a plinth of larger blocks) on an arc of 380 m round the west from the Haram's north-east to its south-east, the Haram's east wall closing it; square towers 7 × 6 m about every 40 m, 12.5 m high with loopholes; three gates — the north gate as Hadrian's triple arch (a 5.4 m central arch between two of 2.5 m, pilasters and a cornice) between towers at the head of the Cardo, single-arched gates at the west (on the street to the Haram) and at the south (the Cardo's foot) [stated: the north gate's origin and the walls' period; inferred: the circuit compressed, the sizes, the towers' form]. Inside the north gate a square with the column on its stepped base, its statue gone [inferred]. The Cardo from that square to the south gate: a 12.5 m roadway terraced level across the slope, colonnades of 5 m columns at 4 m with an architrave, porticoes 5 m deep paved a step up, lean-to tiled roofs on timber, and a row of shops 5.5 m deep behind each, a wide door to each shop under a timber lintel [stated: the Cardo's section; inferred: the shops' form]. The street from the west gate to the Haram's stair is a market street 10 m wide [inferred].

## 4b. The Haram's walls and gates (as built)

- **The walls.** Herodian ashlars mostly of 2–8 short tons, each "framed by fine-chiselled borders", the margins 5–20 cm wide and 1.5 cm deep; at the Western Wall the first seven courses above ground are Herodian and "the next four courses, consisting of smaller plainly dressed stones, are Umayyad work" [stated: Wikipedia "Western Wall"]. As built: the four retaining walls (3 m thick) coursed in 1.1 m Herodian courses of blocks 1.5–4.5 m long and some of 5–8 m, each with a margin of 11 cm round a face standing 4.5 cm proud (exaggerated from 1.5 cm to read), up to 2.4 m below the esplanade; four Umayyad courses of 0.6 m in small plain stones above; a parapet 1 m high with a coping, broken by piers at the head of the west stair, over the Golden Gate and at the palace bridge [inferred: the course heights, block lengths, the margin's relief, the parapet].
- **The Double and Triple Gates** (the Huldah Gates) in the south wall were restored by the Umayyads to give access to the esplanade; at the Double Gate "the lintel and the relieving arch above it are Herodian, while the intricately carved cornice is Umayyad"; inside a vestibule with columns and two pairs of domes of an early Muslim reconstruction; of the Triple Gate the western jamb is Herodian and "the rest of what we see today is Umayyad"; it was blocked only in the 11th century [stated: Wikipedia "Huldah Gates"; Jewish Virtual Library "Jerusalem Architecture in the Umayyad Period"]. As built (the wall is lower than the real one, so the gates are smaller): the Double Gate's two openings 3.4 m wide and 3.4 m high under Herodian lintels and relieving arches, a carved Umayyad moulding round each arch and a carved cornice across the gate under the parapet; the Triple Gate's three arched openings with carved archivolts and imposts; their doors shut, the vestibules behind not built [inferred: dimensions; the passages up to the esplanade cannot be walked under the ground model].
- **The Golden Gate**: two doors, Bab al-Rahma and Bab al-Tawba; its façade stands about 2 m out from the wall; two doorways each 3.90 m wide, separated by a column, under semicircular arches with a decorated frieze; inside a vestibule 20.37 × 10.50 m, divided into two aisles by columns, under six shallow domes; dated by some to about 520 under Justinian and by others to the later 7th century by Byzantine craftsmen for the Umayyads [stated: Wikipedia "Golden Gate (Jerusalem)"]. As built: the façade 24.4 m long, 2 m out from the wall, the two arched doorways with carved friezes and imposts, the column between them, corner pilasters, carved string course and cornice, doors shut; the vestibule's walls rising above the esplanade to 5.5 m under a flat roof carrying six shallow plastered domes, two arched doors (shut) toward the Dome of the Rock [inferred: the vestibule seen from the esplanade, its height, the doors shut, the interior not built].

## 4c. The Umayyad palaces south of the Haram (as built)

- The excavations of the 1970s uncovered six massive buildings south and south-west of the Haram; the main palace near the south-west corner measures 96 × 84 m, inside a wall about 3 m thick of large trimmed stones, many reused from the Herodian walls; a broad stone-paved central court ringed by columns carrying porticoes, many columns taken from Byzantine churches and cut with crosses; rooms round the court paved with small slabs and mosaic; very thick walls plastered with geometric and floral motifs; two gates, east and west; "a bridge ran from the palace roof to the Haram, giving direct access to al-Aqsa mosque"; the "pillared buildings" had large stone-paved courts with rows of square pilasters; paved streets between the buildings, cisterns beneath them; built apparently under al-Walid I (705–715), destroyed by the earthquake of 749 [stated: Jewish Virtual Library "Jerusalem Architecture in the Umayyad Period"]. As built: the palace (84 m east–west × 96 m north–south) at x −110…−26, z 214…310: the outer wall 3 m thick to 1 m above the flat roof, coursed in large blocks below and smaller above, small arched windows to the upper rooms; rooms 11 m deep in two storeys (an upper floor at 4.4 m, the roof of plaster at the esplanade's level) with a door and a window to each from the portico; a portico 5 m deep of arches on reused columns of mixed marbles and granite, every third one cut with a cross, under a lean-to tiled roof; the court paved, a well-head over the cistern; a painted band of ornament along the rooms' wall; gate passages east and west; the bridge, one arch over the street from the palace roof to a gap in the Haram's parapet west of the Aqsa. East of it a "pillared building" (84 × 74 m) of the same kind with square pilasters; paved streets round both [inferred: the plan of the rooms, the storeys, the heights (the roof set at the esplanade's level so that the bridge is level), the second building's size and place].


## 4d. The Cardo's shops and the moving life (2026-11-11)

- The Byzantine Cardo: a central roadway of about 12 m between two colonnaded walks, open to the sky in the middle and roofed on both sides with terracotta tiles on wooden planks; column bases 5.77 m apart under Byzantine-style Corinthian capitals; an arcade on the east side with stalls and workshops leased by craftsmen and merchants [stated: Madain Project "Cardo (Jerusalem)"]. The street had shops along its south-western side and more behind an arcade on the east; paving of well-hewn stones in parallel rows [stated: Wikipedia "The Cardo (Jerusalem)"]. The eastern Cardo's width shrank over the centuries from about 77 ft to 9 ft as the street's edges filled with building and stalls [stated: Bibleplaces "The Eastern Cardo"].
- After the Arab conquest the Cardo became an Arab-style market [stated: Madain Project]; an early Muslim tradition (al-Musharraf b. al-Murajja, Umayyad period) reports three markets on the central Cardo, expropriated from the Christians, perhaps dividing it into three streets [stated: Hebrew University, "The markets of Jerusalem after the Arab conquest"].
- Built: columns at 5.77 m; behind each portico a row of shops about 4.5 m wide and 5.5 m deep: dressed-stone jambs and lintel with a drip moulding, a relieving arch of seven voussoirs, a transom window, timber shutters folded back against the piers (about one shop in ten shut), a flagged floor (walkable from the portico), a plastered barrel vault, party walls with collision, a lit hanging lamp, timber shelves along the back wall and a table at the side set with the wares of the trade (pottery, cloth, grain, produce, oil and lamps, copper, glass, bread, spice) over a sign board coloured by trade [inferred: shop size and trades from the market tradition; the vault from the stone shops of Roman and Byzantine practice].
- Living systems: walkers by trade and faith (Muslim men in robes and turbans, veiled women, Christian townsmen in tunic and hose, Jewish townsmen, black-robed clergy, cloaked pilgrims with staffs, porters, boys) along the Cardo, the street to the Haram, the esplanade and the street under the south wall; doves and sparrows on the paving, swallows and crows aloft; kitchen smoke at the meals, steam at the shop fronts, incense at the Holy Sepulchre and the Dome of the Rock [inferred].

## 1d. The Haram furnished (deepening pass, 2026-11-11)

- Under ʿAbd al-Malik the Dome of the Rock was open to the public only on Mondays and Thursdays, on other days only its attendants entered; ten gatekeepers stood at each gate; the caliph's slaves (al-Akhmus) served on the Haram; Jews and Christians did the cleaning, made the glass for the lamps and prepared the wicks, exempt from the poll tax and passing the tasks down; the attendants purified themselves, changed clothes, burned incense and anointed the Rock with perfume, and prayers followed the incense; olive oil was sent from far to light the mosque of Jerusalem; the Rock was ringed by an ebony balustrade, curtains woven with gold hung between its pillars, a chain hung from the middle of the dome; pilgrims prayed at the Dome of the Rock, the Aqsa, the Dome of the Chain and other domes, and at the hajj season the ceremonies of Mecca were held there [stated: Elad, "Jerusalem in the Umayyad period" (University of Michigan course reader), pp. 300–314, early Muslim traditions in later sources, so the numbers are not firmly verified].
- Built: paved walks of flags with dressed kerbs from the platform's stairs to the west gate's stair, to the Aqsa's forecourt, to the Golden Gate; a forecourt before the Aqsa's north façade in flags with bands of marble; seven cistern heads (round dressed-stone head and lid, a trough beside it) on the esplanade; in the Aqsa the minbar of cedar (eight steps, a seat with a back and a gilded canopy) to the right of the mihrab, reed and woollen prayer mats in rows down every aisle, glass lamps in pairs on chains from the tie-beams of the side aisles, Qur'an stands (rahla) at the nave's arcades, censers before the doors; on the platform and esplanade six circles each of a reader and seated listeners, attendants sweeping with brooms, two gatekeepers with staffs at every stair and gate, pilgrims at prayer, in twos talking, with water jars, and rows at prayer in the Aqsa's aisles [inferred: the placing, the furniture's form (the minbar is later in type), the numbers]. Planting: cypresses and olives, thinly, kept off the platform, the walks and the Aqsa's forecourt [inferred].

## 1f. The Kidron valley's rock-cut tombs (2026-11-11)

- The Tomb of Absalom (Yad Avshalom) is about 20 m tall: a nearly cubical monolith about 6 m square and 6.4 m high cut from the Mount of Olives slope and surrounded on three sides by passages, with pairs of Ionic half-columns on each face and quarter-columns and pilasters at the corners, a Doric frieze of triglyphs and metopes under an Egyptian cornice; above it a square ashlar block, a round drum with a rope-like decoration, and a concave-sided cone ending in a half-closed lotus; of the 1st century AD [stated: Wikipedia "Yad Avshalom"]. The Tomb of Zechariah, a few metres from it, is a monolith cut from the rock of the valley's eastern escarpment directly across from the Temple Mount: a stepped base (crepidoma), two Ionic columns between two half-columns with pilasters at the corners, an Egyptian cornice carrying a pyramid; only the west façade finely worked; 1st century AD [stated: Wikipedia "Tomb of Zechariah"].
- Built: both on the valley's east side at the foot of the Mount of Olives (x 288–292, z 58–80), facing west to the Haram; Absalom with columns on three faces; Zechariah's size (5 × 5 m, a pyramid to about 9 m) and the placing along the Haram's wall are ours, the Zechariah's columns shown on the west face only [inferred].

### 5a. The courtyard house (2026-11-07)

- Umayyad houses excavated at Pella (destroyed in the earthquake of 749) were built round a courtyard, House G with "a paved internal court connected doorways to ground-floor rooms"; "thick double-faced stone walls formed the ground floors"; houses of probably two storeys, "perhaps three in some cases or with roof access"; "the courtyard drain carried water away" and "the porous jars stored water"; Houses A and B had "small courtyard ovens called tabuns"; a room held "cooking and storage pottery" and "the remains of a metal-banded wooden bucket"; a basalt quern ground grain; "feed benches along the walls" for animals; pottery included "oil lamps for lighting rooms after dark" [stated: Ancient History Sites, "Houses and Living Conditions in Umayyad Societies"].
- Built (every lot, replacing plain boxes): ranges of coursed limestone round a paved court — the main room across the north (one storey of 4.2 m, or two of 7.4 m on four houses in ten), a store range on the west, a kitchen range on the east on six in ten, the court closed to the lane by a wall with the house door; the house stands on its own level terrace (its court at the highest corner of its lot, steps down from the door to the lane where it falls away, at most three as the lanes may be a metre wide); doorways with stone jambs, lintels and a relieving arch of five voussoirs; small windows with sills and lintels onto the court and high slits to the lanes; flat roofs plastered within low parapets with copings; timber spouts into the court; a stone stair up the west range's court face to the roofs; the cistern's round stone head with its lid and the clay oven with its lid and a hearth of ashes in each court; on some one-storey houses a small plastered dome over the main room [inferred: the plan, sizes and proportions; the cistern under the court from the cisterns under the Umayyad palaces' streets, §4c]. Weathering: the grey-green damp at the foot of every wall, broken at the doors; rain streaks greyed down the faces under the spouts and here and there under the parapets [inferred].
- Four houses north of the street from the west gate to the Haram are open: the door standing open, the main room hollow under a ceiling of cedar beams and reeds, its door and two windows with wooden grilles onto the court; inside, reed mats over the flagstones, a mattress along three walls with bolsters, a cedar chest with an iron band, a bronze tray on its folding stand with a jug, a clay lamp burning in a niche with the soot over it; the store hollow too; the stair walkable up to the roofs.
