# Mamluk Cairo (Sultan Hasan and the Citadel), about 1363 — dossier

Settlement id: `mamluk-cairo` · Style: `mamluk` (with `vernacular` houses) · Region: Egypt (Cairo, below the Muqattam) · Code: `src/settlements/mamluk-cairo/`
Status of this dossier: L1–L2 (the Madrasa of Sultan Hasan — the sheer stone block with its tiers of window bays, muqarnas cornice and crenellated crown, the deep portal, the open court, the founder's domed tomb behind the qibla flanked by minarets; the Rumayla square; the Citadel on its spur, walled with round towers, the Mosque of al-Nasir Muhammad inside; the dense city; ground, planting, weather, music). ## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Mosque-Madrasa of Sultan Hasan, built 1356–1363, about 150 m long, its walls about 36 m high. Its portal is about 38 m high and its tallest minaret about 81.6 m. A cruciform court with four iwans holds the four madrasas, and the domed mausoleum stands behind the qibla iwan [stated: Wikipedia "Mosque-Madrasa of Sultan Hasan"].
- **The setting:** it stands below the Citadel of Saladin, facing it across the Rumayla square (today Salah al-Din square) [stated: Wikipedia]. The Citadel holds the Mosque of al-Nasir Muhammad, built 1318–1335 [stated: Wikipedia "Al-Nasir Muhammad Mosque"].
- **Moment shown:** 1363, the madrasa completed (its dome and minarets in their first form, approximated).

## 4. The style

Cut limestone; tiers of tall window recesses; a muqarnas cornice and crenellated crown; the portal's recess; a stone dome on a drum; minarets in tiers with balconies and a bulb. [stated: materials; inferred: details]

**The court and the qibla iwan inside (L3, built).** A four-iwan plan round an open paved court with the four madrasas in the corners, entered through doors in the court's corners; the four iwans unequal, the south-eastern (qibla) the largest [stated: Smarthistory; mosqpedia]. The exterior is stone, the interior mostly brick with stucco [stated: Smarthistory]. The qibla iwan is spanned by an enormous pointed vault; its walls are panelled in coloured stone and marble below a stucco band of Kufic Qur'anic inscription [stated: mosqpedia], which runs round the iwan at the springing of the vault [stated: Kenyon slide, after Blair & Bloom]; the mihrab in marble panels of contrasting colours, the marble minbar to its right, the dikka on columns in front of the iwan [stated: Kenyon slide; Smarthistory]; windows with bronze grilles either side of the mihrab [stated: ask-aladdin] and an oculus above it [stated: sharm-club]; doors either side of the mihrab into the tomb behind, one original bronze door surviving [stated: Smarthistory]; about 155 oil lamps hung at one height [stated: Smarthistory]; the iwans' arches with red and black rims [stated: Wikipedia mirror]. The domed fountain at the centre is probably Ottoman [stated: Wikipedia mirror], so only a plain octagonal basin is shown. Ours: the qibla iwan 20 m wide and 16 deep with its vault springing at 14.5 m (our block is smaller than the real one); the other three iwans 14 and 12 m wide; the way in from the portal: a domed vestibule 8 × 8 m (stone benches along its sides, a dado of dark and white marble courses, tiers of muqarnas cells across its corners, the dome of plastered brick on its ring, a lamp from the crown), then a passage bent east through the north range and south into the court's corner, so that no one in the street sees into the court [inferred: the real vestibule's plan and the bend's turns simplified]; glass lamps on chains in every iwan.

### 4.1 The minarets (L3, built)

- Two minarets flanked the domed mausoleum; only one survives [stated: Archnet], the tallest about 81.6 m [stated: mosqpedia]; one fell while the building was going up [stated: mosqpedia]. The Mamluk minaret rises in three tiers from a square base through an octagonal middle to a round top, the balconies carried on muqarnas corbels, the shafts carved, the top ending in a bulb-like head [stated: Vectree, "Mamluk architecture"]. Ours, both standing as built in 1363: a square stage to 0.4 of the height with two tiers of keel-arched niches and slit windows on each face; a balcony on four tiers of muqarnas cells with a parapet of carved panels between posts; the octagonal stage with a niche on each face and its balcony; the round, lightly fluted stage and its balcony; a pavilion of eight slender marble columns round the stair's core, a ring, the bulb on its neck, and a bronze finial with its crescent [inferred: proportions, the niches and the pavilion follow the type].

### 4.2 The Citadel (L3, built)

- **The mosque of al-Nasir Muhammad** (completed about 1318, heightened 1335): a free-standing hypostyle mosque 63 × 57 m round a court of 35.81 × 23.32 m, the sanctuary on the qibla side, galleries on the other three; the outer walls plain with a rectangular crenellation; the main entrance projecting from the west wall; two minarets, both of stone, their bases below the roof's line: the taller at the north-east corner, the other at the western portal above the main entrance, its top a garlic-shaped bulb unique in Cairo and its upper sections covered with green, white and blue faience mosaic, thought to be the work of a craftsman from Tabriz; both with finely carved bulbous sections [stated: Wikipedia "Al-Nasir Muhammad Mosque"]. Ours: the block at that size (its court 35.8 × 23.3 m, set north of the middle for the deeper sanctuary), the west portal; the north-west minaret (36 m): a square base, a round shaft carved in bands, a balcony on muqarnas, the upper stage in bands of green and white faience with a blue band at its head, the neck and the ribbed garlic bulb in green faience, the bronze finial; the north-east (42 m): a round shaft carved in chevrons, two balconies, a carved bulbous stage, an open pavilion of eight columns, a ribbed stone bulb [inferred: proportions and the distribution of the carving].
- **The curtain** of the Citadel on its spur in ten straight runs with round towers at the angles, square merlons along the walk [inferred]; it was previously drawn as the axis-aligned boxes round each run, now built along each run.
- **The road and the gate.** The road climbs from the Rumayla's south-east corner round the spur's western face, 8 m wide, cut into the slope and built up on it, at about 13°, with a revetment on its inner side where cut and a parapet on its outer side where raised, to a gate in the north-western curtain flanked by two round towers under an arch of red and white voussoirs [inferred: the north-west is the side of Bab al-Mudarraj, Saladin's gate of 1183 reached by a stepped ramp; the line and the gate's form are ours].

### 4.3 The covered market street (L3, built: `suq_covered`)

Cairo's markets lined its streets — al-Maqrizi wrote that the Qasaba had once held 12,000 shops; some markets were covered, one with a roof of wood, and an amir shaded the Qafaysat market with canvas drawn on ropes [stated: Belleten, "Al-Makrizi's Khitat and the markets in Cairo during the Mamluk era"]. A market of arms, the Suq al-Silah, stood on the Qasaba [stated: same]; the street now called Shari' Suq al-Silah runs north from Sultan Hasan [stated: Archnet "Suq al-Silah" for the street's name]. Ours: a covered market street 100 m long running north from the madrasa's portal, in the manner of the Qasaba's markets [inferred: its place here]: a row of shops each side, each a cell 3 m wide and 5 deep with a raised wooden floor and shelves of goods (pots, cloth, brass trays, blades, baskets) behind a stone bench (the mastaba) where the seller sat, the shutters folded up under a lintel of joggled stones; over them a plastered storey with small projecting lattice windows; across the street beams carrying mats, a bay left open every 24 m for light and air; a gate at each end with an arch of dark and white voussoirs [inferred: dimensions and goods].

## 5. The town

About 1,400 houses of mud brick and stone, two or three storeys, with roof beams and shelters. [inferred]

**The houses (L3, built: `house_mamluk`, style `vernacular`).** The house type, from the sources: the foundation walls to the height of the first floor cased outside with the soft limestone of the Muqattam; the superstructure of brick, often plastered, its front projecting about two feet on corbels or piers; the upper windows projecting a foot and a half or more, mostly of turned wooden lattice screening those inside from the street (the mashrabiya), with small projecting cages for water bottles to cool; the ground-floor windows small, grated and set high, above the sight of passers-by; the court reached by a passage with one or two turnings so that no one outside can see in (the bent entrance); the door often painted, with a knocker and a wooden lock; many houses with a malqaf, a sloping shed of boards directed to the north or north-west, catching the breeze for the rooms below; the roof flat and plastered [stated: Lane, *Manners and Customs of the Modern Egyptians*, ch. 1 — an 1830s account of a type continuous since the Mamluk period; Wikipedia "Residential architecture in Historic Cairo": the bent entrance, the high grilled ground-floor openings, the larger projecting upper windows, the malqaf as a square roof structure with an inclined roof over the reception hall]. Ours, on all about 1,400 lots: the ground storey 3.7–4.1 m of yellow limestone ashlar on a grey footing, a moulded course where it ends; the upper storeys plastered (limewashed, plain, or earth-plastered on the poorer houses), stepped out 0.5–0.7 m over the lane on a row of three-tiered stone corbels and a wooden beam, a three-storey house sometimes stepping out again on wooden brackets; on about two thirds of the houses a mashrabiya on the first upper storey (on some three-storey houses a second), on wooden corbels with a painted apron, the lattice in front and at the sides, a finer band above, a hood of boards, and often the little cage for the water jars at its sill; small projecting lattice windows on brackets in the other bays, plain framed grilles at the back; in the stone storey the recess of the bent entrance, 1.7 m wide and 1.5 m deep, its ceiling of palm-trunk beams, the painted door in its side wall in a stone frame with knocker and lock, a stone bench at its back, joggled voussoirs of two stones (or a plain lintel) over its mouth; small high windows with wooden grilles; a stone bench in the lane; a cornice of two plaster steps on a wooden band, the parapet with its coping and wooden spouts; on about half the roofs a malqaf facing north — side walls, posts and a board hood rising 3–4 m, its mouth barred — and on others the head of the stair, a shelter of palm sticks with a reed mat, water jars [inferred: dimensions, frequencies, the joggled lintels and corbel forms after the period's monuments]. The houses are built in 100 m tiles, in full near and simplified far (src/settlements/lod.js); the earlier shells (`house_egypt`) and their dressed fronts (`cairo_front`, style `mamluk`) are no longer used here.

**Sabil-kuttabs (L3, built).** A standard part of Mamluk foundations from the 14th century on, later also standing alone; the sabil room always at a corner of the building; water handed out through large grilled windows (bronze grilles) to people outside, a basin below each window; an underground cistern filled each year from the Nile's flood; the kuttab (the children's Qur'an school) above in a spacious loggia of arches on marble columns, shaded by large awnings [stated: AramcoWorld, "The Sultan's Fountain"]. Ours, three on street corners in place of a house (two at the crossing of the lanes west of the madrasa, one at the north-west corner of the square): 8 × 8 m, the sabil's two street faces each with a window 2 × 2.5 m in a marble frame with a bronze grille and a marble ledge, joggled voussoirs of dark and white marble above, dark courses in the stone, an engaged marble column at the corner; the loggia above wrapping the corner on marble columns with stepped keel heads, a wooden lattice balustrade, the wooden awning on rows of brackets, the kuttab's door at the back, a crest of merlons [inferred: sizes and details].

## 10. Phase D: the merchant's house with its qa'a, Sultan Hasan's lamps and floors, the street life (fourth pass)

**The qa'a house.** The qa'a is "the most common hall type in medieval architecture", "a living space with two iwans facing each other on the main axis"; the durqa'a between them is the entry to the qa'a, "one step lower and has a higher ceiling", usually roofed with a lantern or a dome; the badhahanj, an open shaft over one iwan, its open side to the wind behind a wooden grille, turns the air down into it; the Qa'a of Muhibb al-Din (1350) is "a rare example of a merchant dwelling off the central artery" [stated: MIT 4.615, "Mamluk domestic architecture" (handout 12)]. The qa'a with the takhtabush, "a receiving area off the courtyard", make the men's rooms of the ground floor; the mashrabiya screens the windows [stated: Wikipedia "Residential architecture in Historic Cairo"]. Ours (`cairo_qaa`, `src/styles/mamluk/qaa.js`, on four lots west of the covered market street, `layout.js` `QAA`, built with the shared courtyard house of `src/styles/mughal/courthouse.js`): walls of the yellow Muqattam stone, their foot darkened by the lane; the street door on the east with its studded leaves and knockers into a vestibule whose door to the court is at its far end (the bent entrance); the court paved in flags with a marble well-head under a pulley frame and two water jars on their stands; on the north the qa'a, 15 × 5.8 m inside: the durqa'a in its middle, its floor of white marble set with a star of eight points in dark and rose marble and a border of the two, an octagonal marble fountain with a bronze spout and its jet; above it a square opening in the painted ceiling to the lantern (shukhshaykha), a drum of eight lattice panels on posts under a dome of boards, an enamelled glass lamp hung in it; the two iwans either side raised a step (0.3 m, a dark marble edge), each with a Mamluk carpet (red with green and blue octagons), benches with mattresses and back cushions along the end and the outer walls, the beam across its front on corbels of three stepped tiers with a painted band; a brass tray on its stand, an ewer and a brazier; the suffa — a marble shelf on two colonnettes facing the door — with china and brass on it; latticed windows to the court; the door to the court in a frame of dark and white marble under a lintel of joggled voussoirs; the malqaf over the west iwan, its mouth turned north behind a lattice under a sloping board roof; on the south the takhtabush open to the court by a wide arch, a bench and a reed mat in it; the merchant seated on his iwan, a servant in the court (`life.js`) [inferred: the plan and sizes, the floor's pattern, the furnishing].

**Sultan Hasan's lamps.** The mosque's glass lamps have "a flaring neck and a wide, globular body", six glass handles for suspension and a disc foot; enamel in red, blue, green and white with gold; the Light verse on the neck in deep blue enamel; 35 cm high, others of the sultan's 38–42 cm; the mosque held "hundreds" of them, nearly fifty surviving, hung on metal chains from the ceiling, evenly spaced across the iwans [stated: Smarthistory, "A glass lamp: illuminating Sultan Hassan's mosque and madrasa"]. Ours: every lamp of the four iwans now that form, about 0.42 m high: the disc foot, the globular body with a blue band and a gilt band, the flaring neck banded in blue, six glass loops, three chains to a ring and one chain up to the vault [inferred: the bands stand for the enamelled inscriptions].

**The floors.** Reed mats laid across every iwan; in the qibla iwan three carpets over them, Mamluk and dark red; a Qur'an open on its folding stand (rahle) by the minbar's far side with a cushion for the reader [inferred].

**The street life.** In the covered market street the sellers sitting cross-legged on their stone benches, buyers before the shops, porters with sacks on their shoulders; water carriers with the goatskin on the back — the water-carriers of Cairo carried it "in an earthen vessel, or a goat's skin, on the back" [stated: Lane, *Manners and Customs of the Modern Egyptians*, of the 1830s — taken for 1363, inferred]; a camel with water skins and its driver on the Rumayla; donkeys saddled for hire with their boys at the market street's north gate and on the square; a string of four camels with bales below the Citadel's road [inferred].

## 11. Phase E: the Bayn al-Qasrayn, the khan, the bath, the shop (fifth pass; the city c. 1400)

Radius grown from 400 to 480 m. The date stays the 1360s–1400 (Barquq's complex, 1384–86, and the Khan al-Khalili, 1382–89, stand).

**The Bayn al-Qasrayn (`bayn_front`, `bayn_bed`; `src/styles/mamluk/bayn.js`, `kit.js`).** The Qalawun complex stands on al-Mu'izz street in Bayn al-Qasrayn, built 1284–85; its facade is about 67 m long, of ashlar with pointed-arched panels of varying sizes each enclosing a window; the portal has a rounded arch enclosing a pointed arch with ablaq (striped) voussoirs, geometric spandrels, a grilled double window and an oculus; the madrasa's prayer hall projects into the street; the three-storey minaret stands near the entrance; the mausoleum (octagon of piers and columns under a high drum and dome) lies between the entrance passage and the madrasa of Sultan Barquq; the maristan behind was an L-shaped hospital of four iwans [stated: Wikipedia "Qalawun complex"]. The madrasa of al-Nasir Muhammad stands next to it and Barquq's beside that; its brick facade carried stucco designs and inscriptions; its marble portal is a spoil of 1291 from a Gothic church in Acre, "a pointed arch with a triple recess flanked by three slender columns on each side"; the minaret stands directly over the entrance and corridor, the lowest stage square with stucco medallions and keel-arched niches [stated: Wikipedia "Madrasa of al-Nasir Muhammad"; MIT 4.615 handout 10]. Ours, on the west side of a 28 m wide paved bed at x = −174…−146, in the order Qalawun (south, z −245…−178), al-Nasir (−284…−245), Barquq (−326…−284) [inferred: the order along the street; the compression of the Bayn]: Qalawun in four sections from the north (the mausoleum 24 m, the entrance passage 9 m with the portal, the madrasa 17 m whose hall projects 1.8 m, the lower wall of the maristan), each a wall of cut limestone with recessed pointed panels 3.2 m wide at 4.4 m, every panel's head ringed with alternate dark and white voussoirs, in it a grilled window in a marble frame, a pointed window with its ring and an oculus with a spoked grille, a band of inscription; the stepped cornice hung with stalactite cells and the parapet of stepped merlons; the portal between two piers under a 12 m ablaq arch with six tiers of muqarnas, studded bronze-bound leaves, a grilled double window and oculus above, stone benches either side; the minaret and the dome of the mausoleum (`mamluk_minaret`, `mamluk_dome`). Al-Nasir's front in plaster (brick and stucco in life) with the Gothic portal of three orders of marble archivolts, three slender columns each side and a tympanum, its minaret over it; Barquq's with a recessed ablaq portal, a minaret and a dome [inferred: dimensions, the panels' distribution and the heights].

**The Khan of Jaharkas al-Khalili (`cairo_wikala`; `khan.js`).** Jaharkas al-Khalili, Barquq's Master of the Stables, demolished the Fatimid Turbat az-Za'faraan and built "a large khan at the heart of the city" (1382–89), a multi-storied inn round a courtyard where merchants stored goods, the upper floors living quarters, shops on the ground level of the street facades; khans and wikalas were grouped with souq streets between them (21 by the end of the Mamluk period) [stated: Wikipedia "Khan el-Khalili"]. The wikala type: a rectangle round a large court with a fountain, a monumental portal on the single street facade wide enough for the loaded animals, a portico of tall arches round the court on the lower two storeys, the ground floor for animals and merchandise, the second floor for the merchants, mashrabiyas on the upper floors on court and street (typology from the later Wikala al-Ghuri, 1504-5: five storeys, two of stone and three of brick, the upper three a rab' of apartments) [stated: Wikipedia "Wikala" and "Wikala al-Ghuri"; dimensions of the Mamluk-period khans not given]. Ours (42 × 44 m, x −146…−104, z −308…−264): four storeys of 4.6 + 4.0 + 3.3 + 3.3 m, wings 10 m deep round a court of flags 22 × 24 m with an octagonal fountain; a portico of pointed arches 3 m deep on the court's four sides with the storerooms' doors behind and the gallery floor of beams above; the court's faces with rows of windows and, on the third and fourth floors, projecting mashrabiyas on corbels; on the street front the portal (an open passage 4.4 × 7.2 m through the west wing with its ablaq arch and muqarnas hood) and on each side a row of shops (a pointed opening, a stone bench, a shutter, goods on the bench); bales of sacking and cloth, water jars, rolled carpets and saddlebags in the court, a hitching rail; camels, a donkey and their drivers [inferred: sizes and the plan from the type].

**The covered market lanes of the khan (`suq_covered` with `goods`).** Two more covered streets north–south behind the khan: one of brass trays and glass lamps, spices in mounds, pottery and blades; one of cloth (bolts in indigo, red, green, ochre, white, maroon) and baskets, joined by a cross lane [inferred: the trades; the Khan's later fame as the centre of foreign trade, of slaves and precious stones, is from Wikipedia "Khan el-Khalili"; the sorting of trades by street is from "Work and Markets in Mamluk Cities" (ancient-history-sites.com)].

**The bath (`cairo_hammam`).** Hammam exteriors are windowless, plastered brick walls made much thicker than the structure needs to hold the heat; the entrance small and narrow but richly framed; within, the meslakh (maslakh) with a central, often octagonal fountain of cold water where bathers undress and rest on mattresses and cushions; a passage of small rooms each hotter than the last; the hararah, the central steam room under a masonry dome pierced with small glass openings, with the faskeeyah, a fountain of hot water on a wide octagonal marble platform where bathers are massaged; the maghtas, the plunging pool; floors of marble; the boiler room (dung fuel); attendants who gave clogs and wrapped bathers in towels and turbans; a towel at the entrance marked the hour of men or women [stated: AramcoWorld, "The Joys of the Bath"]. Ours (24 × 24 m on the Qasaba south of the Bayn, x −155.5…−131.5, z −166…−142, walkable): the door in the west wall in a pointed marble frame into a small vestibule (a bench, the clogs, a lamp), north through a door into the maslakh (11 × 16.5 m; the octagonal fountain with a jet, platforms of marble along two walls with cushions and folded towels, a brazier, ewers, a dado of white marble with a dark course), east through a door into the hot room (the wide octagonal platform with the hot fountain, bowls, the plunge basin at the north end, niche benches), each under a dome with eight small glass lights; the furnace and fuel store in the south-east corner under a brick chimney [inferred: the compression of the sequence to two rooms; the plan's proportions].

**The shop (`cairo_shop`) and the sherbet stand (`sharbat_stall`; `shops.js`).** Each neighbourhood had small shops, a public bath and a bakery; vendors sold cold drinks from sealed jars; *fuqqa'*, a foamy drink, sold in a market of about twenty shops, sealed jars arranged on marble under fountains; *sukkar wa laymun* (sugar and lemon) was prepared for travellers [stated: Rawi, "What Was Cooking in Medieval Cairo?"; Wikipedia "Khan el-Khalili" for the khans]. Coffee reached Cairo only about 1500, so no coffee seller stands in 1400 [inferred, from the standard history of coffee's spread from Yemen: not sourced here]. Ours: a spice-and-drugs merchant's shop 9 × 8.5 m that one can enter: the front opening 3.4 m under a lintel of joggled stones, the shutter boards folded above, stone benches either side; the room with a raised boarded floor, a painted ceiling on beams, shelves on three walls with about a hundred jars and bottles, sacks of spices and grain, the counter with a brass balance and cups, the merchant on his mat and cushion, a chest, herbs and bags hung from the beams, lamps; a plastered storey over it with a lattice window. The sherbet stand: a wooden counter, a rack of three big glazed jars with sealed mouths, a tray of brass cups, a brass-lidded jar, sugar loaves, a striped awning; the seller behind it and a customer [inferred].

## 12. Living systems and weathering (fifth pass)

**Weathering.** A desert city: `damp` 0.04 (no moss, no rain streaks), the limestone sun-bleached and dust-grey at the foot, the lanes and the Qasaba beaten earth; the khamsin's dust haze already in the weather list [inferred; Cairo's climate].

**The walkers (`movers.js`).** Men in the long robe with the wound turban and a sash; merchants in green, maroon and indigo; soldiers of the sultan's mamluks with a staff and a coloured cap under the turban; scholars in white with the large turban; women wrapped in black or white and veiled; porters with baskets; water carriers (*saqqa*) with the earthen pot (the goatskin of the street life is a still figure); peasants in blue; boys in the shirt; 46 within reach by day, 4 at night; the covered streets weighted heaviest [inferred; Lane's account of dress, 1830s, is the base (dossier §10)].

**Animals (`fauna.js`).** Kites over the Citadel's spur and the Bayn, swifts over the roofs in the evening, pigeons and sparrows on the Bayn's paving, the khan's court and at the shop fronts [inferred].

**Smoke and steam (`smoke.js`).** Kitchen smoke from about a quarter of the houses at the meals; the bath's chimney all day and steam from its domes; steam from cook-shops in the three covered streets by day; incense at the portals of Sultan Hasan, Qalawun and the khan at the hours of prayer [inferred].

**Still life added.** Sellers on the benches of the two new covered streets; a scholar on the bench at Qalawun's portal; talkers before the complexes; a water carrier; a donkey with its boy; the shop's merchant and a customer; the sherbet sellers; three kneeling camels with bales and drivers in the khan's court; the bath's attendant and bathers.
