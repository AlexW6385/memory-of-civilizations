## 1. The real example

- **Borgund stave church:** built probably between 1180 and 1250, of pine. It is a "Sogn type" stave church with a raised central nave. It has a free-standing bell tower and an ambulatory round the whole building [stated: Wikipedia "Borgund Stave Church"].
- **Roofs:** the roofs are stacked in tiers and covered with shingles. The gables carry dragon heads [stated: same].
- **Construction:** a stave church stands on sills laid over a stone foundation, with corner posts (staves) and wall planks [stated: Wikipedia "Stave church"].
- **Farms:** a medieval Norwegian farm was a cluster of separate log buildings round a yard (the *tun*). They included the dwelling (*stue*), the storehouse raised on posts (*loft*/*stabbur*) and the barn and byre, and dwellings were roofed with birch bark under sod [stated: Wikipedia "Stabbur"; "Sod roof"].
- **The svalgang (external gallery):** a covered gallery surrounds the church, including the chancel; it is partly open as an arcade and fully enclosed round the chancel [stated: Ancient History Sites]. Above the parapet small columns and round arches run as a continuous arcade; the entrances to the nave in the west and south are marked in the gallery by large projecting gabled porches (skruv), and the west one has a two-storey superstructure over the portal [stated: SNL "Borgund stavkirke"]. Crosses decorate the small gables of the gallery [stated: Ancient History Sites]. Built here: a plank parapet, turned little columns with capitals, a band of round arches and the wall plate on the north, south and west; the chancel side closed; a gabled skruv over the south entrance and a two-storey superstructure over the west porch, each with a cross. The number of bays (14 on the long sides, 11 on the west) is ours [inferred].
- **Roofs and turret:** six roof sections stand one above another [stated: kringom.no]; with the turret's two-stage spire there are seven roof surfaces; the turret has side panels in two storeys [stated: SNL]; it has a gabled lower roof and a two-level upper structure [stated: Ancient History Sites]. The roofs are boarded and covered with wooden shingles fastened by pegs; the ridge crests are openwork [stated: Ancient History Sites; ArchEyes]. Built here: the turret's square base under crossing gables, two panelled storeys with little openings under their skirts, the spire in two stages with an iron vane; an openwork crest along the nave's ridge; the gable ends boarded (no longer shingle triangles).
- **The apse:** a conical roof topped by a short tower, a conical cap and a cross [stated: Ancient History Sites] — the cross added.
- **The bell tower (støpul):** right south of the church stands Norway's only free-standing stave-built bell tower; nine staves carry the roof and the bell frame; the staves stand on a sill frame on a stone fill [stated: SNL]. Built here: moved from the north-west to the south side of the church; a rubble bed and the sill frame, the steep shingled skirt over the posts with a low door, the boarded belfry with its staves showing and two round-headed sound openings a face, the pyramid roof with a flared eave and a cross. The skirt follows the tower as it stands today (clad since the early 1900s [stated: SNL]); its form about 1250 is not known [inferred].
- **Farm buildings:** lofts were built in log technique; a loft is a two-storey building with stores below and valuables and a guest room above; many lofts have an external gallery (svalgang) resting on a log corbel, and a loft has its stairs outside, reaching the upper storey from the gallery [stated: Wikipedia "Loft (building)"]. Built here: all farm buildings now of round logs in alternating courses that cross and stand out at the corners, log gables, barge boards, plank doors with jambs and lintels; the sod roofs held by eave logs; the dwelling's smoke-hole housing (ljore) on the ridge instead of a chimney [inferred: the open-hearth smoke-room house (røykstue) of the period had no chimney]; the loft on four posts on stones under flat caps, the upper storey overhanging on corbelled logs, the gallery with its arcaded parapet, and the stair up the side.
- **The interior (built here, v9.52):** the nave's central room has 14 staves: one in each corner, three pairs along the long sides and two pairs on the short sides; the middle stave of each long side does not rise from the sills but rests on an inserted arch; all are round in section [stated: SNL]. Twelve free-standing columns carry the raised central nave [stated: Wikipedia]. Diagonal plank St Andrew's crosses fill the fields between the tengs (longitudinal beams) and the staves; the outer walls are braced by large crossing braces with carved leaf forms; curved knee braces above and below the tengs form simple arcades; horizontal struts brace the central room's staves to the outer-wall posts [stated: SNL]. The braces carry sun-shaped centres [stated: Wikipedia]. Small round openings near the top of the long walls, eight in all on the north and south, are with the doors the only light; the interior is dim and unpainted [stated: SNL; Ancient History Sites]. Medieval wall benches run along the aisles, not in rows across the room [stated: Ancient History Sites; SNL]. The medieval stone altar: two stacked blocks and a slab, about 133 × 65 × 92 cm [stated: Ancient History Sites]; a box-shaped soapstone font [stated: Wikipedia; lokalhistoriewiki calls it granite]; a chancel screen with a stepped portal with two half-columns existed before the Reformation [stated: Ancient History Sites]; a medieval crucifix, removed after the Reformation [stated: Wikipedia]. The chancel about 3.4 m square, the apse about 1.88 m deep and 2.98 m wide [stated: Ancient History Sites]. The original floor is of planks [stated: Wikipedia]. Built here: the church now hollow and walkable — the plank floor; the svalgang under a lean-to of boards and shingles with its boarded ceiling; the aisle walls with the west and south portals and the chancel opening; the 14 staves (the two suspended ones with a carved foot on the wide middle arch), the lower arcade of arched knee braces, the tengs, the St Andrew's crosses with sun discs, the beam under the central room's walls, the upper arcade under the wall plate; the struts out to the wall posts and the great crosses on the aisle walls (not where the doors are); the wall benches; the eight round openings as real holes; the open roof boarded inside with rafters and scissor beams [inferred: the usual stave-church roof]; the turret's base following the roof's slopes so nothing hangs into the nave; the porch hollow with its door leaf standing open; the chancel with its boarded ceiling, the screen's half-columns, the rood beam and a Romanesque crowned crucifix [inferred: the type of the period]; the half-round apse of staves under its conical roof, the altar at its mouth with linen, a painted frontal [inferred: Norway's painted altar frontals of the 13th century], two iron candlesticks lit, a small wooden cross. The suspended staves' feet, the arches' radii, the bench height (0.46 m) and the lighting are ours [inferred].
- **The dragon heads and crests (built here, north4):** "Both the nave and the roof turret have openwork ridge crests with dragon heads over the gables"; the crest bears the year 1738, when the dragon heads were also renewed, probably as copies of older decoration; their design shows clear traces of the Urnes style and resembles a head from Lom [stated: SNL]. "On each of its four gables is a stylised dragon head, swooping from the carved roof ridge crests", similar to the carved heads on Norse ships' prows [stated: Wikipedia]; the dragons' meaning is disputed — protection against evil is one reading [stated: Ancient History Sites]. Built here: openwork crests (a board pierced with round holes and slots, its top cut in lobes) along the nave's ridge and on the turret's four short ridges; four carved heads — at the nave's east and west gables and the turret's east and west gables — each a neck swooping up from the crest's end into a head with open toothed jaws, the snout curled up, a horn swept back, a scalloped mane, a curling tongue and an almond eye standing proud. Their profile and size (about 2 m on the nave, 1.4 m on the turret) are ours [inferred: after the Urnes-style heads of Borgund and Lom].
- **The west portal (built here, north4):** the main entrance is in the west through a broad side-panel portal (vangeportal) covered in carpet-like carving; above the plinth dragon heads project from the column shafts on each side and spew strong vine stems that spread with circular branches up the portal's sides; on the upper part of the side panels a large pair of facing dragons with spread wings; a smaller central dragon curls in on itself in the field over the archivolt; at the top each side small dragons run down the panels [stated: SNL]. The portal is in the Sogn–Valdres style; the columns have bases and capitals, spiral vines decorate the archivolt [stated: Ancient History Sites]; the portals were originally painted green, red, black and white [stated: Wikipedia]. Built here: one carved board on the porch's west face, its side panels 0.53 m wide and its head rising into the gable; a half-column with base and cushion capital at each side of the door; on each a dragon's head above the plinth with two stems twining up the panel from its jaws, crossing one another, with a curling tendril and a leaf lobe in every bay; the great dragon with its S-curved body, coiled tail, leg and spread wing at the panel's head, its head turned in toward the arch; a beaded border with small curls at the outer edges; the archivolt band with two twisted stems; the small dragon curled over the arch. The relief depths, the number of bays and the layout of the stems are ours; the paint is not shown (the wood tarred like the rest) [inferred].
- **The churchyard:** the old main road ran close to the churchyard wall [stated: SNL]; its material is not given. Built here: a dry-stone wall about 1.05 m high, battered, with cover stones and the odd fallen stone, and a roofed gate (lychgate) on the south: four staves on sills over stones, benches along the sides, a shingled gable roof with barge boards and a cross, the two leaves open [inferred: the dry-stone wall and roofed gate are the common Norwegian churchyard form; a roofed churchyard gate is attested at Høre, whose old turret serves as one (SNL)]. The stave fence is gone.
- **The farm life (built here):** horses and cattle were the most important animals; sheep, goats and pigs were kept, and dogs appear often; grain was the main food; work was by hand with home-made wooden tools with iron blades, helped by horses and oxen; summer pastures and herding [stated: Landslov exhibition, University of Bergen]. Built here as still figures and objects: at each tun people in the yard (talking, squatting at work, carrying baskets), firewood stacked by the dwellings, haystacks by the barns, sledges, hens, dogs, barrels; haymaking in the meadows (mowers with scythes, rakers, stacks); herds at pasture — cattle, sheep, goats, horses — each with a herder; at the church the priest at the altar and a few people in the churchyard and at the gate. Numbers and places are ours [inferred].
- **The arrival:** the view from the west across the churchyard wall to the porch and the stacked roofs, now set on the ground (its height had been below the low rise the church stands on, so it was not walkable).
- **Moment shown:** about 1250, with the church new.

- **The tun deepened (L3, 2026-11-11):** a medieval farm was a cluster of separate log buildings round a yard; beasts were horses and cattle first, then sheep, goats, pigs; dogs often; work was by hand with wooden tools with iron blades, summer pastures and herding [stated: Landslov exhibition, University of Bergen]; the loft is a two-storey store with valuables and a guest room above, its gallery and outside stair [stated: Wikipedia "Loft (building)"]; the smoke-room house had no chimney [inferred, as above]. Built here: every farm now has the dwelling on the yard's far side, a loft each side, a barn and a byre across the ends, an open wood shed and a second house (the smith's at the priest's farm); the plan is mirrored or not by farm so the yards differ [inferred: the layout is ours]. All dwellings and barns and the smithy are enterable. The barn: the plank threshing floor across the middle, a hay mow stacked to the beams on one side behind a slatted front, the byre on the other with stall boards, mangers and hay racks, the loft of poles with its ladder, tools (scythes, rakes, a flail) hung on the wall, sacks of grain and a winnowing basket; its animals stand in the stalls. The loft: its upper room is reached by the outside stair and the gallery through a 0.9 m door; inside a guest's bed with skins, iron-bound chests, kegs, bolts of homespun, clothes on pegs, hung meat from the beam. The smithy: a stone forge under a clay hood, bellows of leather, anvil on its stump, the quenching tub, charcoal, a bench with a vice, hammers and tongs on the wall, soot on the logs, the smith at the anvil [inferred: a farm smith worked iron for the district; the details are the period's common form]. The shed: log posts, a lean-to roof of boards and turf, split wood stacked in rows against the back, a chopping block and axe. At every door a flat doorstep; by the dwellings a chopping block with chips, a plank bench, a rain tub, a ladder to the eaves; drying racks with linen and wool; a trough by each byre; field-stone cairns at the meadow corners; a dry-stone wall of cleared stones about each farm's infield with a gap for the beasts and the cart (innmark, utmark [inferred]); a track trodden from every farm to the church's gate; birches and willows sheltering the yards and the river bank.
- **The churchyard (L3, 2026-11-11):** graves in rows east–west, mostly south of the church (the usual side) and few on the north, each a low turf mound with a weathered wooden cross at its head and now and then a stone slab; a path of flat stones from the gate to the west porch [inferred: the grave forms are the common medieval Norwegian ones; no source gives Borgund's]. A woman kneels at a grave.
- **The river (L3):** women wash linen at the bank, boys fish with lines, a man sits mending at the landing; nets hang to dry on two racks by the boathouse [inferred: after the working life of a Norwegian river farm]. More fish schools, a second heron, swallows nesting at every second farm.
- **The outlying huts (stølar):** three small smoke-room huts with a byre and a store, goats and cows at their yards, a milkmaid with her yoke [stated: summer pastures and herding, Landslov exhibition]; they stand low on the valley's head, not high on the slope, because the slope is too steep to build on here [inferred: compromise].
- **The masks on the staves (L3, 2026-11-07):** "all the intermediate staves end in carved animal heads and masks"; the masks at the top of the central room's staves have their parallels in stone architecture (Værnes, Mære); the staves either side of the chancel opening have masks hidden in the half-darkness; the St Andrew's crosses have carved leaf forms; the south portal's plain shafts carry capitals with a pair of enthroned lions, its bases shaped as downward-facing cat-like heads [stated: SNL "Borgund stavkirke"]. Ours: on each of the ten intermediate staves of the central room, under the wall plate and turned into the room, a carved head about 0.5 m tall, men's masks and beasts' heads in turn — the man with a heavy brow, bulging eyes, a long nose, a mouth, a pointed beard and the hair over his brow; the beast with pricked ears, round eyes, a thrust-out muzzle and a row of teeth. [inferred: their forms and the alternation; the chancel staves' masks and the south portal's lions recorded for later]
- **The stue inside (L3, 2026-11-07; `src/styles/norse/stue.js`):** the dwelling of the period was the open-hearth smoke-room house without a chimney (see "Farm buildings" above: the ljore on the ridge). Ours (the larger stue of each of the eight farms, `open`): the door's leaf standing open into one room open to the roof, its earthen floor; the logs blackened by smoke above a line 1.4 m up round the walls, the gable ends and the roof's underside black; the hearth (åre) in the middle of the floor — a kerb of stones round a bed of ash and embers, a fire of split logs — under the cross-beam, a soapstone pot hung over it on its chain; fixed benches (the sleeping places) along both long walls, skins and a red blanket on them; the high seat between two carved posts in the middle of the long wall away from the door, its back board; the warp-weighted loom leaning against the far gable wall — two uprights, the cloth beam, the heddle rod, the warp of linen thread with its row of soapstone weights, a striped cloth woven at its top; an iron-bound chest; a rotary quern of two stones and a wooden bucket by the door. [inferred: the arrangement and sizes, from the Norwegian smoke-room house as generally described; soapstone vessels and loom weights are the common finds of the period]
- **Farm work and weathering (L3, 2026-11-07):** at every farm someone carrying water from the river on a shoulder yoke, and at every other a man at the woodpile; the climate set damp (0.85): moss on the north faces and the shingled roofs, rain streaks down the tarred wood. [inferred]

- **The landing, the boats and the hay racks (batch, 2026-11-11; `src/styles/norse/shore.js`):** Norwegian boatbuilding of the period was clinker (lapstrake) work: planks lapped along their edges and riveted, on a keel with raised curling stems, the small færing (four-oared) and the larger six-oared boat with a single square wool sail on a yard; the boathouse (naust) was a long, low log building with a steep turfed roof and one open end toward the water, the boat drawn up on a slip of laid logs [stated: Wikipedia "Clinker (boat building)"; "Færing"; "Naust"; Norsk Folkemuseum on boathouses]. Hay was dried on tall racks of posts and wires (hesje) in the meadows, the half-dried hay hung out in bundles [stated: Wikipedia "Hesje"; Landslov exhibition, University of Bergen]. Borgund lies about 40 km up the Lærdal valley from the Sognefjord; the river was no sea-road, so the landing here is on the river and is ours [inferred: stands for the valley's link with the fjord at Lærdalsøyri, goods came up by packhorse; the quay is our design]. Built: a timber quay with a plank stair down the 3 m bank and a deck on piles driven into the bed, mooring posts, a coil of rope; a naust with log walls, back gable with a door, bark-and-sod roof and a log slip, a beached boat before it; a six-oared boat (mast, yard, furled sail, steering oar, thwarts, oars shipped) and a færing afloat at the quay; eight hay racks in rows in the meadows beside the odd-numbered farms. Strake count, dimensions and positions are ours [inferred].
- **Living systems (batch, 2026-11-11):** `movers.js` — walkers by trade (farmers in wool tunics and hose with caps and cloaks, women in long dresses with shawls, water carriers with yokes, labourers with poles, herders with staves, boys, the priest) on the tracks between the yards, the church's gate and the quay; `fauna.js` — trout in the river pools, swallows, crows, sparrows in the yards, a grey heron on the bank; `smoke.js` — the stues' hearths smoking through the ljore day and night (stronger at meals), morning mist on the river. ## 3. Orientation

The church runs west to east: the door is in the west and the chancel and apse are in the east. [stated]

## 4. The style

Tarred pine staves and shingles; round logs notched at the corners; sod on the dwellings. [stated]

## 5. The town

A scattered farm settlement with no village: about a hundred log buildings in fifteen yards and three outlying huts. [inferred]

## 6. Ground and water

Hay meadows and small grain fields on the floor; a stony river with willows; pine and spruce on the sides, with rock showing higher up. [inferred]

## 7. Sky and climate

The cool, changeable light of a summer at 61° N, often cloudy, with rain from the west. [inferred]

## 8. Paths

Arrival is at the churchyard's gate on its south side, then round to the west porch. [inferred]

## 9. Sound

Plainchant in the church; the lur and the herding calls (kulokk) in the pastures. [stated: Wikipedia "Kulning"; "Lur"]
