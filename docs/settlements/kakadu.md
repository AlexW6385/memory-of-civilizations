## 0. What is shown, and what is not

- Ubirr's paintings are the shared, public art of Bininj/Mungguy country; the neighbouring clans (Bunitj, Manilagarr, Mandjurlgunj) look after the site, and "the act of painting is generally more important than the painting itself" so older works are covered by newer ones [stated: Parks Australia, "Ubirr"; Kakadu "Rock art"]. Some paintings could be made only by people holding the right knowledge, and creation-ancestor sites were repainted [stated: kakadu.gov.au "Rock art"].
- So we paint only the public subjects the park itself shows: food animals (fish, turtles, wallabies, goanna, geese), people hunting and carrying, hand stencils, and layers of older faded paint under newer. **No named ancestral figure, no Rainbow Serpent, no Lightning Man, no copy of any particular panel**: every motif is a generic figure in the X-ray manner, drawn by us [inferred: generic, respectful]. Nothing sorcery-related; nothing that is restricted.

## 1. The real place

- **Ubirr:** a group of rock outcrops with several natural shelters on the edge of the Nadab floodplain in the East Alligator region [stated: Wikipedia "Ubirr"]. A short walk from the car park passes the main art sites and leads to the foot of Ubirr Rock; the main circuit is 1 km, and a steep climb of about 250 m leads to a rocky lookout with 360° views of Arnhem Land and the floodplain [stated: Parks Australia "Ubirr walk"; Wikipedia]. The shelters were cool places where people camped, with smooth stone surfaces good for painting; the art is layered [stated: Parks Australia]. Groups historically camped under the shelters and used the East Alligator River and the Nadab floodplain [stated: Dept of Agriculture, Water and the Environment, "Ubirr"].
- **The Main Gallery:** the most photographed of the three galleries; many X-ray paintings, mostly of the Freshwater period (within the last 1500 years), of fish, waterfowl, mussels, wallabies, goannas, echidnas and yams [stated: Wikipedia; DAWE "Ubirr"]; barramundi, catfish, mullet, long-necked and pig-nosed turtles, rock ringtail possums [stated: Wikipedia]. Newer paintings are often painted over older ones [stated: DAWE]. The gallery's size is not given by these sources: ours is about 46 m of painted wall under a low overhang [inferred: from photographs; the walk's circuit is 1 km].
- **Nourlangie (Burrungkuy) for comparison:** an outlier of the Arnhem Land escarpment; the Anbangbang shelter was first occupied more than 6000 years ago, used occasionally up to 20 000 years ago, and used intensively 800 to 1200 years ago, when the nearby lagoon was fully formed [stated: Wikipedia "Nourlangie Rock"]. That intensity is the moment we show [inferred].
- **Moment shown:** about AD 1000, the late Freshwater period, at the end of the wet season, before the monsoon storms; before any contact art (the first Macassan, Chinese and European images come later) [stated: periods; inferred: the date].

## 2. The art: periods, style, pigments (what the paintings are made of)

- **Periods:** Pre-estuarine (about 50 000–8000 years ago), Estuarine (about 8000–1500 years ago, river valleys flooded, mangrove swamps), Freshwater (under 1500 years ago: billabongs and paperbark swamps replaced the saltwater systems) [stated: "Styles of rock art", Parks Australia/Environment Australia]. Freshwater subjects: waterlilies, magpie geese, people with goose spears, goose-wing fans, complex spear-throwers and didgeridoos [stated: same].
- **X-ray art:** "shows the external shape and internal structures of humans, animals and objects"; the later decorative X-ray divides the body into sections and loses interest in the organs [stated: same]. Ours: a fish in outline with a backbone line, a swim bladder, a gut, ribs and parallel infill bands; a turtle with its shell plates and inner patches; a wallaby with heart and gut.
- **Pigments and tools:** pigments crushed on a stone palette and mixed with water to a paste; brushes of human hair, reeds, feathers and chewed sticks; pigment blown round objects by mouth to make stencils (hand stencils at Ubirr); haematite, a reddish iron oxide, lasts longest, which is why most old paintings are red [stated: kakadu.gov.au "Rock art"]. Mainly yellow, white and red ochre [stated: Wikipedia "Nourlangie Rock"]. Ours: red ochre (`clay_robe`, fresh `paint_minoan_red`), yellow ochre (`paint_ochre`), white clay (`paint_white`), charcoal (`paint_minoan_black`).
- **Layers:** the oldest layers faded to a dull dark red, newer paint of white and yellow on top and across them [stated: layering, kakadu.gov.au; inferred: the colours' order].

## 3. The rock

- Outliers of the Arnhem Land sandstone plateau: sandstone with horizontal bedding, broken by vertical joints into big blocks, weathered into boulder piles with shelters under overhanging slabs; the faces warm pink, buff and red-brown, streaked dark where water runs [inferred: general knowledge of Kombolgie Sandstone outliers; not given by the sources above]. Ours: blocks of rounded, jointed sandstone stacked in tiers; the Main Gallery's roof a slab on its end piers over a back wall.
- Size: the shelter's back wall about 3.4–4.2 m high, the roof slab's lip about 8 m from the wall; the outlier about 16 m over the Main Gallery and about 22 m at the lookout [inferred].
- Rangers fit silicon drip lines to turn water from the paintings, and boardwalks and rails keep people from the walls [stated: DAWE]; in AD 1000 none: the floor is earth.

## 4. The country round it

- **Floodplain and billabong:** the end of the wet is Yekke (May to mid-June): morning mists over the plains, water lilies cover the shallow wetlands; Wurrkeng (mid-June to mid-August): creeks stop flowing, the floodplain dries, magpie geese crowd the shrinking billabongs, patch burning goes on; Kurrung (hot, dry, mid-August to mid-October): wood swallows arrive, long-necked turtles are hunted, "goose time" [stated: kakadu.gov.au "Seasons"]. Ours: the dry season, late Wurrkeng: low water, lilies in flower, geese crowded.
- **Paperbark** (`Melaleuca`, the weeping or white paperbark): trees under 20 m with thick papery white bark, weeping branches, narrow curved leaves, cream flower spikes, along rivers and streams; its bark was tied to frames for waterproof huts, wrapped round food for cooking underground and taken in large pieces for canoes [stated: Wikipedia "Melaleuca leucadendra"]. Ours: 7–12 m, white-cream trunks with peeling sheets, drooping crowns, grouped round the billabong.
- **Pandanus** (`Pandanus spiralis`, screw palm): to about 10 m, slender, often clumped, leaves 1–2 m long and 4–7 cm wide spiralling round the trunk and crowded at the branch tips, the old leaf bases leaving a spiral on the trunk, fruit an orange-red cluster of segments; grows on creek banks and floodplain margins; leaves woven into baskets, mats and fish traps, leaf fibre made into string for dilly bags [stated: Wikipedia]. Ours: 3–7 m, clumps on the banks.
- **Magpie geese:** black-and-white, long necks and legs, hooked bill, orange legs and feet, 75–90 cm; noisy flocks of up to thousands outside breeding; on open wetlands eating grass, seeds, spike-rush bulbs; honking call [stated: Wikipedia "Magpie goose"]; an important food since the wetlands formed about 1500 years ago, shown in rock art, including figures with goose-feather fans [stated: same]. The goose is the Freshwater period's animal, with the lilies [stated: Styles of rock art].
- **Woodland on the rock and the plain's edge:** stringybark (*Eucalyptus tetrodonta*) is the usual Top End woodland [inferred; the bark is named as a shelter material in the aboriginalculture.com.au page below]; flowering Darwin woollybutt signals the time for patch burning [stated: kakadu.gov.au "Seasons"]. Ours: slender gums with a thin, open crown, grass of the woodland floor burnt in patches.
- **Termite mounds** on the dry floodplain edge [inferred: usual in Top End savanna; not sourced]; they are low earth cones.
- **Light and sky:** a high hot sun, clear skies for most of the dry season, the sky growing to storms toward the build-up (Kunumeleng) [stated: seasons]. Ours: clear weighted, a build-up of clouds, rain rare.

## 5. The camp

- **Shelters:** bark huts (paperbark or stringybark sheets over a wooden frame, waterproof), lean-tos of leafy branches or large bark sheets leaned against a tree, rock or sapling frame, sapling-frame shelters covered with bark or leafy branches, domed shelters of bent sticks; natural rock shelters were wet-season shelter, some with stringybark beds on low wooden frames; small fires inside gave warmth and smoke against mosquitoes; floors of earth, bark sheets or woven mats for bedding; large woven mats; explorers noted paintings on the inside walls of some bark shelters [stated: aboriginalculture.com.au "Housing and shelters"]. Stringybark was chopped into large sheets with stone axes [stated: same]. Ours: a bark hut (a frame of poles, three slanting sheets and a ridge), and a lean-to of sheets against a pole frame, each with a fire at its mouth and a mat inside, sized 2.5–3.5 m [inferred: sizes].
- **Containers and weaving:** dilly bags (string bags) woven from bush string teased from the bark of banyan, kurrajong and other plants; pandanus-leaf strips woven into baskets (coiled basketry is later, 1922); stringybark and paperbark folded into shallow trough-like water carriers, the ends pleated and tied with bush string; a grindstone among the five main items found in each hut [stated: aboriginalculture.com.au "Aboriginal women's bags, baskets and containers"]. Ours: dilly bags hung from the poles and on shoulders; folded bark troughs by the fire; a flat grinding stone with its handstone; a woman weaving a pandanus mat.
- **Food:** magpie geese and their eggs, long-necked turtles, fish, file snakes, goannas; yams; the stranded animals of the wet [stated: seasons; Wikipedia]. Ours: geese and a goanna hung on a rack; a turtle roasting in the coals; fish on a bark tray; yams.
- **Weapons and tools:** goose spears, spear-throwers (the Freshwater paintings show "complex spear-throwers") [stated: Styles of rock art]; digging sticks [inferred]; stone axes [stated: aboriginalculture.com.au].
- **Dress:** little: men and women mostly unclothed or with a waist belt and a small covering of fibre or hair string; body paint in ochre and white clay; arm and neck bands of pandanus [inferred: the sources give only the body art in the paintings, the pandanus bands from Wikipedia "Pandanus spiralis"]. Ours: fibre-string belts and small aprons, a few headbands of white, pandanus armbands, ochre; dark skin tones. Figures of the engine's kit are kept modest.
- **Dogs:** the dingo has been in Australia for several thousand years [inferred: general knowledge]; ours: a few tan dingoes at the camp.

## 7. Orientation and sound

- The rock's face looks south over the floodplain; at sunset the light is on the paintings from the west [inferred: the walk is recommended for sunset, Parks Australia].
- **Sound:** geese honking, wood swallows, kites, the whistle of a crow-like call [inferred]; frogs by the water at dusk; wind in the paperbark; a drone and clapsticks (didgeridoos are shown in Freshwater rock art [stated: Styles of rock art]). Ours: a low drone and a clapstick beat under the wind.

## 8. Deepening pass (what was added, and its sources)

- **Use of the shelters:** Ubirr was a favoured camping place in the wet season and its faces were painted and repainted for millennia [stated: Met Museum, "Ubirr"]. So the shelters are furnished as a camp, not a gallery: hearths under the overhang with the roof blackened over them, grass beds with a bark sheet beside, stacks of bark, spears with a spear-thrower, hanging dilly bags, yams on bark, a mat being woven, painters' stones with lumps of red, yellow and white ochre, shell cups and brushes, and a pole ladder lashed with fibre for the high panels [inferred: from the reported use; the items from the sources below].
- **Painting practice:** pigments crushed on a stone palette and mixed with water; brushes of hair, feathers, reeds and chewed sticks [stated: kakadu.gov.au "Rock art"; Met: "brushes of bark, feathers, or chewed sticks"]. The oldest layer is the Pre-estuarine red, with slender animated "dynamic" figures in head-dresses [stated: Met]; we draw only generic slender figures in faded red, never named beings. New motifs (all generic): yams, an echidna, rows of tally dots; patches where the surface has scaled off and taken paint with it [inferred: weathering of sandstone; flaking is general knowledge, not sourced here].
- **Bags and containers:** soft bags of bush string made from bark fibre; pandanus leaf strips for coiled work (a later introduction in Arnhem Land, so only plain pandanus mats and strips are shown, no coiled baskets); paperbark and stringybark folded into shallow troughs, tied with bush string [stated: aboriginalculture.com.au, "Aboriginal women's bags, baskets and containers"]. Ours: string dilly bags on a rail, a folded bark trough at two fires, a conical woven fish trap, a pandanus mat with its strips.
- **Bark canoes:** paperbark was taken in large pieces for canoes [stated: Wikipedia "Melaleuca leucadendra", §4]. Ours: three sewn-bark canoes of 3.4 m with upturned ends, spreaders, and a clay pad for a fire; one poled on the billabong, one with a seated woman, one drawn up on the west margin [inferred: size and form].
- **Woodland:** stringybark gums (grey fibrous trunk, sparse crown) on the dry ground and the rock's tops, cycads (Cycas, short trunk and crown of arching fronds), spinifex and dry-grass tufts thick at the rock's foot, fallen logs, scree of sandstone fragments at the foot of the faces [inferred: Top End woodland; §4].
- **Six camps** (was two): under the small shelter's apron, on the apron before the arrival, on the billabong's west bank, by the east hollow; each with its goods, a dog, a woman at work, trodden ground [inferred]. Smoke from every hearth. People sit on the ledges and the summit over the plain.
- **Still below the standard:** no photographed reference for the rock's drip streaks and lichen (left to the damp of the climate); the paintings stay generic by design.
