# Persepolis (the terrace of the Achaemenid kings), about 480 BC — dossier

Settlement id: `persepolis` · Style: `achaemenid` · Region: Persia (Fars, the Marvdasht plain) · Code: `src/settlements/persepolis/`

The Unfinished Gate is not built: it was begun under Artaxerxes III (about 338 BC), after the moment shown.

## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the terrace, about 455 m north–south by 300 m east–west and 14 m above the plain [stated: satyori; bananas-for-scale], part cut from and part built against the foot of Kuh-e Rahmat [stated: studiomatrx]. It is reached by a double stair of two flights [stated: universalis], about 7 m wide [stated: bananas-for-scale]. The Gate of All Nations stands at its head [stated: studiomatrx]. The Apadana had 72 columns of about 20 m [stated: geographyworlds, satyori], 8.65 m apart and 1.6 m thick [stated: peymanmeli]; Wikipedia gives 24 m. The Hall of a Hundred Columns is 70 × 70 m [stated: Wikipedia "Persian column"]. Column heights: about 17 m at the Gate, about 12 m in the Hundred Columns, 7 m in the Treasury, the Hadish and the Queen's Palace [stated: cgtrader text].
- **Moment shown:** about 480 BC, under Xerxes: the Apadana and the Hadish finished, the Hundred Columns begun (shown complete).

## 3. Orientation

The terrace's sides run roughly with the cardinal directions; the stair faces west over the plain. [stated: plans]

## 4. The style

Dark grey limestone for the terrace, stairs, columns and door frames; thick mudbrick walls, plastered; tall slender fluted columns with bell bases and double-protome capitals carrying cedar beams; flat earth roofs; stepped merlons on the parapets; winged human-headed bulls at the gate. [stated: columns, capitals; inferred: plaster colour, merlons on roofs]

## 5. The town

Flat-roofed mudbrick houses round small courts, with gardens of poplar, cypress and plane trees. [inferred]

## 6. Ground and water

The dry plain; the bare mountain behind; orchards and fields at the edge. [inferred]

## 7. Sky and climate

High and clear; spring clouds sometimes. [inferred]

## 8. Paths

From the town along the road to the stair, up either flight to the landing, back to the top, through the Gate of All Nations. [inferred]

## 9. Sound

Harps, lyres, double pipes and drums are shown in the art of the Near East; little is known of Persian court music. [inferred; a lyre, a double pipe and a drone stand in]

## 10. Detail (2026-11-06)

**Columns.** Bases are stone, round, often fluted; large shafts at Persepolis have 40 or 48 shallow flutes with arrises, the flute width nearly constant so that thicker shafts carry more of them; capitals are much longer than in other orders: a lower bell of drooping plant ornament, an upper "palm capital" spreading as it rises, on the larger capitals a long fluted square zone with double volutes at its top and inverted volutes at its foot, and over all two animals back to back — bulls most often, also lions, man-headed bulls and griffins — whose flat backs carry the timbers; horns and ears were separate pieces; columns were polished and at least the capitals painted [stated: Wikipedia "Persian column"]. Ours: a square plinth, a bell base hung with petals and a torus; 48 flutes on the Apadana's shafts (1.6 m), 40 on the thinner; the volute zone on columns of 16 m and more (the Apadana, the Gate) and left out on the lower halls; two kneeling bulls, forelegs folded, in polished dark stone (`marble_dark`) with the main cedar beam lying across their saddle and the ceiling above [inferred: the proportions of each part, the kneeling pose, bulls everywhere].

**The Apadana's stairways.** Two monumental stairways on the north and east [stated: ancient-history-sites; ISAC]; the north stairway has inner and outer flights and east and west wings [stated: ISAC]. The east stair's central panel shows eight guards, Medes and Persians, with an empty space between them, and above them the winged disc between two sphinxes; the guards replaced a first panel of the king's audience [stated: Livius; Lonely Planet: "a ring with wings" and two winged human-headed lions]. The triangular sections carry repeated lions attacking bulls [stated: ancient-history-sites; romeartlover]; four lion-and-bull scenes on the courtiers' side [stated: Livius]. The north wing shows Persian dignitaries, the Imperial Guard and the Immortals, Persians in fluted (feathered) headdresses and Medes in round caps, in three tiers [stated: Lonely Planet]; the south wing the 23 delegations bringing gifts — rams, camels, oxen, mules among them [stated: Lonely Planet; ancient-history-sites]; stylised trees, a cypress among them, separate the groups [stated: ISAC; romeartlover]; guards stand on the flights [stated: ISAC]; the soldiers are never shown fighting, always parading [stated: romeartlover]. Ours: on the north face (74 m between the towers) and the east face (57 m), a block 32 × 7.5 m projecting with two flights of 26 steps of 10 cm rising toward its middle [inferred: the composition from the flights; the 10 cm risers of the terrace stair kept]; the central panel, the lion and bull in each triangle, six guards climbing each parapet, stepped merlons along the parapets and the wings, rosette bands above and below; three registers of Persians and Medes on both wings of the north stair and on the east stair's north wing, delegations (an usher, three bearers with vessels, a ram) divided by cypresses on its south wing. The figures are cut in polished stone about 6–9 cm proud, their edges slanted, belts, sleeves and pleats raised within them [inferred: depth, figure sizes 0.63–1.1 m, the order of the delegations; the paint left for later]. The old stair stopped 6 m short of the plinth and the corner towers hung 7 m past it in the air; both mended (the towers now rise from the terrace).

**Doors.** Doorways and windows of the palaces are framed in dark stone with an Egyptian cornice [inferred, as §4]; the doorways of the Hundred Columns carried reliefs [stated: romeartlover, "Above each doore is ingraven the Idea of a Majestique Monarch" (Herbert 1638)]. Ours: jambs the depth of the wall and a lintel in polished stone, a roll, the flaring gorge of upright leaves and a fillet; on the jambs of the Hundred Columns' doors the royal hero stabbing a rearing lion, his dagger gilt [inferred: the hero on all four doors — the north doors had the enthroned king carried by the nations, left for later].

## 11. Detail (L2+): the colossi, the Hundred-Column Hall's doorways, the colours

- **The Gate of All Nations.** One large room on four columns with bell-shaped bases, a stone bench along the inner walls broken at the doorways, very large stone doorways in the east, west and south walls (the south, toward the Apadana, the widest); "a pair of massive bulls secured the western entrance", at the eastern "two Lamassu in the Assyrian style, albeit of colossal proportions"; above each of the four colossi a trilingual inscription of Xerxes [stated: Wikipedia, Gate of All Nations]; the entrance protected by "bulls with the head of a bearded man" [stated: Livius]. Ours (`lamassu_gate`, `lamassu`): the west pair bulls without wings, the east pair winged bulls with men's heads; their foreparts out past the facade, their bodies lining the passage (the doorway widened to 7.6 m for them); carved square in polished stone — four columnar legs with hooves, cleft claws and dewclaws, forequarters, barrel and haunches, a fringe of locks along the belly, the tail with its tuft; on both flanks the wing: a plate set with four rows of covert feathers and twelve long primaries sweeping up and curving forward at the tips; the man's head with almond eyes, brows, nose, cheeks and lips, bull's ears with gold rings, the long square beard in six rows of curls, moustache, curls at the nape, the tall crown on its band of three pairs of horns with a band of rosettes and a crest of feathers; the bull's head with dewlap, muzzle, nostrils, eyes, level ears, horns and brow curls [inferred: forms after photographs of the Persepolis colossi; four legs, not the Assyrian five]. Above each colossus on the jamb a panel of three columns of incised lines (Old Persian, Elamite, Babylonian) [inferred: layout]; the bench round the inner walls; the south door added.
- **The Hundred-Column Hall's doorways.** The eight doorways are decorated "on the south and north with reliefs of throne scenes" and on the east and west with the king in combat with monsters; the north doorways show the audience scene with guards; the south doorways throne-bearers and the enthroned king with an attendant; the northern portico "flanked by two colossal stone bulls" [stated: ISAC, The Throne Hall]. Ours (src/styles/achaemenid/throne.js): on the north doorways' jambs five registers of guards, Persians and Medes by turns with upright spears, under the audience — the king on his lion-footed throne, his feet on a stool, staff and lotus in his hands, the crown prince behind the throne, two incense burners, the official bowing with his hand at his mouth, the baldachin with its rosette band, fringe and posts, the winged figure above; on the south doorways the throne-dais carried in three tiers of four bearers with raised arms in three kinds of headdress between its blue lion-footed legs, the king enthroned on it under the baldachin and the winged figure [inferred: the number of tiers and bearers, the headdresses, the composition]; the east and west doorways keep the hero and the lion. The two portico bulls stand on antae flanking the north portico.
- **Colour (sourced traces only).** On a fragment of the winged figure from this hall: Egyptian blue in the hair and beard; the robe bright and dark red, the sleeve perhaps purple; the three rows of small feathers red, blue, red, the three rows of long feathers green, red, green, the long feathers with blue eyespots; no paint on the background. The bull on the northern portico preserves extensive traces of blue on its skin. Blues on the king's beard (Darius's tomb). Herzfeld (1923) at this hall's south-west doorway: "the throne legs laid bare were of a bright blue color", red ochre higher on the throne. Gold leaf on the edge of the king's cloak (the Tripylon) [stated: Nagel et al., An Achaemenid God in Color, Heritage 2024]. Ours: the winged figure's hair and beard blue, robe red, sleeve dark red, wing rows and eyespots as found; the king's beard and hair blue, the edges of his cloak gilded; the throne's and the dais's legs bright blue, its seat, back and the dais's top tier red ochre; the portico bulls painted blue; the background bare stone.

## 12. Detail (2026-11-06, third pass): the palaces' platforms and stairs, the Tachara, the Hadish, the Treasury

- **Why the Hundred Columns could not be reached.** Its platform stood 0.8 m above the terrace with no step up to it, and its walls (3.5 m) stood 0.5 m out past the platform's edge in the air (the Apadana's south wall 2 m out). Now every hall's platform reaches 0.5 m beyond its walls where there is no portico, and a flight of 10 cm steps rises before the portico and before each other door [inferred: the platform's height kept; the steps' riser that of the terrace stair]. The Tripylon (1.2 m) has flights on the west and the south; the Tachara and the Hadish their double stairs. The wings' parapet merlons of the Apadana's stairways hung 2.5 cm out past the plinth's edge; they now stand on it.
- **The terrace stair** is kept as it was: two flights rising apart from the middle of the west face to landings, turning back to arrive together at the top, shallow steps of 10 cm [stated: Lonely Planet "Grand Stairway" (111 steps, each shallow); worldhistory.org (wide enough to ride up)].
- **The Tachara (palace of Darius).** About 1,160 m², the smallest palace on the terrace, its platform about 2.5 m above the Apadana's court, of grey stone polished almost black; facing south, its portico on the south with rooms either side; a main hall of about 15.15 × 15.42 m on three rows of four columns; stairways either side of the portico, a double reversed staircase down to the south court (the west stair is Artaxerxes III's, not built here); the stair walls carved with servants, attendants and soldiers in Median and Persian dress; inscriptions on "all 18 niches and window frames"; a complete window about 2.65 × 2.65 × 1.70 m cut from one block of some 18 t; the main doorframe three monoliths; on the main doorways Darius leaving the palace with two attendants, one holding a parasol, the other a towel and fly whisk, his crown gilded; on the smaller east and west doorways the king stabbing a lion [stated: ancient-history-sites "Tachara"]. The south stairs carry servants bringing food; the lintels are Egyptianizing [stated: Livius "Palace of Darius"]; at the stair's centre a long trilingual inscription of Xerxes [stated: romeartlover]; servants carrying animals and food in covered dishes [stated: Iran Chamber; it puts the procession on the east and west jambs and the combats on the north and south — we follow ancient-history-sites]. Ours: the hall 16 m square on 4 × 3 columns, the portico of eight (two rows of four) on the south, walls 1.7 m thick, the platform 3 m high and 6 m wide round it (about 1,000 m²); in each wall either side of the door a window, each a frame of the dark stone the depth of the wall, 2.65 m high with its raised sill, lintel and Egyptian cornice; the main (north and south) doorways with the king walking out, crown and robe's edge gilded, staff and lotus, the parasol-bearer behind holding the parasol over him and the attendant with fly whisk and towel; the king and the lion on the east and west doors; the double stair up to the portico (two flights of 30 steps rising toward the middle), the trilingual inscription between guards at its centre, servants (bearers, Medes and kids) climbing its parapets and walking toward it on the platform's face in three registers [inferred: the window's opening 1.2 × 1.3 m, eight windows for the 18 openings, the figures' sizes; the side rooms left out].
- **The Hadish (palace of Xerxes).** Twice the Tachara, on a platform cut from the rock; a square hall of 36 columns with 19 windows and 4 niches; a 12-column portico on the north; a balcony on the south; two side apartments; two double reversed staircases up from the west and east courts with sculpture like the Tachara's; the jambs with Xerxes and his parasol- and towel-bearers; the inner faces of the windows with people carrying utensils or leading wild goats [stated: Kaveh Farrokh "The Palace of Xerxes"; Livius "Palace of Xerxes"]. Ours: the 6 × 6 hall and the portico of twelve kept; doors on all four sides, the king scene on the north and south; four windows in each wall (sixteen), a servant carved on each inner face of their frames; the two double stairs on the west and east, servants climbing and in the registers, the winged disc and sphinxes at their centres [inferred: the windows' number and places, the doors east and west].
- **The Treasury.** Begun by Darius I, finished by his successors; archive, storehouse, armoury and, at its front, a reception hall with reliefs of Darius and Xerxes [stated: worldhistory.org]; halls of over 10,000 m² [stated: Heritage Institute]; two large reliefs found here show Darius enthroned greeted by a dignitary, Xerxes behind the king, followed by officials; its most spacious room served as a court of reception before the Throne Hall was built [stated: Iran Chamber]. At Persepolis columns were of wood (cedar, teak) where stone was not used, on stone bases, the structure finished with bright paint [stated: Heritage Institute]. Ours (`treasury`): thick plastered walls round 80 × 140 m, a door on the west; the north hall of 10 × 10 wooden columns, the hall of 9 × 11 to the south-east, a court with porticoes on its north and east sides and at the middle of each back wall the audience relief on a panel of the dark stone (guards, the official bowing, two censers, the king enthroned in colour as in §11, the crown prince, an attendant, guards), five long storerooms opening off the court's south side; the columns on square stone bases with a torus, the shafts plastered and painted red ochre with white collars, a cedar block under the beams; cedar ceilings and flat earth roofs [inferred: the plan's arrangement, the numbers of columns after the halls' common names, the colour].
