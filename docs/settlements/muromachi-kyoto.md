## 1. Moment and choice of landmark

- **Moment shown: about 1490**, the year Ashikaga Yoshimasa died and his Higashiyama villa became the Zen temple Jishō-ji [stated: Wikipedia, Ginkaku-ji]. It is the "Higashiyama culture": the dry landscape garden, the shoin room, the tea of Murata Jukō, wabi taste, Noh [stated: DESIGN §7 row; general]. The brief for this settlement said "c. 1450"; Ryōan-ji was founded in 1450 [stated: Wikipedia, Ryōan-ji] and rebuilt about 1488–1499 after the Ōnin War (1467–77) [stated: Wikipedia, Ryōan-ji], so 1490 holds both and Ginkaku-ji's Tōgu-dō (1486), which 1450 would not.
- **Landmark A: Ginkaku-ji / Jishō-ji** (the Silver Pavilion, Kannon-den, and the Tōgu-dō with its Dōjinsai). Kannon-den begun 21 Feb 1482, two storeys, modelled on Yoshimitsu's Kinkaku; the silver foil was planned and never applied; building stopped in the Ōnin War era and the pavilion stands as Yoshimasa last saw it; the name "Silver Pavilion" is Edo-period [stated: Wikipedia, Ginkaku-ji]. Ours is therefore bare dark timber, as it is. Yoshimasa moved stones and pines from the Flowery Palace (Hana no Gosho) and Muromachi hall to the villa in 1474 [stated: same]. Garden: moss, a pond shaping the design, the northeast room planned as a library lit by light off the pond [stated: same].
- **Landmark B: Ryōan-ji, the hōjō and its rock garden.** Founded 1450 by Hosokawa Katsumoto on an old Fujiwara estate with a pond (Kyōyōchi, 12th c.); destroyed in the Ōnin War, rebuilt by his son Hosokawa Masamoto about 1488; the garden: a rectangle 25 × 10 m, 15 stones in five groups (5, 2, 3, 2, 3), white gravel, moss only round the stones, a clay wall stained brown and orange by age, viewed seated from the hōjō veranda so that not all fifteen show at once [stated: Wikipedia, Ryōan-ji]. Its date is disputed (second half of the 15th c. to the Edo period; the earliest descriptions of 1680–82 show nine stones; the present form comes from the rebuild after the fire of 1779) [stated: same]. We show it as in the standard attribution to the late 15th c. [inferred, flagged: a possible but unproven state].
- Kinkaku-ji (Rokuon-ji) also stood (1399; three storeys in shinden, buke and Zen styles, the top two gilded, 12.5 m high, a pyramidal shingled roof with a bronze phoenix, over the Mirror Pond with ten islets) [stated: Wikipedia, Kinkaku-ji]. Not built in this pass; the Kannon-den is the landmark. [Left out for the density of the two precincts.]

## 3. The landmark: Kannon-den (Ginkaku)

- **Plan [inferred from photographs and the building's bays]:** lower floor 5 × 4 bays (≈ 10.5 × 8.4 m at 2.1 m a bay), upper floor 3 × 3 bays (≈ 6.3 m square); height to the ridge ornament about 13 m. Two storeys [stated: Wikipedia]. A pent roof (mokoshi) skirts the upper floor over the lower; the upper floor has the cusped windows (katōmado) of Zen halls and a pyramidal (hōgyō) roof of cypress bark (hiwadabuki) with a bronze phoenix [inferred: standard for the building].
- **Style:** the lower floor in the manner of the shoin houses, the upper in Zen style [inferred: the building's usual description; Kinkaku's pattern copied].
- Openings on the south-east face the pond; the lower room's sliding shutters (shitomi-do) open to a verandah [inferred].
- **Materials:** unfinished cypress boards and posts darkened by 500 years of weather would be anachronistic; at 1490 the timber is new, black-brown lacquered on the posts and rails, plain on the walls; roofs of cypress bark in warm brown [inferred]. Palette: `wood_dark`, `lacquer_black`, `roof_bark`, `plaster_white`.

### 3.1 Tōgu-dō (1486 [stated: Wikipedia])
- Built 1485–86 as Yoshimasa's private hall of Buddhist worship (jibutsudō), about 7 m square, hip-and-gable roof (irimoya) of cypress bark; four rooms: the largest with the altar, east of it the narrow four-mat ni-no-ma, which opens into the **Dōjinsai**, and the large empty san-no-ma beside it separated by sliding screens (fusuma) [stated: JAANUS, Tōgudō].
- **Dōjinsai**: a 4½-mat room; along the north wall a writing bay with sliding panels opening on the view — a tsukeshoin, perhaps the oldest example; to its left a half-bay alcove with staggered shelves (chigaidana); the remains of a hearth found in 1965 were not rebuilt [stated: JAANUS]. This is the model for the shoin room. The 4½-mat plan is the same size Jukō's wabi tea room took [stated: general; the link is the standard account].
- Ours: the Tōgu-dō is open from its verandah into the ni-no-ma and the Dōjinsai (the sliding screens drawn back); mats laid in the Muromachi way (all mats laid, a half-mat in the middle); the tokonoma is not in the Dōjinsai itself (it is not documented there), so none is built in it; the room has the tsukeshoin and the chigaidana [stated], a low hearth is left out [stated: none rebuilt].

## 4. The landmark: Ryōan-ji's hōjō and rock garden

- The 15 stones in five groups, 5-2-3-2-3, on a 25 × 10 m field of white gravel raked in lines along the long axis and in rings round the stones, each stone with a moss collar, the clay wall (oil-stained, brown-orange) low on three sides behind, with a roofed top (bark) [stated: Wikipedia]. The hōjō (abbot's quarters) on the south of the garden with its long verandah (engawa) along the whole north face; the six main rooms of a hōjō in two rows of three (shitsuchū, the abbot's, the Buddha room in the middle of the rear row) [inferred: the standard hōjō plan]; the garden to be seen seated on the veranda [stated].
- Our stone groups' positions follow the common published plan from west to east: the group of five farthest east, two, three, two, then three at the west [inferred: arrangement taken from the standard reading of the garden, seen from the hōjō].
- Kyōyōchi pond, 12th c., with trees round [stated: Wikipedia].

## 5. The townscape

- **Population and form:** Kyoto after Ōnin lay half in ruins, with the two halves (Upper Kyoto, Kamigyō, round the shogun's quarters; Lower Kyoto, Shimogyō, the merchants'), ringed by earthen ditches and walls (the "Ōnin ditches") [stated: general; not sourced here]. About 100,000 people [inferred: common estimates; unsourced].
- **Machiya of the Muromachi type [inferred: the Rakuchū rakugai screens (16th c.), Wikipedia/JAANUS]:** most are one storey, a few with a low second storey (tsuishi nikai); gabled roofs of planks (itabuki) weighted with stones, not tile (tiled roofs spread later; JAANUS: "roofing generally shifted from stone-weighted shingles or thatch to tiles", partly for fire protection [stated]); the front a shop (mise) behind folding shutters (shitomi) that open up and down into a counter (tsuri-mise), curtains (noren) over the entry, the through-passage (doma) running to the back and a kitchen yard with a well; frontage of 3 to 3.5 ken (≈ 6 m) was the Edo standard [stated: JAANUS], ours 5 to 7 m. The plot is narrow and deep, the shop in front, the storehouse (kura) behind [stated: JAANUS].
- **Streets:** earth, drained by a gutter along each side; the houses' fronts continuous, with the eaves close; no wooden lattice fronts yet; fences of split bamboo and plank at the lanes. [inferred]
- **Trades:** weaving and dyeing, lacquer, metal, paper, carpentry, ceramics; porters and carters; itinerant vendors [stated: dailylifehistory.pages.dev]. Sake brewers and moneylenders (dosō) kept the storehouses [inferred, general]. Tea sellers at the temples [inferred].
- **Tea houses:** the grass-hut tea room of 4.5 mats; a thatched roof, an earthen wall, a small crawling-in door (nijiriguchi, a later invention by Rikyū, so ours has a low sliding door), a stone wash-basin (tsukubai) in the garden path [inferred: Jukō's wabi style; the nijiriguchi left out as anachronistic].
- **Noh stage** [stated: JAANUS, nōbutai]: a main stage of three bays, 5.7 m square, on joists of cypress, no base stones; the bridge (hashigakari) running obliquely from the rear left to the mirror room; three small pines in white gravel along it; the pine painted on the back wall (kagami-ita, not described by JAANUS — [inferred: the standard]); the musicians' seat (atoza) at the rear; the chorus seat (jiutaiza) on the right; jars under the floor. Some scholars say it was not roofed until the 20th c. [stated: JAANUS]; the shrine stage with its own roof is an [inferred] compromise: ours carries a roof on the stage, a Muromachi shrine's kagura-den being roofed.
- **Temples and shrines:** the shrine's torii (unpainted/vermilion box beam), stone lanterns, a hall with a bark roof; temple gates; earthen walls with tile tops (tsuijibei) [inferred].
- **Fire:** dense wood; communities fought fires from wells and canals [stated: dailylifehistory].

## 6. Planting

Red pines (pruned), cedars (sugi) and cypress on the hills, maples, bamboo, moss; the pond's pines [stated: Wikipedia, Ginkaku-ji: wooded areas covered in various mosses; pines and stones moved in 1474]. In the town: persimmon, willows by the stream, small trees in the courts. [inferred]

## 7. People and life

Samurai with two swords (the long and short sword were common, the edict is of 1629 — in 1490 a retainer carries a tachi/katana and wears the eboshi on duty [inferred]); monks with shaven heads in black robes; townspeople in robes of hemp and cotton, with straw hats; porters and pedlars; women with the hair worn long and bound low or loose (the knots and buns of the Edo period are later [inferred]); Noh actors and a taiko player; children. Dress: hemp, ramie, silk for the elite [stated: dailylifehistory]. Sandals of straw and wooden clogs [stated: same].

## 8. Sound

Noh: flute (nōkan), the shoulder drum (kotsuzumi), hip drum (ōtsuzumi), a stick drum; the chant (utai); temple bells (bonshō) sounded at the hours; wood blocks; cicada and frogs in the summer. Scale: the yo scale, pentatonic [inferred; documented in our `music.js`].

## 9. Built (this pass)

Style `muromachi`: Kannon-den with pent roof, cusped windows, hōgyō roof and phoenix; Tōgu-dō with the Dōjinsai (tsukeshoin, chigaidana, mats, shelf, scroll) and the ni-no-ma; the Zen gate and the tsuiji walls; the Ryōan-ji hōjō with its veranda; the karesansui with 15 stones, rings and raked gravel; the machiya with board roofs weighted with stones, tsuri-mise and noren, open inside as shops (the doma, the shop floor of mats, a hearth, the goods of the trade); the tea hut (thatched, a roji with tsukubai and stepping stones, inside a 4½-mat room with the tokonoma-less hanging, kettle and brazier); the shrine with torii and the Noh stage with its bridge and three pines; stone bridges and a stone-lined stream; lanterns, wells, fences.
