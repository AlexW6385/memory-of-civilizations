## 1. Moment and choice of landmark

- **Moment shown: about 1490**, the year Ashikaga Yoshimasa died and his Higashiyama villa became the Zen temple Jishō-ji [stated: Wikipedia, Ginkaku-ji]. It is the "Higashiyama culture": the dry landscape garden, the shoin room, the tea of Murata Jukō, wabi taste, Noh [stated: DESIGN §7 row; general]. The brief for this settlement said "c. 1450"; Ryōan-ji was founded in 1450 [stated: Wikipedia, Ryōan-ji] and rebuilt about 1488–1499 after the Ōnin War (1467–77) [stated: Wikipedia, Ryōan-ji], so 1490 holds both and Ginkaku-ji's Tōgu-dō (1486), which 1450 would not.
- **Landmark A: Ginkaku-ji / Jishō-ji** (the Silver Pavilion, Kannon-den, and the Tōgu-dō with its Dōjinsai). Kannon-den begun 21 Feb 1482, two storeys, modelled on Yoshimitsu's Kinkaku; the silver foil was planned and never applied; building stopped in the Ōnin War era and the pavilion stands as Yoshimasa last saw it; the name "Silver Pavilion" is Edo-period [stated: Wikipedia, Ginkaku-ji]. Ours is therefore bare dark timber, as it is. Yoshimasa moved stones and pines from the Flowery Palace (Hana no Gosho) and Muromachi hall to the villa in 1474 [stated: same]. Garden: moss, a pond shaping the design, the northeast room planned as a library lit by light off the pond [stated: same].
- **Landmark B: Ryōan-ji, the hōjō and its rock garden.** Founded 1450 by Hosokawa Katsumoto on an old Fujiwara estate with a pond (Kyōyōchi, 12th c.); destroyed in the Ōnin War, rebuilt by his son Hosokawa Masamoto about 1488; the garden: a rectangle 25 × 10 m, 15 stones in five groups (5, 2, 3, 2, 3), white gravel, moss only round the stones, a clay wall stained brown and orange by age, viewed seated from the hōjō veranda so that not all fifteen show at once [stated: Wikipedia, Ryōan-ji]. Its date is disputed (second half of the 15th c. to the Edo period; the earliest descriptions of 1680–82 show nine stones; the present form comes from the rebuild after the fire of 1779) [stated: same]. We show it as in the standard attribution to the late 15th c. [inferred, flagged: a possible but unproven state].
- Kinkaku-ji (Rokuon-ji) also stood (1399; three storeys in shinden, buke and Zen styles, the top two gilded, 12.5 m high, a pyramidal shingled roof with a bronze phoenix, over the Mirror Pond with ten islets) [stated: Wikipedia, Kinkaku-ji]. Not built in this pass; the Kannon-den is the landmark. [Left out for the density of the two precincts.]

## 3. The landmark: Kannon-den (Ginkaku)

- **Plan [inferred from photographs and the building's bays]:** lower floor 5 × 4 bays (≈ 10.5 × 8.4 m at 2.1 m a bay), upper floor 3 × 3 bays (≈ 6.3 m square); height to the ridge ornament about 13 m. Two storeys [stated: Wikipedia]. A pent roof (mokoshi) skirts the upper floor over the lower; the upper floor has the cusped windows (katōmado) of Zen halls and a pyramidal (hōgyō) roof of cypress bark (hiwadabuki) with a bronze phoenix [inferred: standard for the building].
- **Style:** the lower floor in the manner of the shoin houses, the upper in Zen style [inferred: the building's usual description; Kinkaku's pattern copied].
- Openings on the south-east face the pond; the lower room's sliding shutters (shitomi-do) open to a verandah [inferred].
- **Materials:** unfinished cypress boards and posts darkened by 500 years of weather would be anachronistic; at 1490 the timber is new, black-brown lacquered on the posts and rails, plain on the walls; roofs of cypress bark in warm brown [inferred]. Palette: `wood_dark`, `lacquer_black`, `roof_bark`, `plaster_white`.

### 3.1 Tōgu-dō (1486 [stated: Wikipedia])
- Built 1485–86 as Yoshimasa's private hall of Buddhist worship (jibutsudō), about 7 m square, hip-and-gable roof (irimoya) of cypress bark; four rooms: the largest with the altar, east of it the narrow four-mat ni-no-ma, which opens into the **Dōjinsai**, and the large empty san-no-ma beside it separated by sliding screens (fusuma) [stated: JAANUS, Tōgudō].
- **Dōjinsai**: a 4½-mat room; along the north wall a writing bay with sliding panels opening on the view — a tsukeshoin, perhaps the oldest example; to its left a half-bay alcove with staggered shelves (chigaidana); the remains of a hearth found in 1965 were not rebuilt [stated: JAANUS]. This is the model for the shoin room. The 4½-mat plan is the same size Jukō's wabi tea room took [stated: general; the link is the standard account].
- Ours: the Tōgu-dō is open from its verandah into the ni-no-ma and the Dōjinsai (the sliding screens drawn back); mats laid in the Muromachi way (all mats laid, a half-mat in the middle); the tokonoma is not in the Dōjinsai itself (it is not documented there), so none is built in it; the room has the tsukeshoin and the chigaidana [stated], a low hearth is left out [stated: none rebuilt].

## 4. The landmark: Ryōan-ji's hōjō and rock garden

- The 15 stones in five groups, 5-2-3-2-3, on a 25 × 10 m field of white gravel raked in lines along the long axis and in rings round the stones, each stone with a moss collar, the clay wall (oil-stained, brown-orange) low on three sides behind, with a roofed top (bark) [stated: Wikipedia]. The hōjō (abbot's quarters) on the south of the garden with its long verandah (engawa) along the whole north face; the six main rooms of a hōjō in two rows of three (shitsuchū, the abbot's, the Buddha room in the middle of the rear row) [inferred: the standard hōjō plan]; the garden to be seen seated on the veranda [stated].
- Our stone groups' positions follow the common published plan from west to east: the group of five farthest east, two, three, two, then three at the west [inferred: arrangement taken from the standard reading of the garden, seen from the hōjō].
- Kyōyōchi pond, 12th c., with trees round [stated: Wikipedia].

## 5. The townscape

- **Population and form:** Kyoto after Ōnin lay half in ruins, with the two halves (Upper Kyoto, Kamigyō, round the shogun's quarters; Lower Kyoto, Shimogyō, the merchants'), ringed by earthen ditches and walls (the "Ōnin ditches") [stated: general; not sourced here]. About 100,000 people [inferred: common estimates; unsourced].
- **Machiya of the Muromachi type [inferred: the Rakuchū rakugai screens (16th c.), Wikipedia/JAANUS]:** most are one storey, a few with a low second storey (tsuishi nikai); gabled roofs of planks (itabuki) weighted with stones, not tile (tiled roofs spread later; JAANUS: "roofing generally shifted from stone-weighted shingles or thatch to tiles", partly for fire protection [stated]); the front a shop (mise) behind folding shutters (shitomi) that open up and down into a counter (tsuri-mise), curtains (noren) over the entry, the through-passage (doma) running to the back and a kitchen yard with a well; frontage of 3 to 3.5 ken (≈ 6 m) was the Edo standard [stated: JAANUS], ours 5 to 7 m. The plot is narrow and deep, the shop in front, the storehouse (kura) behind [stated: JAANUS].
- **Streets:** earth, drained by a gutter along each side; the houses' fronts continuous, with the eaves close; no wooden lattice fronts yet; fences of split bamboo and plank at the lanes. [inferred]
- **Trades:** weaving and dyeing, lacquer, metal, paper, carpentry, ceramics; porters and carters; itinerant vendors [stated: dailylifehistory.pages.dev]. Sake brewers and moneylenders (dosō) kept the storehouses [inferred, general]. Tea sellers at the temples [inferred].
- **Tea houses:** the grass-hut tea room of 4.5 mats; a thatched roof, an earthen wall, a small crawling-in door (nijiriguchi, a later invention by Rikyū, so ours has a low sliding door), a stone wash-basin (tsukubai) in the garden path [inferred: Jukō's wabi style; the nijiriguchi left out as anachronistic].
- **Noh stage** [stated: JAANUS, nōbutai]: a main stage of three bays, 5.7 m square, on joists of cypress, no base stones; the bridge (hashigakari) running obliquely from the rear left to the mirror room; three small pines in white gravel along it; the pine painted on the back wall (kagami-ita, not described by JAANUS — [inferred: the standard]); the musicians' seat (atoza) at the rear; the chorus seat (jiutaiza) on the right; jars under the floor. Some scholars say it was not roofed until the 20th c. [stated: JAANUS]; the shrine stage with its own roof is an [inferred] compromise: ours carries a roof on the stage, a Muromachi shrine's kagura-den being roofed.
- **Temples and shrines:** the shrine's torii (unpainted/vermilion box beam), stone lanterns, a hall with a bark roof; temple gates; earthen walls with tile tops (tsuijibei) [inferred].
- **Fire:** dense wood; communities fought fires from wells and canals [stated: dailylifehistory].

## 6. Planting

Red pines (pruned), cedars (sugi) and cypress on the hills, maples, bamboo, moss; the pond's pines [stated: Wikipedia, Ginkaku-ji: wooded areas covered in various mosses; pines and stones moved in 1474]. In the town: persimmon, willows by the stream, small trees in the courts. [inferred]

## 7. People and life

Samurai with two swords (the long and short sword were common, the edict is of 1629 — in 1490 a retainer carries a tachi/katana and wears the eboshi on duty [inferred]); monks with shaven heads in black robes; townspeople in robes of hemp and cotton, with straw hats; porters and pedlars; women with the hair worn long and bound low or loose (the knots and buns of the Edo period are later [inferred]); Noh actors and a taiko player; children. Dress: hemp, ramie, silk for the elite [stated: dailylifehistory]. Sandals of straw and wooden clogs [stated: same].

## 8. Sound

Noh: flute (nōkan), the shoulder drum (kotsuzumi), hip drum (ōtsuzumi), a stick drum; the chant (utai); temple bells (bonshō) sounded at the hours; wood blocks; cicada and frogs in the summer. Scale: the yo scale, pentatonic [inferred; documented in our `music.js`].

## 10. Deepening pass (the Suzhou standard)

Facts used and how each is held. **[stated]** has a source; **[inferred]** is our reading, kept honest where the sources are silent.

- **The city:** after the Ōnin War (1467–77) Kyoto lay split in two, Kamigyō and Shimogyō; afterwards "the only road between them" was Muromachi Street [stated: Wikipedia, Muromachi Street]. The Rakuchū-rakugai screens, whose early versions put the official buildings first and only later turn to festivals, street activity and the daily work of townspeople [stated: JAANUS, rakuchū rakugai-zu], are our reference for the street scene; they are of the 16th century, so the machiya here are read back from them [inferred].
- **Gates at the street ends (kido):** the neighbourhood associations of the late Muromachi town closed their streets with gates and ditches against raiders [inferred: general knowledge of the machishū; not sourced here]. Ours stand near the two ends of each long street, with the leaves open. The kido, the notice board (kōsatsu) and the Jizō and Inari shrines are the Edo style's own pieces reused [inferred: the form is Edo's; the age of the custom is the open point].
- **Houses:** about a third carry the low second storey (tsushi nikai) over a pent hood (hisashi) with two slit windows in plaster; most are board-walled under plank roofs weighted with stones, a fifth have bark, a tenth straw (the tiled roof came later [stated: JAANUS, quoted in §5]). Closed fronts are of three kinds: the shop's shutters shut, a latticed window over a plastered wainscot, a plank door with a paper window [inferred]. Moss patches and newer mending boards on the roofs, a band of splashed earth at the foot of the walls [inferred: weathering of a damp basin].
- **Trades (thirteen):** cloth, sake, pottery, rice, tea, lacquer (§5), and now smith, dyer, paper-maker, sandal-maker (straw waraji), eating-house, moneylender (dosō, with strong chests and strings of coins) and timber-dealer [inferred from §5's list of trades in dailylifehistory: weaving and dyeing, lacquer, metal, paper, carpentry, ceramics, porters; sake brewers and moneylenders (dosō) kept the storehouses]. Nearly half the houses (391 of 703) are open inside: the shop floor of mats, the earth passage with its kitchen range (kamado), water jar and hung straw hat, the room behind with the hearth, a chest, the folded bedding and the god-shelf (kamidana) [inferred: the standard plan of a town house].
- **Street furniture and life:** a stone-edged gutter 1.2 m in from each house line under the eaves' drip; hanging paper lanterns, cloth banners, sake barrels, pots, sacks, straw sandals, dye cloths and trays of paper out on the street; benches with old men, tea tables with customers, water jars, firewood, hens, dogs, cats, children [inferred]. Washing women at stone steps down to the stream, punts and an angler [inferred: the Kamo's canals, §2]. Pedlars' stalls and handcarts at rest [inferred].
- **Yards:** each block's yard holds a storehouse of white walls (the Edo kura reused: the dosō's storehouses are the one fireproof building of the town [stated: JAANUS, §5; the tiled roof of the reused piece is [inferred]]), vegetable beds in ridged rows, a woodshed, a rack for drying cloth or straw, the wash on its line, a persimmon or maple on the west side [inferred].
- **Kannon-den, inside:** the lower floor (the Shinkū-den) has mats over the room, the Buddha on a dais against the back wall, an offering table with the incense burner, a library corner in the north-east (the planned library, whose light came off the pond [stated: Wikipedia, Ginkaku-ji]), a stair along the east wall; the upper floor (the Chōon-kaku) is a boarded room under the hipped roof with the standing Kannon on a dais, cusped windows seen from inside, lamps [inferred: the standard account of the two floors' names and uses; the page cited does not describe the interiors]. The stair's form is invented for walking: the real stair is steeper [inferred]. The sand mound and cone (Ginshadan, Kōgetsudai) are later and are not built [stated: Wikipedia names them without a date; understood as Edo].
- **Temples:** a bell tower (shōrō) in each, stones set in islets in the pond at Ginkaku-ji (stones and pines moved there in 1474 [stated: Wikipedia, Ginkaku-ji]); the hōjō's alcove (tokonoma) with its scroll and flowers, the brazier room, the kitchen (kuri) beside the gate of Ryōan-ji [inferred].
- **People:** the walkers now include the Ōhara woman with a bundle of firewood on her head and the pilgrim with staff and straw hat [inferred: both are known Kyoto figures; the date of the former is general knowledge, unsourced]. Visitors stand on the south bank of the pond and in the court, a monk sweeps the gravel, worshippers stand before the shrine hall, guests sit on the hōjō's veranda.

The painted board on a post that every open house carried is removed. Each house is now known by its noren, cloth and mark by trade (white and indigo for sake, red for tea and eating-houses, black for the dosō, and so on) [inferred: the colours]; sake shops hang a sugidama from the eave [stated: sugidama as a sign; its use in 1490 is inferred]; potters a string of pots, sandal-makers a bar of waraji, smiths their tools, cloth-sellers a dyed pennant, lacquerers a red bowl, all from the eave [inferred: goods shown instead of a board].

Added: sets of brackets of two tiers on the head beam at every post and every mid-bay on all four faces, with cross arms, a purlin (keta) running round on the second arms, plaster between the sets, a row of rafter ends beyond the purlin; the roof stands on them (raised 0.85 m) [inferred: the number of tiers and the spacing; the style is stated]. The building is bare timber, as in §1.
- **Rock garden.** The fifteen stones are no longer bodies of revolution: each is a faceted oval body of five rings with its own radii, a sloping top, leaning slightly outward, its foot in the ground and the moss collar round it; the tallest of a group stands narrow and upright, the others broad and low [inferred: the stones' shapes are not documented; the grouping 5-2-3-2-3 stands as in §4].
- **Hōjō screens.** The partitions between the rooms carry ink landscapes (suibokuga) on both faces, panel by panel: grey-wash hills in one run, the sky left as bare paper, a black foot of bank, pines with flat clusters on some panels [inferred: Zen abbots' quarters were painted in ink in this age; no painting survives for Ryōan-ji's 1490 hall].
- **Cloth shops** now have the collision of what stands (the bolts for two metres, the board beyond), not a block across the room.

## 12. To the Suzhou reference: the walk, the night, the sound, the animals, wear and writing

**2. Night.**
- A lantern hung from an eave is a chōchin: the earliest record of a chōchin is of 1085 [stated: Wikipedia, Traditional lighting equipment of Japan]; the shops' hanging lanterns are lit at night [inferred]. The andon (paper-shaded oil lamp) "became popular in the Edo period" and "rapeseed oil was popular", candles dearer [stated: same]; so no andon is shown, and a room's lamp is a small one, its window-light weak (strength 0.5 to 0.9) and lit in a minority of houses (0.4) [inferred: the lamp was oil in a dish].
- Stone lanterns: in Japan tōrō were first used in temples, "lit lanterns were then considered an offering to Buddha" [stated: Wikipedia, Stone lantern]; the shrine's, Jishō-ji's and Ryōan-ji's stone lanterns are lit [inferred for c. 1490]. Their use as ornaments of a tea garden was popularised by tea masters in the Azuchi–Momoyama period (1568–1600) [stated: same], so the tea hut's lantern is not lit [inferred].
- Made with the engine's `halo` material (added light, night only, no light object, day view unchanged): a glow, a pool on the street or gravel and a wash on the wall behind each hanging lantern; a pool across the doorstep of an open shop (70 % of them); a streak on the pond under a stone lantern at its bank; a sheet of lamp-light on the paper (shoji) and the bamboo lattice (koshi) of the windows of houses and halls (`src/settlements/muromachi-kyoto/nightlight.js`) [built].

**3. **5. Wear and writing.**
- Wear: every tread of the stream's steps and of the Tōgu-dō is worn into a shallow dish (1.5 cm) by feet; moss on the Tōgu-dō's lower tread and along the stone gutters' outer course [inferred: use and damp; the clay wall's staining of §4 is stated].
- Writing. The names on the boards: 慈照寺 over Jishō-ji's gate (the temple took the name of Yoshimasa's posthumous name after his death in 1490 [stated: Wikipedia, Ginkaku-ji]; the board at that date [inferred]); 龍安寺 over Ryōan-ji's gate (the name Ryōan-ji [stated: Wikipedia, Ryōan-ji]); 東求堂 over the Tōgu-dō's front (the hall's name [stated: JAANUS]; the board [inferred]). Black lacquer, gold characters, read right to left as horizontal boards were [inferred]. On the shop curtains (noren; JAANUS says kanban signboards are Edo [stated, §10]), one character on the middle panel by trade: 酒 sake, 茶 tea, 米 rice, 布 cloth, 當 pawnbroker, 瓷 pottery, 漆 lacquer; the other trades keep a plain mark [inferred: the trades' curtains carried a mark; which, inferred].
- Glyphs: six new characters (慈 13 strokes, 照 13, 龍 16, 安 6, 東 8, 求 7) in `src/styles/muromachi/script.js`, each in the standard (traditional) stroke count, laid over a typeface stroke by stroke with `tools/script-sheet-kyoto.mjs` and `tools/script-sheet.py` until every stroke sits on its stroke (the sheet's typeface is a measure only, nothing of it kept); 龍 is the 16 strokes of Suzhou's verified 瓏, the heart of 慈 that of Suzhou's verified 恩; 酒 茶 米 布 當 瓷 漆 are Suzhou's own entries, read unchanged [built]. Other characters (Kannon-den's 心空殿 and 潮音閣 boards, the hōjō's) are not drawn: no glyph is written that is not verified.
