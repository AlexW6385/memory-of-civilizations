# Mount Wutai (Foguang Monastery), 857 — dossier

Settlement id: `tang-wutai` · Style: `chinese` (the hall), `song` (the pillar and the pagoda), `vernacular` (houses) · Region: China (Shanxi, Mount Wutai) · Code: `src/settlements/tang-wutai/`
Replaces the legacy district of the previous design. The Great East Hall itself, with its terraces and courtyard, is the full building from that design (`src/buildings/foguang-east-hall`, dossier `docs/buildings/foguang-east-hall.md`).
Status of this dossier: L1–L2 (the monastery's lower courtyard, middle terrace and the Great East Hall on the upper terrace against Foguang Hill; the founding master's small brick pagoda south of the hall; the valley village of earth- and brick-walled houses; pines on the hills, fields below; ground, planting, weather, music). L3 built (§10): the dhāraṇī pillar of 857 and the Zushi pagoda in detail, the monks and pilgrims. L3 built (second pass): the hall's ping'an ceilings, its oil lamps, the monks' quarters, pilgrims on the stairs (§10). L3 built (third pass): the inner ring's bracket-eye panels and the Tang mural, the portraits of Ning Gongyu and Yuancheng, damp weathering (§10). L3 recorded for later: the Song murals of the other inner panels, the ink inscriptions on the beams, the Samantabhadra Hall (date unknown), the second (877) pillar, other monasteries of Wutai. The Wenshu (Manjusri) Hall on the courtyard's north side is Jin (1137, or 1147 by another account) and is left out of 857 [stated: Wikipedia (wikiwand)].

## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Great East Hall of Foguang Monastery, completed in 857, standing on a high terrace on the slope of Foguang Hill, facing west [stated: `docs/buildings/foguang-east-hall.md` and its sources].
- **The Zushi pagoda:** a small brick pagoda of the Northern Wei or Northern Qi, south of the East Hall, in two storeys, hexagonal [stated: Wikipedia "Foguang Temple"]. Built hexagonal (§10).
- **The setting:** the monastery lies in the hills of Mount Wutai, Shanxi [stated: Wikipedia "Foguang Temple"].
- **Moment shown:** 857.

## 5. The town

About 120 houses in the valley. [inferred]

**Houses that can be entered (L3, built).** The three houses nearest the arrival are open (DESIGN §4.3): the kang (the heated brick bed) along the back wall with its mat and low table, the stove feeding it with its pot, glazed water jars, a chest; a step up to the door, the leaf open inside, a ceiling of boards on beams [inferred: the period's village interiors].

**House detail (vernacular `house_china`).** Windows in red timber frames with paper lattice and brick sills; plank doors in frames under a lintel with a step; on earth-walled houses a grey-brick dado, corner piers and a band under the eaves; roofs with real thickness, boards under the eaves and verges, the ridge with raised ends; on most houses the kang's brick flue rising at one gable end [inferred].

## 10. Detail (L3)

- **The dhāraṇī pillar of 857.** 3.24 m, hexagonal, built in 857 recording the hall's construction [stated: Wikipedia (wikiwand) "Foguang Temple"]. Ours, before the hall on the upper terrace (where the hall plan had a simple stand-in): an octagonal base of three tiers with niches round its waist, an up-turned lotus seat of sixteen petals, the hexagonal shaft with two bands, the canopy with a swagged drapery and tassels at its corners, a short drum, a lotus and the pearl [inferred: the usual Tang form of dhāraṇī pillars, see the Wikipedia article "Dharani pillar"]. The record of 857 and the dhāraṇī are cut as three columns of small incised marks on each face of the shaft (not legible: the text is not in our sources), and two square courses of stone lie under the base.
- **The Zushi pagoda.** South of the Great East Hall; white, hexagonal, 6 m; the first storey holds a hexagonal chamber, the second is purely decorative; lotus petals; the steeple carries a flower-shaped vase [stated: Wikipedia (wikiwand)]. Ours: on the upper terrace south of the hall (the earlier octagonal stand-in on the slope to the north is removed): a brick plinth, the white first storey with its chamber door on the west under a flame-shaped arch of brick [inferred: the arch, and the door's face], an eave of corbelled courses stepping out and back, a band of lotus petals as the seat of the second storey, its blind doors, a small corbelled eave, a lotus bud and the vase. Added: six corner pilasters of brick with capitals and bases, blind straight-lattice windows in the faces beside the door's [inferred: after brick pagodas of the period; Wikipedia gives only the chamber, the lotus petals and the vase], two stone steps to the door, a hexagonal apron of bluestone edged with limestone, moss on the apron.
- **Monks.** Tang monks were called the "black-robed" (*ziyi*), the kasaya over the robe of a dark reddish brown [inferred from thepaper's account of kasaya colours and the term]. Ours: monks in black or grey robes with a brown kasaya sash and shaven heads at the pillar, at the pagoda, on the hall's platform, on the middle terrace and in the courtyard; a few lay pilgrims in blue, one with an offering basket.

- **The ceilings (ping'an).** "A lattice ceiling conceals much of the roof frame from view" [stated: Wikipedia (wikiwand)]. The ping'an is a ceiling that bears no load: its backing boards rest on the ceiling beams, squared rafters run under them over the seams, Liang Sicheng found its grid in only two buildings, smaller than any surviving pingqi grid, and unlike pingqi it has no pattern, being lacquered red; the Great East Hall is one of the two examples, its ping'an installed with the structure [stated: Architectura Sinica "píng'àn"]. The hall's beams are divided by the ping'an into the visible "crescent beams" (月梁) below and the rough frame (草架) above [stated: EDB, Nan Lian Garden notes]. Ours: in the outer aisle a flat ceiling at the crescent beams' tops; over the inner zone a higher one; each of red-lacquered boards with a grid of square strips at 0.3 m under them (the strips one way 5 mm deeper than the other) and a frame round it; the inner ceiling's edges slant up 1.25 m over 1.2 m on boards carried by rafters at the same pitch, from the inner ring's beams to the flat [inferred: the pitch, the heights, the slanting edge after the published sections of the hall].
- **Lamps.** Six oil lamps on bronze stands, a pair before each of the three Buddhas, each a lotus foot, a slender shaft and a dish with its flame, lit (warm, short reach) [inferred: no source describes the hall's lighting; the open doors are its daylight].
- **The monks' quarters.** In 845 the temple was burned and only the Zushi pagoda survived; in 857 it was rebuilt, the East Hall on the site of a three-storey pavilion, with Ning Gongyu's money under the monk Yuancheng [stated: Wikipedia (wikiwand)]. No account of the other buildings of 857 survives in our sources; a monastery of monks needs its quarters. Ours: two ranges of seven bays (3.9 m) by one span of 7.2 m along the north and south sides of the lower courtyard, facing in: a brick-faced platform 0.45 m high with three flights; red columns on stone bases, architraves, white plastered walls and bands up to the eaves; a plank door of two leaves with its threshold in every other bay and barred (zhiling) windows with paper behind in the others; a hipped roof of grey tiles on rafters with small chiwei [inferred: all, after the East Hall's forms and Tang depictions; their places kept clear of the Jin hall's site].
- **Pilgrims.** Lay pilgrims climbing both flights of stairs with a monk; monks before the quarters [inferred].

- **The bracket-eye panels and the Tang mural (third pass).** "Today a few bracket-eye panels (gongyan bi) on the inner columns' architraves still bear murals"; "the oldest is above the front inner architrave of the right secondary bay"; about 450 cm long and 66 cm high, in three groups — in the middle a Buddha (perhaps Amitābha) with seven attendants, at the sides groups centred on bodhisattvas (likely Guanyin and Mahāsthāmaprāpta), flying apsaras, monks and officials as donors at each end; stone green, the other pigments darkened to an iron blue; the panel of the left secondary bay (seven halos of ten Buddhas, an inscription of the Xuanhe era) and the seventy bodhisattvas of the left wall are Song; the walls and the platform's back were painted once and are now whitewashed [stated: thepaper 13478440]. Ours: plastered panels (paint) between the bracket sets on every bay of the inner ring, from the inner architrave to the first column-head fang; on the west face of the front inner line's north secondary bay ("right" from the hall's own side, facing west) the painting, 4.4 × 0.66 m, on an ochre ground between red borders: the seated Buddha in a green mandorla among six standing attendants and a small one before him, a seated bodhisattva on clouds with four attendants to each side, an apsara with trailing scarves over each side group, two kneeling donors (a monk in dark blue, an official in red) at each end [inferred: the figures as flat silhouettes; the Song panels left plain, as 857 had its own lost paintings there].
- **The founders' portraits (third pass).** "At the left end of the platform, to the right of the heavenly king, a small life-size figure seated cross-legged: the donor, 'hall patron Ning Gongyu'"; a woman of about forty, full-faced, hands in her sleeves, a large-collared robe under a ruyi-cloud cloak; the monk Yuancheng "under the window of the south end bay, seated cross-legged facing the platform", austere, high forehead and cheekbones; the platform is five bays wide, one and a half deep, 74 cm high [stated: thepaper 13478440]; Wikipedia counts them among the earliest realistic portrait sculptures in Chinese Buddhist art [stated: Wikipedia "Foguang Temple"]. Ours: both in painted clay (red-brown robes, Ning Gongyu with a green collar and her hair in a bun, Yuancheng shaven), seated cross-legged with hands joined at the waist; she on the platform's south end between the groom and the king, facing west; he on a low red-lacquered seat under the south end bay's window, turned toward the platform [inferred: the seat; the cloak simplified to a collar].
- **Weathering.** The climate's dampness set to 0.25 (the mountain's summer rains): faint streaks and some moss on the shaded foot of walls [inferred].

## 11. B1 pass: the whole settlement

Radius cut from 360 to 250 m (small class): the monastery (x −58…94) and the village (x −232…−62, lanes to z ±88, yards to ±106) sit inside; the hill to the east falls away at the rim as before. Ground: the valley floor falls 0.025 to the west; the valley's flanks rise in seven terraces (fields) [inferred: terraced millet and oat fields are the Wutai uplands' farming; no source for their layout].

- **Village layout.** A main lane runs west from the monastery gate; cross lanes every 40 m and east–west lanes every 44 m; walled yards in rows facing the lanes, the house at the back, a gate on the lane, a side wing in the wider yards, a green before the gate with a well and a stone roller mill [inferred].
- **Houses of the region.** Three forms: the flat earth-roofed house (parapet, tiled coping, stone drain spouts, the kang chimney, beam ends under the edge), the single-pitch house (yipo shui, roof falling to the back), the brick-arched dwelling (gu yao, arches with fanlights, a door in the middle arch); walls of rubble stone, rammed earth or grey brick with a stone footing; big square papered lattice windows; corn and chillies hung to dry; a water vat by the door; the three yards nearest the gate are open with a furnished interior (kang, stove, jars, chest) [inferred: from the general character of north Shanxi vernacular building; the search for Wutai-specific sources returned no readable text, so every dimension is ours; arched and flat-roofed brick/earth houses are a documented Shanxi type, but 857 examples do not survive, so the forms are later vernacular used as a stand-in].
- **Planting.** Chinese pine and arborvitae on the hill and about the walls; poplars and jujubes among the yards [inferred, as §6].
- **East Hall joinery.** Plank doors in jambs and head, each leaf of vertical planks with three battens, rows of iron studs and ring pulls, swung open against the inner face; the end bays' straight-lattice (zhiling) windows with square bars set on the diagonal, as the Architectura Sinica page names the type [stated: five central front bays have doors, the outer bays straight-lattice windows, Architectura Sinica place/000169]; the stud rows, battens and diagonal bars are [inferred].
- **Painted beams.** White and green lines along the arrises of the crescent beams and banded ends, on a red ground [inferred: Tang red-and-white colour scheme; no source seen for the beams' detail].
- **Ink inscription.** An ink inscription on the underside of a beam of the hall names the female disciple Ning Gongyu [stated: thepaper 29966700, "梁底题记 … 女弟子宁公遇"]; the stone pillar before the hall carries her name and "大中十一年十月" (857) [stated: same]. Ours: columns of brush strokes on one crescent beam's underside, placed on the front aisle's north-of-centre beam [inferred: which beam; the strokes are not legible text].
- **The statues.** The count is not settled in our sources: Wikipedia gives "thirty-six sculptures" in one place and "35 Tang-dynasty clay sculptures on the altar platform" in another [stated: Wikipedia "Foguang Temple"]; a 2022 paper on Nanchan Temple counts 34 on the main platform [stated: 《南禅寺大殿重建背景…》, jgcm.ac.cn]. Ours has 35 (see third pass). Positions, sizes and looks after Liang Sicheng's survey, see the third pass below.
- **The Hall of Manjusri.** Its standing building is of the Jin (1137 or 1147), §header; the assignment places it in this settlement, so a hall of the bodhisattva of the mountain stands on the north of the middle terrace: five bays by one span, no inner columns (the reduced-column plan the Jin hall is known for) [inferred], a platform, door and barred windows, ping'an ceiling, Manjusri on his lion with attendants and guardians, a hipped tile roof [inferred: the 857 monastery's lost buildings are unknown; this is a stand-in]. Its figures follow the East Hall's measures: the attendant bodhisattvas 3.7 m, the guardians 4.1 m, the kneeling ones 1.95 m, the rider 3.4 m on a 1.3 m lion; the columns are 4.8 m so that the ceiling clears the tallest figure. Furnished: a dado of red earth paint round the inside walls, an offering table with a red cloth and two oil lamps before the altar, three kneeling mats, a path of worn bluestone slabs from the door, two long banners at the sides [inferred: usual furnishing of a hall of worship].
- **Bell pavilion and censers.** A square open pavilion on the middle terrace's south side with a bronze bell on its beam; bronze incense burners before the Great East Hall and the Manjusri hall; side flights at the ends of the terrace fronts [inferred: no 857 source; a monastery of the Tang kept a bell, and the engine's schedule for Wutai rings the monastery bell].

## 12. Living systems and sound

- **Walkers.** Monks (dark robe, brown kasaya, shaven head), pilgrims, villagers (men with topknot and brimmed hat, women with the hair in a bun, farmers in straw hats, porters with poles), 22 by day and 3 at night, more monks on the monastery's axis and terraces, mostly villagers on the lanes [inferred].
- **Animals.** Swallows over the village, courtyard and terraces; crows over the pines; tree sparrows on the paving and lanes [inferred: the climate's soundscape already lists warbler and crow].
- **Smoke.** Incense from the two burners all day; the monks' kitchen; kitchen smoke from the kang chimneys of a share of the houses at three meals [inferred].
- **Sound.** The hall's chant and wooden fish inside; outside the pipa and flute, now with a low breath of wind and a bowl-bell now and then; the dawn and dusk monastery bell comes from the engine's soundscape for this settlement (bells: monastery).

## Third pass: the hall's statues, the village, the weathering

Sources added: Liang Sicheng and Lin Huiyin's 1944 survey [thepaper 10143234 and 13478440]; Wikipedia "Foguang Temple"; the Nanchan Temple paper (jgcm.ac.cn); the UNESCO Pingyao guidelines; the artron article on Shanxi dwellings.

- **The statues after Liang's survey.** The altar platform is of clay figures [stated]. Centre bay: the Śākyamuni "subduing Māra", seated on a rectangular sumeru seat, the right shoulder bare, the right hand touching the earth, the left holding a bowl, hair in snail curls; left bay: Maitreya, seated with both legs pendant, a lotus under each foot ("typical of Tang images"); right bay: Amitābha, hands in a consoling gesture, on a hexagonal sumeru seat, the robe falling over it, hair straight in the Gandhāran manner; in the end bays Samantabhadra on the elephant (left, a "獠蛮" leading it) and Guanyin on the lion (right, a groom leading it; the small Amitābha in her crown marks her; later authors call her Mañjuśrī); at the front corners of the end bays two armoured heavenly kings with swords; each main figure has "five or six" attendants — Kāśyapa and Ānanda and two bodhisattvas for Śākyamuni, and in front two offering bodhisattvas kneeling on tall lotuses, holding fruit, one leg squatting and one kneeling, a form Liang knew only from Dunhuang and the Huayan Temple of Datong [stated: Liang, thepaper 10143234]. Heights with their seats: the three Buddhas about 5.3 m, Guanyin and Samantabhadra with their mounts about 4.8 m, the standing attendant bodhisattvas about 3.7 m, the kneeling offering bodhisattvas about 1.95 m, the two kings about 4.1 m [stated: same]. **Correction:** the second pass built the attendants at 2.5–2.8 m and the kings at 3.2 m; they are now 3.7 and 4.1 m, the kneeling ones 1.95. The faces: full cheeks, arched brows, firm lips, "clearly Tang"; the standing bodhisattvas lean a little forward with a bent waist and a slightly swelling belly (Liang links this to Dunhuang); Guanyin's dress has a ruyi-shaped breast ornament, spiral curls on the breasts and flame-shaped sleeves; the belts' knots and folds of Maitreya and Amitābha follow a fixed Tang pattern [stated: same]. The figures "have been heavily repainted in recent years: bright blue, green, red and white show, the craftsmanship is crude" [stated: same]; hence our bright blue, green, red and white paint, and no claim that it is the colour of 857.
- **Ours.** One figure family built once per type and varied by seed (src/styles/chinese/statues.js): the standing bodhisattva with the lean, a bare chest with a necklace, a ruyi breast ornament, a long skirt in folds with a gilt hem, a belt with a knot and hanging sash ends, a scarf (blue, white or green) over both shoulders and across the thighs, armlets, hands with four fingers and a thumb (one raised with the palm out, one down holding a vase or a bud), a high chignon with side locks, a gilt crown, painted features (black eyes and brows, red lips, the urna), a halo of rings; the monk (Kāśyapa older, with a heavy brow; Ānanda young) in a kasaya over the left shoulder with gilt patch lines, the hands joined; the king in boots and greaves, plates, a cuirass with two gilt discs, lion-head shoulder guards, a plumed helm, his mouth open, both hands on the sword, on a rock; the kneeling bodhisattva holding a tray of fruit; the groom, bearded, in a short coat and turban, holding the rein. In the East Hall the hanging silk banners (fan) hang only from the inner ring's rear beams, one in each bay, never between the door and a Buddha (the front ring's banners had hidden the three Buddhas from the doorway); the central and southern Buddhas' robes are ochre, since the red-brown robe vanished in the half-light [inferred: colours; Liang records none]. The attendants keep Liang's heights and stand beside their Buddha at about 2 m (the groups are 5.04 m apart, the kneeling pair before the seat), so the Buddha is seen between them from the door. The Buddhas' faces now have painted eyes, brows and lips, their curls blue [inferred: the details of the garments and the poses beyond Liang's list; the flesh colours (pale, gilt on the Buddhas)].
- **Not built.** About 290 Ming–Qing "five hundred arhats" along the side and rear walls, which Liang calls crude; a 752 jade Śākyamuni of 1 m; the Tang murals found behind the platform's base in 1964 [stated: Liang; left out: later than 857 or hidden].
- **The village: the single-pitch house corrected.** "All the roofs are inclined toward the courtyard" [stated: artron]; wing houses of Pingyao have "a single-slope or double-slope tiled roof", the main house a flat roof, and kangs heat the bedrooms [stated: UNESCO, §3.1, §3.2.3]. Our single-pitch houses had the roof falling to the back, away from the yard; **corrected**: the high wall is the back one and the roof falls toward the yard. The brick-arched dwellings with flat roofs are the documented "brick arching under flat roofing" [stated: artron].
- **Weathering** (earth walls eroded at the foot, the plaster fallen to show adobe, straw showing, smoke-black over doors and windows, patched roofs: replaced tiles, mud, straw; repair patches and a stone roller on earth roofs) [inferred: the weathering is ours, from what the climate and the walls' materials do; the sources found give the protective details (thin tiles on the wall top) and the repair categories (roof cleaning, desalination of walls, "cleaning and desalination" and vapour barriers under walls in the Pingyao guidelines point to salt and damp at the wall's foot), not a Tang village's condition; a dry mountain climate with summer rains per §7].
- **The plots and the green.** Every plot the rows left empty is filled: the village's small temple (a hall of three bays, a porch, a stone burner, a stele, two pines), threshing floors (a round floor of beaten earth with the stone roller, heaps of straw and grain, a fork and a wattle windbreak), vegetable plots (beds of cabbages, onions, bean tents, pumpkins, a lean-to), and some more yards; the green becomes a village open space of beaten earth with a great old pagoda tree (huai) and a stone seat round it, the well on its flagged apron, the roller mill, an earth-god's shrine, a pedlar's mat, a cart and the beasts; worn by use: cart ruts in two lines, trodden straw, stones, weeds at the edges and the tree's fallen leaves, the same ruts and litter along every lane; red peach-wood charms (taofu) and a strip of red cloth at the gate of each yard; the three open houses furnished as Shanxi farmhouses are (the kang with its table, the stove, a low table and stools on a reed mat, the kitchen god's paper over the stove, a shelf of bowls, strings of chillies and corn, a water vat, an oil lamp) [inferred: the gate charms (a Tang custom of the central plains, here assumed for Wutai) and the furnishing after the UNESCO Pingyao guidelines, no source for this village] [inferred: the village temple, the earth-god shrine, the tree and the plots' uses follow the general character of North China's villages; no source about Wutai's village in 857 was found].

## 13. Deepening pass (towards the Suzhou standard)

All of this section is [inferred]: no source describes 857 Wutai's village trades or its hillside; forms follow the village's own vernacular (§5, §11) and Tang monastery custom.

- **Trades on the main lane (shops.js).** The four plots nearest the gate on the main lane, after the three first open houses, take a pilgrims' inn (a hall with counter, wine jars, four tea tables and a stove, a partition, and a long communal kang with a row of quilts behind; hitching rail with donkey and ox, a red-lantern eave, a wine banner), a smithy (open front, brick hearth with hood and a stack through the roof, bellows, anvil on a log, quench trough, tools on the wall, a smith and apprentice, a donkey being shod), a shop of incense, candles and lamp oil for pilgrims (shopfront with a counter, shelves of incense bundles, red candles, oil jars, a hanging scale, shutter boards stacked aside), and a hostel of four cells under one roof with a veranda (each cell a kang, quilt, low table with lamp, chest, staff). Pilgrim traffic is attested by the monastery's cult; the trades are our reading of what the lane would serve.
- **More houses to enter.** Up to thirteen flat-roofed houses nearest the lane are open (kang, stove, jars; as the first three).
- **Trees.** Jujube, poplar and willow in the yards' front corners (a cheap tree of our own; the old huai on the green is unchanged).
- **The fields.** Rows of millet along the flat of each terrace, found from the ground (the stretches where it does not slope), some bands left in stubble with stooks; scarecrows, cairns of cleared stones, mud-brick field huts, farmers bent at work, a cart and an ox (fields.js).
- **The gate and the levels (dress.js).** Stone drums flanking the way into the monastery, a dismount stele, hitching posts, lanterns; stone lamp pillars (shi deng) and benches on all three levels; fire vats at the halls; the monks' well, washing and rack in the courtyard.
- **The hill.** A path of bluestone slabs from the upper terrace's south-east corner up Foguang Hill past cairns and a wayside shrine to a hermit's stone cell under old pines, a hermit, a pilgrim and a farmer on the path.
- **Life.** Two more walker types (children, elders), a market pitch of walkers before the shops, 52 walkers by day; sparrows at the shop doors and along the lanes; swallows over the fields and crows over the hill path; the inn's kitchen, the smithy's forge and the shops' hearths smoke all day.
