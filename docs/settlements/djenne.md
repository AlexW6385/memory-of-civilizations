## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Great Mosque of Djenné, the largest mudbrick building in the world [stated: Wikipedia via wikiwand]. The first mosque on the site was built in the 13th century; the present building dates from 1906–07 (or 1909), built under Ismaila Traoré, head of the masons' guild [stated: gohistoric; New World Encyclopedia].
- **Moment shown:** the town under the Mali Empire, 14th c. — but the mosque's form is that of 1907, the best-documented, built in the town's old manner. The symmetric three towers of the qibla wall were "almost certainly novel" in 1907; Caillié in 1828 saw "two massive but not high towers" [stated: Wikipedia (IPFS)]. We show the 1907 form and record the anachronism here. [inferred]

## 11. Doors, the roof stair and market day (2026-11-06)

- **Why the hall could not be entered:** its walls were closed boxes — no door in the north, south or qibla walls, and the hall's west wall on the court solid. The two "north entrance towers" stood on the south wall (+z is south). Fixed as follows.
- **Main entrance:** "The main entrance is on the northern side of the building" [stated: Wikiwand]. Ours: a 2 m doorway with a palm-wood lintel in the hall's north wall, into its fourth aisle, between the two entrance towers, now on the north; the towers' inner faces buried in the wall, a cornice and five pinnacles on each, engaged ribs and toron on their outer faces [arrangement inferred from photographs of the north front].
- **Court to hall:** the court is "surrounded on three sides by galleries"; "the walls of the galleries facing the courtyard are punctuated by arched openings" [stated: Wikiwand]. Ours: six pointed-arched openings (1.8 m wide, springing at 2.5 m) in the hall's west wall, in line with the bays between the piers. The galleries are built (§10, the arcades on three sides of the court).
- **Piers:** the nine arcaded rows are now spaced evenly between the west wall and the qibla wall (aisles of 2.3 m clear), so that the aisle along the west wall is walkable [inferred].
- **Roof stair:** "The towers in the qibla wall do not contain stairs linking the prayer hall with the roof"; two square towers hold the roof stairs, one at the south-west corner of the prayer hall, the other near the main entrance on the north, "only accessible from the exterior" [stated: Wikiwand]. Ours: the south-west tower (6.4 m square) against the hall's south wall, entered by a door from the hall's first aisle; inside, seven steep flights of banco steps (≈ 19 cm risers) winding round a solid newel with landings at the corners, slit windows, a door at the top onto the hall roof; pinnacles at its corners and toron on its outer faces [internal arrangement inferred]. The north stair tower is not built.
- **Market day:** Djenné holds "one of West Africa's most colorful markets — held on Monday — with merchants coming from throughout the region"; the town provisioned Timbuktu "with much needed cereals and dried fish" [stated: Archaeology magazine]. René Caillié (1828) described the market: kola sellers sitting in two lines, each with a small basket, selling for "eight or ten cowries"; butchers, meat in small pieces on skewers dried with smoke; fresh and dried fish, earthen pots, calabashes, mats, salt (wholesale in shops, retail in the market); fabrics, honey, vegetable and animal butter, milk; firewood brought in by women from 12 to 15 miles round; cowries the money [stated: Caillié via afrolegends]. Caillié is five centuries after our moment; the goods are those of the inland delta's long-lived trade and we use them for the 14th c. [inferred]. Ours (`town.js` `market()`, the `market_mat` element): stalls under mats in four north–south rows with 5 m aisles; sellers seated on carved stools behind mats of dried fish, rock-salt bars (lashed in pairs) and broken pieces, pots, calabashes, millet and rice, shea butter, narrow-strip cotton, rolled mats and smoked meat on skewers; at the market's north end the kola sellers in two facing lines with their small baskets; buyers in the aisles (women in wraps and head-ties, some carrying a pot or basket on the head; men in robes and caps); stacks of firewood with women by them; donkeys with panniers and goats along the east edge. ## 12. House fronts, the landing and the court galleries (2026-11-06, third pass)

- **Why:** the town's houses were plain boxes; worse, their facade's outward normal was reversed, so the pilasters and pinnacles stood buried inside the houses and the lanes showed blank walls.
- **The Sudan facade:** "Most traditional courtyard houses had a decorated facade that specialists call the Sudan facade", with pillars and decorated entrances, signalling the owner's status [stated: Aramco World 1990]. The houses "have imposing façades with pilaster like buttresses", with "an elaborate arrangement of pinnacles forming the parapet above the entrance door"; toron project about 60 cm; ceramic pipes from the roofline [stated: Wikipedia, Djenné]. The facade's parts are named by Maas & Mommersteeg: the corner pilaster *sarafar har* ("male pilaster"), the mitre-shaped pinnacle *sarafar idye* ("child pilaster"), the panel over the entrance, the toron, the window *tuney*, and the facade bench *tintin* [stated: Bulletin KNOB, glossary]. Ours (`sahel_house`): corner pilasters rising as tall pinnacles; the two pilasters framing the entrance taller still; between them a proud panel over the door up past the parapet with a frieze of small blocks, a moulding and a row of five mitre pinnacles; a lower intermediate pilaster on a long front; benches either side of the door; a plank door with battens and an iron ring in a banco frame under a palm-wood lintel; toron in a row on each storey; two clay spouts. Number and size of pinnacles, the frieze [inferred from photographs].
- **Two house types:** "Some of the houses built before 1900 are in the Toucouleur-style and have a massive covered entrance porch", with "a single small window onto the street set above the entrance door"; "many of the more recent two-storey houses are in the Moroccan-style and have small ornate windows" and lack the porch [stated: Wikipedia, Djenné]. Caillié: the entrance "closed by a door made of wooden planks" [stated: ibid.]. Ours: about half the two-storey houses Tukulor (a porch of two banco piers under a roof of palm beams and banco, one small window with timber bars above), the rest Moroccan (two or three small windows in the upper storey, each in a moulded frame with a stepped pointed head and a sill, a grille of laths; a small canopy over the door); one-storey houses Tukulor. Both types are 19th-c. forms; the 14th-c. houses are not documented and we use them as the town's long-lived manner [inferred].
- **The landing:** the town lies on the floodplain of the Niger and the Bani; Malfante's informant: "There are many boats on it, by which they carry on trade"; Leo Africanus: merchants used "small narrow canoes" for the trade with Timbuktu during the flood [stated: Wikipedia, Djenné]. Ours (`town.js` `landing()`): on the south shore toward the Bani, long narrow plank canoes moored along the bank (one in five with a mat shelter), two poled out into the channel, more drawn up on the bank; nets folded over poles to dry (`net_rack`), baskets, sacks and jars; porters, boatmen and women with loads [arrangement and gear inferred].
- **The court galleries:** the court is surrounded on three sides by galleries whose walls on the court are punctuated by arched openings [stated: Wikiwand]; the galleries are about 4 m wide [stated: Aramco World 1990]. Ours: arcades of pointed arches (1.7 m wide, springing at 2.5 m) between low pilasters with small pinnacles, 4 m in from the north, west and south walls, roofed with palm beams under banco at 5.2 m, the beams showing in the ceiling [arch rhythm and height inferred].
- **Found, left alone:** the shared life layer (`src/objects/life.js`, kit `africa`) moors dhows with lateen sails in Djenné's channel; dhows belong to the Indian Ocean, not the Niger.


## 13. L3: a year's rain on the banco, and the weeks before the crépissage (2026-11-07)

- **The moment in the year.** The dry season under the harmattan (§8) is the end of the year's wear: the rains have been on the walls since the last replastering and the next is weeks away. "Mali's hot, dry climate, punctuated by torrential rain, is hard on mud and quickly causes leaks and fissures"; without the annual layer the mosque "would crumble" [stated: Roads & Kingdoms, 2018]; the replastering "shields the mosque from harsh weather" before the rainy season [stated: Arab News, 2025].
- **Erosion on every banco face** (`erode` in `src/styles/sahel/index.js`): between the pilasters, at the foot of the walls where the rain splashes back, patches where the plaster has washed off and the mud bricks show in their courses; now and then such a patch higher up; and runnels washed paler down the face from the parapet. On the mosque's walls (all of `bancoWall`: the hall, the court, the galleries, the market's walls) and on the four walls of every house, on the house fronts above the facade benches and clear of the pilasters, the panel and the door [inferred: the form of the wear, after photographs of Djenné's walls before the crépissage; sizes and frequency ours].
- **The clay soaking.** "Clay is left to soak in round pools on the riverbank for 20 days" before the crépissage; it arrives by donkey cart; "younger masons break it into chunks and mix in water"; rice husks are added at the end, working "like a glue"; young men carry it in wicker baskets from the pits to the mosque; the workers climb on wooden ladders and the protruding palm sticks [stated: Roads & Kingdoms]; women carry the water for the mix [stated: Arab News]; the banco mix of water, earth, rice bran, shea butter and baobab powder [stated: Arab News]. Ours (`town.js` `crepissage()`, element `mud_pit`): three round pits of wet mud with trodden lips on the south bank east of the landing, lumps of clay on their edges and a heap of rice husk by each; a young mason standing in each pit working it with a hoe, another squatting at the lumps; a donkey with panniers and its driver; women with water jars. At the mosque's north wall four long ladders already set up between the pilasters, baskets stacked at its foot, the chief mason and an elder looking the face over [inferred: their places]. The festival day itself — thousands at work, each quarter racing to finish its section [stated: Roads & Kingdoms] — is not shown: it would not fall on a market day.

## 19. Upstairs: the opened upper floors of the houses nearest the market (2026-11-18)

- **What was shut.** The enterable houses (§16) already had a banco stair in the court up to one furnished room over the vestibule; the windows of its facade were painted on a closed wall, there was no way to the roof terrace the type has, no second room, no gallery. Built now (`src/styles/sahel/homes.js`, `homeplan.js`; the facade's windows in `index.js`) for the 24 two-storey houses nearest the arrival (`upperHomes()` in `layout.js`), in the near copy only.
- **What the sources give.** "Most have two storeys and roof terraces"; the Sudanese-style house has a vestibule "located between the street and the courtyard or veranda"; "wood is used for floors, ceilings and roofs" [stated: Springer 2021, §15]; the Tukulor front has "a single small window onto the street set above the entrance door", the Moroccan front "small ornate windows" [stated: Wikipedia, Djenné, §12]; the household head's use of the vestibule (Qur'anic school, artisan's workshop, meeting place) [stated: ibid.]; the houses have a "male" and a "female" part [stated: Aramco World 1990, as §17].
- **The way up.** From the street through the door and vestibule into the court, up the court stair (banco steps along the court's west side, §16) to a doorway in the court wall of the front block, 2.05 m high, onto the upper floor. In a house 7.4 m or more wide a second stair, steep banco steps (41°, 3.6 m run, sixteen steps a little uneven, 1 m wide) along the court wall rises in the room to the roof terrace; the roof's opening over it is edged by low banco rails (0.8 m) on its open sides. Narrower houses have the room only. [Form of the roof stair inferred: the roof terrace is stated, its stair is not; the mosque's roof stair is banco steps, §11.]
- **The windows are open reveals.** The facade's window openings (Tukulor: the one small window over the door; Moroccan: the three ornate windows; Plain: the one or two small shuttered squares) are cut through the 0.45 m wall, their grilles, sills and shutters kept; the street can be seen through them from the room, and the room's light comes through them.
- **Rooms by use** [inferred from the households of the sources; the kinds are ours]. Six households in turn from the nearest house: family (sleeping room, a store), merchant (store of jars, sacks of grain and bales of cloth; sleeping room), craftsman (the weaver's workroom with its loom upstairs, as the artisans' vestibule workshop below; sleeping room), marabout (boards and a reading mat of the Qur'anic school upstairs; sleeping room), tenement (a wall with a doorway divides the floor into two cells, each a bed, a chest and a little brazier), inn (mats with bedding and travellers' bundles, a bench and a water jar). Beds are banco platforms under a mat and a blanket with a log for the head; chests of timber; a shelf of bowls on the street wall. In a house 9 m wide or more a partition (0.3 m) with a doorway divides the floor. Every bed, chest and bale is varied by a hash of its own position (no two alike).
- **The loggia.** In a house 8 m wide or more a roofed gallery over the court (1.35 m deep, west of the court's furniture, on two or three banco posts with timber heads, a balustrade of 0.85 m on its three open sides) is reached by a doorway from the room, with a window beside the doorway; it is the veranda of the stated typology, here on the court, not on the street [the street front keeps the stated small windows; a balcony onto the street is not in the sources and was not built].
- **The roof terrace.** A flat roof of palm beams under banco behind its parapet (now solid for the walker), a mat spread to dry with cloth on it, water jars along the parapet, a bundle of palm sticks (the next replastering's toron, §13) [inferred, as §15].
- **Walk routes** (`walks` of the mosque plan, `src/settlements/djenne/plan.js`): for each of the 24 houses a route from the street through door, vestibule and court up the stair into the room and on up the roof stair to the terrace; a second to the loggia in the houses that have one; 41 routes in all, walked by `walk-check --sites djenne`.
- **Still shut:** the houses beyond the 24 keep the one furnished room (§16); no stair to the roof from one-storey houses' upper floor (they have none); the back block's roof is not reached from the upper floor; no lamps upstairs.

### 20.2 Night
- What lit Djenne at night: small clay oil lamps (a dish with a cotton wick, shea butter or sesame oil) in niches and on counters, the three-stone hearth in the court and the kitchen, no street lighting; in the mosque lamps in the niches of the qibla wall and by the north door. [inferred; no source for lamps at this place and date]
- Ours (`nightlight.js`, the additive `halo` material, night only, day unchanged): the lamp and hearth of every enterable house (glow ball and floor pool) and the lamplight spilling over its step; the upper windows of solid houses glow in about a third of cases; a lamp on the counter of about half the shops with a pool in the market before it; the court house's hearth, vestibule and workshop lamps; five lamps of the mosque (three mihrabs, the minbar niche's line, either side of the north door). The market itself is dark at night (it is held by day).
