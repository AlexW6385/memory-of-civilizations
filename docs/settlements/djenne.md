## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Great Mosque of Djenné, the largest mudbrick building in the world [stated: Wikipedia via wikiwand]. The first mosque on the site was built in the 13th century; the present building dates from 1906–07 (or 1909), built under Ismaila Traoré, head of the masons' guild [stated: gohistoric; New World Encyclopedia].
- **Moment shown:** the town under the Mali Empire, 14th c. — but the mosque's form is that of 1907, the best-documented, built in the town's old manner. The symmetric three towers of the qibla wall were "almost certainly novel" in 1907; Caillié in 1828 saw "two massive but not high towers" [stated: Wikipedia (IPFS)]. We show the 1907 form and record the anachronism here. [inferred]

## 3. Orientation

The qibla wall faces east toward Mecca and the market. [stated]

## 4. The style

Sun-dried mudbrick rendered in banco [stated: Studio Matrx]; pilaster-like buttresses ending in pinnacles; toron — bundles of rodier palm projecting about 60 cm from the walls, decoration and permanent scaffolding for the yearly replastering [stated: Studio Matrx; ArchEyes]; ceramic drainage spouts [stated: Studio Matrx]; walls about a metre thick at the qibla [stated: illustrarch].

## 5. The houses

Two storeys, flat roofs with parapets, a facade of pilasters framing the door and rising as pinnacles, a canopy over the door, a small window above. [inferred from the Tukulor-style facades of Djenné]

## 6. Ground and water

The town's mound level; the channel round it with water 1.6 m down; the floodplain beyond, a little lower than the town. (`ground.js`)

## 10. Detail added (L2–L3)

- **Prayer hall:** the roof stands on nine interior walls running north–south, pierced by pointed arches, making "a forest of ninety massive rectangular pillars" [stated: Wikipedia via Wikiwand]. Ours: nine arcaded walls of eleven piers each (half-piers against the north and south walls), pointed arches of two arcs (radius 0.75 × span) springing at 4.6 m, the roof of palm beams over them. Arch shape and spring height [inferred].
- **Roof vents:** "Small vents in the roof are topped with removable inverted kiln-fired bowls" [stated: Wikiwand]. Ours: banco collars capped with terracotta bowls, 24 over the hall.
- **Spouts:** "Ceramic half-pipes also extend from the roofline and direct rain water from the roof away from the walls" [stated: Wikiwand]. Ours: terracotta half-pipes about a metre long, between the pilasters of the north and south walls.
- **Qibla towers:** three box-like towers, the central about 16 m, cone-shaped pinnacles topped with ostrich eggs [stated: Wikiwand]. Ours adds engaged ribs on each tower's three outer faces, battered with the face and rising through the cornice as small pinnacles, as in photographs of the qibla front [inferred from photographs; number of ribs ours].

## 11. Doors, the roof stair and market day (2026-11-06)

- **Why the hall could not be entered:** its walls were closed boxes — no door in the north, south or qibla walls, and the hall's west wall on the court solid. The two "north entrance towers" stood on the south wall (+z is south). Fixed as follows.
- **Main entrance:** "The main entrance is on the northern side of the building" [stated: Wikiwand]. Ours: a 2 m doorway with a palm-wood lintel in the hall's north wall, into its fourth aisle, between the two entrance towers, now on the north; the towers' inner faces buried in the wall, a cornice and five pinnacles on each, engaged ribs and toron on their outer faces [arrangement inferred from photographs of the north front].
- **Court to hall:** the court is "surrounded on three sides by galleries"; "the walls of the galleries facing the courtyard are punctuated by arched openings" [stated: Wikiwand]. Ours: six pointed-arched openings (1.8 m wide, springing at 2.5 m) in the hall's west wall, in line with the bays between the piers. The galleries themselves are not built yet.
- **Piers:** the nine arcaded rows are now spaced evenly between the west wall and the qibla wall (aisles of 2.3 m clear), so that the aisle along the west wall is walkable [inferred].
- **Roof stair:** "The towers in the qibla wall do not contain stairs linking the prayer hall with the roof"; two square towers hold the roof stairs, one at the south-west corner of the prayer hall, the other near the main entrance on the north, "only accessible from the exterior" [stated: Wikiwand]. Ours: the south-west tower (6.4 m square) against the hall's south wall, entered by a door from the hall's first aisle; inside, seven steep flights of banco steps (≈ 19 cm risers) winding round a solid newel with landings at the corners, slit windows, a door at the top onto the hall roof; pinnacles at its corners and toron on its outer faces [internal arrangement inferred]. The north stair tower is not built.
- **Market day:** Djenné holds "one of West Africa's most colorful markets — held on Monday — with merchants coming from throughout the region"; the town provisioned Timbuktu "with much needed cereals and dried fish" [stated: Archaeology magazine]. René Caillié (1828) described the market: kola sellers sitting in two lines, each with a small basket, selling for "eight or ten cowries"; butchers, meat in small pieces on skewers dried with smoke; fresh and dried fish, earthen pots, calabashes, mats, salt (wholesale in shops, retail in the market); fabrics, honey, vegetable and animal butter, milk; firewood brought in by women from 12 to 15 miles round; cowries the money [stated: Caillié via afrolegends]. Caillié is five centuries after our moment; the goods are those of the inland delta's long-lived trade and we use them for the 14th c. [inferred]. Ours (`town.js` `market()`, the `market_mat` element): stalls under mats in four north–south rows with 5 m aisles; sellers seated on carved stools behind mats of dried fish, rock-salt bars (lashed in pairs) and broken pieces, pots, calabashes, millet and rice, shea butter, narrow-strip cotton, rolled mats and smoked meat on skewers; at the market's north end the kola sellers in two facing lines with their small baskets; buyers in the aisles (women in wraps and head-ties, some carrying a pot or basket on the head; men in robes and caps); stacks of firewood with women by them; donkeys with panniers and goats along the east edge. A strip from the arrival toward the qibla front is kept clear. Dress, stools and the mix of goods per mat [inferred].

## 12. House fronts, the landing and the court galleries (2026-11-06, third pass)

- **Why:** the town's houses were plain boxes; worse, their facade's outward normal was reversed, so the pilasters and pinnacles stood buried inside the houses and the lanes showed blank walls.
- **The Sudan facade:** "Most traditional courtyard houses had a decorated facade that specialists call the Sudan facade", with pillars and decorated entrances, signalling the owner's status [stated: Aramco World 1990]. The houses "have imposing façades with pilaster like buttresses", with "an elaborate arrangement of pinnacles forming the parapet above the entrance door"; toron project about 60 cm; ceramic pipes from the roofline [stated: Wikipedia, Djenné]. The facade's parts are named by Maas & Mommersteeg: the corner pilaster *sarafar har* ("male pilaster"), the mitre-shaped pinnacle *sarafar idye* ("child pilaster"), the panel over the entrance, the toron, the window *tuney*, and the facade bench *tintin* [stated: Bulletin KNOB, glossary]. Ours (`sahel_house`): corner pilasters rising as tall pinnacles; the two pilasters framing the entrance taller still; between them a proud panel over the door up past the parapet with a frieze of small blocks, a moulding and a row of five mitre pinnacles; a lower intermediate pilaster on a long front; benches either side of the door; a plank door with battens and an iron ring in a banco frame under a palm-wood lintel; toron in a row on each storey; two clay spouts. Number and size of pinnacles, the frieze [inferred from photographs].
- **Two house types:** "Some of the houses built before 1900 are in the Toucouleur-style and have a massive covered entrance porch", with "a single small window onto the street set above the entrance door"; "many of the more recent two-storey houses are in the Moroccan-style and have small ornate windows" and lack the porch [stated: Wikipedia, Djenné]. Caillié: the entrance "closed by a door made of wooden planks" [stated: ibid.]. Ours: about half the two-storey houses Tukulor (a porch of two banco piers under a roof of palm beams and banco, one small window with timber bars above), the rest Moroccan (two or three small windows in the upper storey, each in a moulded frame with a stepped pointed head and a sill, a grille of laths; a small canopy over the door); one-storey houses Tukulor. Both types are 19th-c. forms; the 14th-c. houses are not documented and we use them as the town's long-lived manner [inferred].
- **The landing:** the town lies on the floodplain of the Niger and the Bani; Malfante's informant: "There are many boats on it, by which they carry on trade"; Leo Africanus: merchants used "small narrow canoes" for the trade with Timbuktu during the flood [stated: Wikipedia, Djenné]. Ours (`town.js` `landing()`): on the south shore toward the Bani, long narrow plank canoes moored along the bank (one in five with a mat shelter), two poled out into the channel, more drawn up on the bank; nets folded over poles to dry (`net_rack`), baskets, sacks and jars; porters, boatmen and women with loads [arrangement and gear inferred].
- **The court galleries:** the court is surrounded on three sides by galleries whose walls on the court are punctuated by arched openings [stated: Wikiwand]; the galleries are about 4 m wide [stated: Aramco World 1990]. Ours: arcades of pointed arches (1.7 m wide, springing at 2.5 m) between low pilasters with small pinnacles, 4 m in from the north, west and south walls, roofed with palm beams under banco at 5.2 m, the beams showing in the ceiling [arch rhythm and height inferred].
