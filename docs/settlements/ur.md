## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Great Ziggurat (E-temen-ni-gur), built by Ur-Nammu (2112–2095 BC), completed by Shulgi [stated: odysseyadventures; Studio Matrx].
- **Moment shown:** about 2050 BC, under Shulgi.

## 4. The style

Mud brick with baked-brick facing, the walls broken by shallow buttresses and recesses; flat roofs; bitumen; reed and palm-trunk timber. [stated: brick and bitumen; inferred: the rest]

The ziggurat's skin (L3, built): a mud-brick core with a casing about 2.4 m thick of baked brick set in bitumen, mats of reed every six to eight courses in the core, and many drains from the core [stated: stevewatson after Woolley]; "weeper holes" pierce the sloping walls so the core can dry out [stated: Britannica; Alberti's Window]. Ours: rows of small weeper holes every 1.8 m up each stage, midway between the buttresses (not behind the stairs); low parapets rising with the central stair [inferred].

### 4.1 The temenos (L2, built)

- **The terrace:** Ur-Nammu's terrace wall "a massive wall, buttressed and sloping sharply inwards", of unbaked brick, with rows of nail-shaped clay cones set in its face whose round heads formed a pattern, their stems inscribed with the king's name and the dedication to the Moon God [stated: Penn Museum Journal, "The Expedition to Ur"]. Ours: the terrace 2.5 m high in mud brick, its faces leaning in 0.6 m, buttresses every 4 m, two rows of cone heads every 0.32 m near the top following the slope; the rooms' walls stand on the terrace 2.5 m back from its edge so the face shows [inferred: spacing, rows, setback].
- **The Edublalmah:** the gate tower in front of the ziggurat steps, a gateway with very thick burnt-brick walls and wide doors [stated: Penn Museum Journal]. Ours: a tower 16 × 12 m, 9 m high, of baked brick at the Court of Nanna's entrance, a passage 3 m wide through it, two cedar leaves bound in bronze standing open inside, buttressed faces; a like tower of mud brick at the temenos's north gate [inferred].
- **Gateways of the courtyard buildings** (the Giparu, E-hursag, the stores; Babylon's Esagila too): a deep doorway in the middle of the side between two projecting towers, cedar doors at the back of the reveal, a brick threshold — after Koldewey's account of Esagila's gateways "elaborated with massive projecting towers" [stated for Babylon; inferred for Ur].
- **Stairs:** brick steps on a wedge of brickwork (the ziggurat's flights, the terrace stair before the Edublalmah and the flights down to the harbour's water), all built by the Sumerian stair builder [inferred].

### 4.1a The upper stages and the stair through the gatehouse (built)

Three flights meet at the top of the first stage below a gatehouse, "after which only the central staircase continues" [stated: stevewatson]. Ours: the second and third stages set back from the centre toward the rear (5 m and 8 m) so that the first stage keeps a broad terrace before them and each flight rises from the front edge of the stage below at a walkable pitch (about 0.16 m risers on 0.25–0.29 m treads); the gatehouse 13 × 4 m and 6.2 m high stands at the head of the three flights, its way 5.2 m wide and 4.8 m high under a palm-trunk lintel, and the single stair climbs through it to the second stage and on to the top [inferred: the set-backs (Woolley's reconstruction places the upper stages toward the back), the gatehouse's size]. Before this the upper stairs started in mid-air and could not be climbed.

### 4.1b The ziggurat in the light (L3, built 2026-11-12)

- **What the browser review found:** from the arrival (the Court of Nanna, 95 m from the ziggurat's face) the ziggurat read as a dark mass: the terrace's enclosure wall (3.5 m on the 2.5 m terrace) hid the lowest 10 m of the first stage and all three stairways, and the north face stands away from the sun (az 150) at the hours of the visit. **Ours:** the front of the enclosure is a 1.2 m parapet of pale baked brick (the side and rear walls keep their height), so the first stage, the stairs and the gatehouse show over it.
- **Skin and weathering:** the casing of fired brick laid in bitumen [stated: albertis-window, "The Bricks of the Ziggurat of Ur": the outer layer in waterproof bitumen; criss-crossed reeds and sandy soil every few courses; holes lined with fired brick pierce the walls to let the core dry]. **Ours:** dark seams of bitumen every 2.4 m across the faces (the joints' bands), patches where the casing has spalled and the mud-brick core shows, a white salt bloom running down from them, rubble and fallen brick banked at the foot of the lowest stage, and buttresses 1.8 m broad and 0.8 m deep (the recesses between them throw shadow and break the long faces; the earlier ones were 1.1 m and 0.55 m and vanished beyond 40 m) [inferred: the dimensions; Woolley's buttresses were shallow].
- **Colour:** stage 1 buff, stage 2 red (as before), stage 3 buff; the gatehouse where the stairs meet and the house of Nanna are limewashed white, as the temples of the Sumerian tradition (the White Temple of Uruk) were [inferred], so that the summit, the gatehouse and the standard read as a crown against the sky.
- **The summit:** a lapis frieze 0.6 m high under the parapet round the shrine, a lapis panel on the face of each tower, and on the roof the standard of Nanna, a bronze-bound pole 4.4 m high bearing a gilded crescent 2.4 m across, horns up, facing the court [inferred: the crescent as the moon god's emblem; standards in pairs flanking temple doors are [stated] for Early Dynastic Girsu and on the model of the Anu ziggurat at Uruk: "two Bügelschafts flank an opening, or a door" — repositorio.uca.edu.ar, "architectural origin mesopotamian standars"; the "ŠEŠ" bügelschaft may represent Nanna, same source].

### 4.2 The house of Nanna on the top (L2, built)

- **What is known:** the Nanna temple on the top "has not survived"; blue glazed bricks found at the site may have belonged to its decoration [stated: Lumen "Ziggurat of Ur"] — but the shrine was later faced with blue glazed bricks by Nebuchadnezzar [stated: stevewatson], so for 2050 BC the full facing is Nebuchadnezzar's; ours has a lapis frieze under the parapet, a panel on each tower's face and a band on the gatehouse (§4.1b) as an [inferred] reading of the glazed bricks found at the site (the earlier removal of the blue band is reversed: the review found the summit unreadable without it). The reconstruction is "a modest sanctuary at the top" [stated: stevewatson]. Sumerian temples of the "house plan" had "a main entrance flanked by towers", the altar reached directly through antechambers [stated: stevewatson]; the faces of the stages and of temples were decorated with buttresses and recesses [stated: stevewatson].
- **Ours:** 14 × 11 m, 5 m high, plastered mud brick with buttresses every 2.2 m; the doorway (1.6 × 2.9 m) in the middle of the north front between two towers 2 × 1.1 m standing 6.4 m high, a palm-trunk lintel, cedar doors bound with bronze standing open, a baked-brick threshold; an antecella 3 m deep across the front; the cella behind it on the same axis (a broad room 11.2 × 4.2 m) with the god's podium (3.8 × 1.5 m, 1 m high, three brick steps) against the back wall under a raised niche, Nanna's crescent in gold on a bronze standard on it, an offering table with three bowls before it, brick benches at the ends, an oil lamp on a stand; floors of baked brick; the roof of mud on reed mats on palm trunks 0.75 m apart, with a parapet [inferred: plan, sizes, the furnishings; the crescent as the moon god's emblem is [inferred] from his name and cult].

## 5. The town

Courtyard houses of one and two storeys, plastered mud brick, blank to the lanes, rooms round an open court. [inferred]

Built (L2): after Woolley's houses (of the Isin–Larsa period, a little later than ours) — kiln-fired brick for the foundations and the lower courses, mud brick above, thick mud plaster sometimes whitewashed; the court paved in baked tiles sloping to a drain; a stairway in the court to the roof or the upper floor; postholes for a wooden gallery round the court; wooden doors and door frames; a narrow street door; windows to the street, if any, only upstairs, shuttered or latticed; mud roofs over matting on rafters, with projecting gutters [stated: odysseyadventures, "Ur of the Chaldees — Houses"]. Ours: a brick footing 0.8–1.25 m high, plaster or (about one house in three) whitewash above; the doorway 1 m wide and 2.1 m high in a reveal 0.3 m deep, with a cedar leaf, lintel and brick threshold, set near a corner of its side; in two-storey houses small latticed windows at 4.1 m; one to four clay spouts through the parapet; a brick stair along the court's longer side to the roof (one storey) or to a gallery of timber on posts round the other three sides (two storeys, courts at least 3.4 m across) [inferred: the dimensions and the share of each kind].

### 5.1 Three houses to enter, furnished (L3, built 2026-11-07)

- **Sources.** Woolley: "There was a central court with a wooden gallery running round it"; "the family lived above; on the ground floor were the reception room and the domestic offices"; "the rooms were lofty"; one room had "a niche in the end wall and in front of the niche a raised block of brickwork like an altar", perhaps "a domestic shrine"; "often beneath the pavement we find clay coffins or vaulted brick tombs", with "clay vessels of offerings" [stated: Penn Museum Journal, Museum Notes ?p=13251]. The "House of Abraham": a paved central courtyard with a drain of terracotta rings, a vestibule between street and court, "a reception room (liwan) for guests", "a kitchen with a brick hearth and bread oven", storerooms, "a lavatory with a brick-paved floor and drainage channel", no ground-floor windows [stated: Madain Project, House of Abraham (Ur)].
- **Ours** (`ur_house` with `open`, src/styles/sumerian/house.js): the three houses on the north side of the market square (§5.2) are built hollow, one storey (the upper floor left out), 3.4 m to the roof, rooms on three sides of a court open to the sky: the street door of cedar standing open into a lobby with a brick bench and a jar of water, its door into the court at its far end (the dog-leg that keeps the court out of sight); a lavatory with brick footrests either side of a pottery drain; a storeroom with a row of great jars, sacks of barley and a basket of dates; on the side range the kitchen — a brick hearth against the outer wall with ash and a cooking pot on it and soot over it, the clay bread oven (tannur) in the corner, a saddle quern of basalt with its rubbing stone on a mud bench, a water jar, dung cakes and palm ribs for fuel; across the back the reception room with its cedar doors standing open — reed mats, a couch (timber frame, linen mattress, a red coverlet, a bolster), a low table with a spouted jug and cups, two stools, a cedar chest bound in bronze, a basket, a clay lamp burning on a shelf with soot above it; beside it the chapel — the niche with its whitened back between jambs, the altar of brick before it with a clay figurine, two offering bowls and a lamp, and a patch of relaid brick where the family's vault lies under the pavement; the court paved in brick with its drain, jars, the brick stair to the roof along one side; flat roofs of mud on reed mats on palm trunks, seen from inside, with a parapet round the roof and the court and clay spouts [inferred: the room sizes and places, the furnishings' forms after the Royal Cemetery's and the Standard of Ur's furniture, the lamps].

### 6.1 The harbour's boats and quay (L2, built)

- Boat models of bitumen and of silver were placed in the graves of the Royal Cemetery, "often loaded with small containers, as if carrying provisions for the dead" [stated: sumerianshakespeare, Royal Tombs]; a bitumen boat model from an early grave at Ur measures 940 × 300 mm [stated: Penn Museum, object 331509]. The silver model from PG 789 (the King's Grave) has the long narrow hull with both ends rising high [inferred: as generally photographed].
- **Ours:** boats 7–9 m long and about a sixth as broad, of reed bundles coated with bitumen, open, the ends drawn up into horns about 1.6–1.9 m high curving in over the hull, the bundles bound with cord at intervals; four thwarts, a reed mat floor, a punting pole; some carry jars or bundles of reeds. Four lie along the quay, five out in the basin. Three flights of baked-brick steps go down from the quay into the water, and palm-trunk mooring posts stand along its edge every 9 m [inferred: sizes, numbers, the quay furniture].

### 4.3 The Giparu (built, walkable)

- **Sources.** Woolley's Giparu: a nearly square compound 79 × 76.5 m inside brick walls over 4.5 m thick, buttressed on three sides; a central passage splits it into two blocks, each with its own formal entrance. The southeast block is the Ningal temple: a monumental gateway with towers and a long passage, two offset lobbies so the street cannot see in, a forecourt, the main court with a lustration tank, a limestone pillar, a platform for a stela, a brick altar (1.55 x 0.95 x 1.05 m) and votive platforms, antechambers that are shrines to lesser gods, the inner sanctum with the statue pedestal and a priestess's platform, a bedchamber, a treasury, a weaver's workspace, and a kitchen court with a well, water tank, fireplaces, bread oven and cooking range. The northwest block has a double doorway, three bitumen-lined tanks for lustration beside the entrance, a forecourt and main court, a lustration antechamber, a room with a platform in a niche, a room of clay tablets, antechambers to a cella with an altar and votive pedestals, a treasury. Under the east corner lie subterranean brick tombs, one per room, perhaps of high priestesses [stated: odysseyadventures, "The Giparu"].
- **Ours** (`giparu`, src/styles/sumerian/temple.js): the same plan scaled to the lot (62 x 64 m), entered by the west gateway between two towers and cedar doors; the street through the middle with the two blocks' gateways and towers on it; every room above built hollow with doorways, roofs of mud on palm trunks, a stair from the forecourt to the north range's roof; furnished as listed above (statue of limestone with a gold crown on a stepped podium, offering table, lamps, loom with warp and cloth, couch and chests, jars and ingots, tablets on a bench, well and tanks); relaid-brick patches over the vaults [inferred: sizes, the furnishings' forms, the scaling].

### 4.4 The Court of Nanna furnished (L3, built)

- **Sources.** The court before the terrace was entered through the Edublalmah, "a gateway with very thick burnt-brick walls" [stated: Penn Museum Journal, §4.1]; its pavement and the cult of the Moon God needed what any Sumerian temple court had — an altar for burnt offerings, tables of offerings, water for the hands, benches for the waiting, braziers [inferred from Sumerian temple practice; no inventory of Ur-Nammu's court survives]. Royal stelae stood in the courts (Ur-Nammu's stela, carved in registers, came from the temenos) [inferred: the stela's form from the surviving fragments' registers]. The brick-making: bricks of about 12 x 12 x 3 in or 14 x 14 x 4 in, burnt or sun-dried, set in bitumen [stated: Penn Museum Journal, "The Builders' Art at Ur"].
- **Ours** (`ur_court`, src/styles/sumerian/court.js): benches of baked brick along the inner faces of the east, west and north walls in runs of 6.5 m with gaps; the altar of burnt offering (a block 3.4 x 2.4 x 1.15 m of baked brick, three steps, a blackened top, horns at the corners, a fire burning with its light) on the west of the axis and the table of offerings (bowls, loaves, a jar, 3.4 m long) on the east; two bitumen-lined basins on plinths either side of the way in, jars of water by them; two stelae of limestone 3.5 m high on plinths, with four registers of relief; four tripod braziers of bronze, two lit; a pavilion of reed mats on palm posts (east) with a bench; a pen of reed mats on palm posts (west) with a trough and fodder; stacks of split palm trunks; five jars on ring stands by the entrance; six date palms in brick-edged pits; four more flanking the avenue before the Edublalmah [inferred: all placing, sizes and numbers].

### 4.5 The palace E-hursag and the storehouses as walkable buildings (L3, built)

- **Sources.** The palace is one of three well-preserved Ur III structures [stated: Madain Project]. Temple and palace crafts using imported materials (precious metal, stone, ivory, copper) were worked in institutional settings; one administrator oversaw craftsmen in wood, ivory, precious metals, fine stones, copper, leather, rope and reeds [stated: encyclopedia.com, "Craft Manufacture and Production"]. Rooms of Mesopotamian palaces were ranged round open courts; audience halls had a dais [inferred from Mesopotamian palace plans generally].
- **Ours** (`ur_hall`, hall.js): rooms of plastered mud brick on a baked-brick footing round a brick-paved court, each with a doorway on the court (cedar leaves open against the jambs, a palm-trunk lintel), the gate through the middle of the front range between two towers, the way up to the roof by a brick stair against the front range; flat roofs on palm trunks and reed matting with parapets. The palace (west gate): the guard's room and a scribes' office by the gate; the audience hall across the back (a dais with the king's seat of cedar bound with bronze, a footstool, benches with red cushions, piers carrying the roof, two lit braziers) between two chambers (couch, chest, lamp); kitchens (ovens, quern, jars, fuel, soot) and stores on the sides; a bitumen-lined basin and four palms in the court. The storehouses: rows of great jars, sacks, a bale of wool, tablets sealed on a bench, lamps; a guard room and an office at the gate [inferred: plan, sizes, furnishings].

### 5.5 Trade houses and the temple's workshops (L3, built)

- **Sources.** Textiles were the most important craft; "temple factory records list more than six thousand workers, mostly women, along with their children"; food processing (beer, bread, cheese) was second, then metalwork, then pottery [stated: encyclopedia.com, "Craft Manufacture and Production"]. Beer was a staple; the stamped brick and bitumen show building as an industry [stated: Penn Museum Journal]. The trades' tools — the ground loom with its loom weights, the potter's wheel (a mud-brick bench and disc), clay kilns, great vats, the clay oven, the bellows and mould — are those of Mesopotamian archaeology generally [inferred].
- **Ours.** About 55 houses are built hollow and furnished as of one trade, each with its worker at his place (a figure in `life.js`): the weaver's loom, the potter's wheel and clay with a kiln in the court, the brewer's vats and straws, the baker's two ovens, the smith's hearth and moulds, the fisher's nets and racks, the reed-worker's bundles, the granary's bins. They stand by the places where the trades were: the market, the north and east streets, the quay, the south road. Inside the temenos's open ground (west of the court, west of the terrace, east of the terrace, south of it) lie 75 more lots on lanes — the temple's workshops (weavers most, then bakers, brewers, granaries, a potter, a smith), one in three entered; the rest solid, their doors dressed with the trade's wares (`TEMPLE_ZONES`, layout.js) [inferred: places, numbers].

About one lot in twelve stayed empty in the plan; in the core they are a pen of reed mats on palm posts with sheep, goats or oxen in it, or a little orchard of three date palms; palms stand at some lane corners (`ur_pen`, `ur_palm`) [inferred].

### 6.2 The harbour's crews and the yard (built)

A punter at the stern of most moored boats, a man seated amidships in some; women kneeling to wash at the three flights of steps with their baskets; fishermen with spears at the quay's edge and a boy with a line; reed-cutters bringing bundles up from the marsh with a donkey; oxen watering at the steps with a herd boy; a boat of reed bundles on trestles in the yard behind the quay with three men binding it, stacks of reeds, a net and its mender. Boats do not yet move (the movers have no reed-boat shape) [inferred].
