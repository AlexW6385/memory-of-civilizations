## 4. The style

Mud brick with baked-brick facing, the walls broken by shallow buttresses and recesses; flat roofs; bitumen; reed and palm-trunk timber. [stated: brick and bitumen; inferred: the rest]

The ziggurat's skin (L3, built): a mud-brick core with a casing about 2.4 m thick of baked brick set in bitumen, mats of reed every six to eight courses in the core, and many drains from the core [stated: stevewatson after Woolley]; "weeper holes" pierce the sloping walls so the core can dry out [stated: Britannica; Alberti's Window]. Ours: rows of small weeper holes every 1.8 m up each stage, midway between the buttresses (not behind the stairs); low parapets rising with the central stair [inferred].

### 4.1 The temenos (L2, built)

- **The terrace:** Ur-Nammu's terrace wall "a massive wall, buttressed and sloping sharply inwards", of unbaked brick, with rows of nail-shaped clay cones set in its face whose round heads formed a pattern, their stems inscribed with the king's name and the dedication to the Moon God [stated: Penn Museum Journal, "The Expedition to Ur"]. Ours: the terrace 2.5 m high in mud brick, its faces leaning in 0.6 m, buttresses every 4 m, two rows of cone heads every 0.32 m near the top following the slope; the rooms' walls stand on the terrace 2.5 m back from its edge so the face shows [inferred: spacing, rows, setback].
- **The Edublalmah:** the gate tower in front of the ziggurat steps, a gateway with very thick burnt-brick walls and wide doors [stated: Penn Museum Journal]. Ours: a tower 16 × 12 m, 9 m high, of baked brick at the Court of Nanna's entrance, a passage 3 m wide through it, two cedar leaves bound in bronze standing open inside, buttressed faces; a like tower of mud brick at the temenos's north gate [inferred].
- **Gateways of the courtyard buildings** (the Giparu, E-hursag, the stores; Babylon's Esagila too): a deep doorway in the middle of the side between two projecting towers, cedar doors at the back of the reveal, a brick threshold — after Koldewey's account of Esagila's gateways "elaborated with massive projecting towers" [stated for Babylon; inferred for Ur].
- **Stairs:** brick steps on a wedge of brickwork (the ziggurat's flights, the terrace stair before the Edublalmah and the flights down to the harbour's water), all built by the Sumerian stair builder [inferred].

### 4.1a The upper stages and the stair through the gatehouse (built)

Three flights meet at the top of the first stage below a gatehouse, "after which only the central staircase continues" [stated: stevewatson]. Ours: the second and third stages set back from the centre toward the rear (5 m and 8 m) so that the first stage keeps a broad terrace before them and each flight rises from the front edge of the stage below at a walkable pitch (about 0.16 m risers on 0.25–0.29 m treads); the gatehouse 13 × 4 m and 6.2 m high stands at the head of the three flights, its way 5.2 m wide and 4.8 m high under a palm-trunk lintel, and the single stair climbs through it to the second stage and on to the top [inferred: the set-backs (Woolley's reconstruction places the upper stages toward the back), the gatehouse's size]. Before this the upper stairs started in mid-air and could not be climbed.

### 4.1b The ziggurat in the light (L3, built 2026-11-12)

- **What the browser review found:** from the arrival (the Court of Nanna, 95 m from the ziggurat's face) the ziggurat read as a dark mass: the terrace's enclosure wall (3.5 m on the 2.5 m terrace) hid the lowest 10 m of the first stage and all three stairways, and the north face stands away from the sun (az 150) at the hours of the visit. **Ours:** the front of the enclosure is a 1.2 m parapet of pale baked brick (the side and rear walls keep their height), so the first stage, the stairs and the gatehouse show over it.
- **Skin and weathering:** the casing of fired brick laid in bitumen [stated: albertis-window, "The Bricks of the Ziggurat of Ur": the outer layer in waterproof bitumen; criss-crossed reeds and sandy soil every few courses; holes lined with fired brick pierce the walls to let the core dry]. **Ours:** dark seams of bitumen every 2.4 m across the faces (the joints' bands), patches where the casing has spalled and the mud-brick core shows, a white salt bloom running down from them, rubble and fallen brick banked at the foot of the lowest stage, and buttresses 1.8 m broad and 0.8 m deep (the recesses between them throw shadow and break the long faces; the earlier ones were 1.1 m and 0.55 m and vanished beyond 40 m) [inferred: the dimensions; Woolley's buttresses were shallow].
- **Colour:** stage 1 buff, stage 2 red (as before), stage 3 buff; the gatehouse where the stairs meet and the house of Nanna are limewashed white, as the temples of the Sumerian tradition (the White Temple of Uruk) were [inferred], so that the summit, the gatehouse and the standard read as a crown against the sky.
- **The summit:** a lapis frieze 0.6 m high under the parapet round the shrine, a lapis panel on the face of each tower, and on the roof the standard of Nanna, a bronze-bound pole 4.4 m high bearing a gilded crescent 2.4 m across, horns up, facing the court [inferred: the crescent as the moon god's emblem; standards in pairs flanking temple doors are [stated] for Early Dynastic Girsu and on the model of the Anu ziggurat at Uruk: "two Bügelschafts flank an opening, or a door" — repositorio.uca.edu.ar, "architectural origin mesopotamian standars"; the "ŠEŠ" bügelschaft may represent Nanna, same source].

### 4.3 The Giparu (built, walkable)

- **Sources.** Woolley's Giparu: a nearly square compound 79 × 76.5 m inside brick walls over 4.5 m thick, buttressed on three sides; a central passage splits it into two blocks, each with its own formal entrance. The southeast block is the Ningal temple: a monumental gateway with towers and a long passage, two offset lobbies so the street cannot see in, a forecourt, the main court with a lustration tank, a limestone pillar, a platform for a stela, a brick altar (1.55 x 0.95 x 1.05 m) and votive platforms, antechambers that are shrines to lesser gods, the inner sanctum with the statue pedestal and a priestess's platform, a bedchamber, a treasury, a weaver's workspace, and a kitchen court with a well, water tank, fireplaces, bread oven and cooking range. The northwest block has a double doorway, three bitumen-lined tanks for lustration beside the entrance, a forecourt and main court, a lustration antechamber, a room with a platform in a niche, a room of clay tablets, antechambers to a cella with an altar and votive pedestals, a treasury. Under the east corner lie subterranean brick tombs, one per room, perhaps of high priestesses [stated: odysseyadventures, "The Giparu"].

### 4.5 The palace E-hursag and the storehouses as walkable buildings (L3, built)

Rooms of Mesopotamian palaces were ranged round open courts; audience halls had a dais [inferred from Mesopotamian palace plans generally].

### 5.5 Trade houses and the temple's workshops (L3, built)

- **Sources.** Textiles were the most important craft; "temple factory records list more than six thousand workers, mostly women, along with their children"; food processing (beer, bread, cheese) was second, then metalwork, then pottery [stated: encyclopedia.com, "Craft Manufacture and Production"]. Beer was a staple; the stamped brick and bitumen show building as an industry [stated: Penn Museum Journal]. The trades' tools — the ground loom with its loom weights, the potter's wheel (a mud-brick bench and disc), clay kilns, great vats, the clay oven, the bellows and mould — are those of Mesopotamian archaeology generally [inferred].
- **Ours.** About 55 houses are built hollow and furnished as of one trade, each with its worker at his place (a figure in `life.js`): the weaver's loom, the potter's wheel and clay with a kiln in the court, the brewer's vats and straws, the baker's two ovens, the smith's hearth and moulds, the fisher's nets and racks, the reed-worker's bundles, the granary's bins. They stand by the places where the trades were: the market, the north and east streets, the quay, the south road. Inside the temenos's open ground (west of the court, west of the terrace, east of the terrace, south of it) lie 75 more lots on lanes — the temple's workshops (weavers most, then bakers, brewers, granaries, a potter, a smith), one in three entered; the rest solid, their doors dressed with the trade's wares (`TEMPLE_ZONES`, layout.js) [inferred: places, numbers].

### 5.8 The fronts of the houses by class (L3, built 2026-11-16)

- **Sources.** The houses of Ur had thick solid outer walls, windows to the street only in the upper storeys, "shuttered or fitted with latticework screens"; "a relatively narrow door led straight from the street to a paved lobby"; wooden doors on posts set in brick or stone sockets; "some doorways were arched. A fallen example of baked brick was found at No. 3 New Street"; larger houses had a second courtyard suite and a private chapel at the rear [stated: odysseyadventures, "Ur of the Chaldees — Houses"]. A wedge-shaped brick mould for the bricks of an arch is attested from c. 3000 BC [stated: biblicalanthropology.blogspot.com, "Brick making in antiquity"]. House scale and layout varied [stated: same, odysseyadventures].
- **Ours** (`ur_house` of the style `sumerian-ur`, src/styles/sumerian-ur/house.js; the shared `sumerian` style is left as it was, Babylon builds on it): the class of a front is drawn from the lot's seed and its nearness to the temple (richer near it) — 0 plain (a flat cedar lintel in the wall face; about 40 % of the houses), 1 framed (a plastered limewashed frame round the door, a brick step, merlons at the parapet's corners), 2 arched (an arch of baked-brick voussoirs, alternating baked and plain brick, proud of the face, the corners above it filled with plaster, the door's leaf rounded to the arch; also the ends of the palm-trunk rafters showing under the parapet), 3 grand (arched door, a triple lattice window above it, two pilasters rising above the parapet beside the door and a crown block over it; only on lots at least 11 m across, always limewashed and two-storey). The classes' share in the core is larger than at the edge. The houses that can be entered get the outer features of class 1 and 2 (frame, step, rafter ends, merlons) [inferred: the classes and their rule; the arch and the lattice windows after Woolley's finds; the rafter ends and merlons from the palm-trunk roofs and the parapets; the crown block and pilasters from the towered gateways of the temples].

### 5.9 The brickyard (L3, built 2026-11-16)

- **Sources.** The bricks of Ur-Nammu's city wall were stamped bricks about 0.37 m square and 0.10 m thick, "specially moulded for the construction of the city wall" [stated: Woolley 1974, ch. X–XI, ancientportsantiques.com]. Workers kneaded the clay with their feet or wooden tampers, with water and chopped straw; the clay was pressed into wooden moulds and dried in the sun, up to two weeks; kilns reached about 900–1000 °C, fired with reeds, farm waste and possibly bitumen, and stood near clay and water [stated: historyrise.com, "Uruk's construction techniques"]; baked brick "was stronger", the ziggurats had a sun-dried core and a kiln-baked skin [stated: biblicalanthropology.blogspot.com]; bitumen served as mortar and waterproofing [stated: historyrise.com]. Brickmaking was a trade of its own, a skilled class [stated: same]. The shaduf appears on a cylinder seal of about 2200 BC [stated: Wikipedia, "Shadoof"], before our date.
- **Ours** (`brickyard`, src/styles/sumerian-ur/brickyard.js; the lots in its 70 × 66 m rect are cleared, the ground there is trodden clay): two clay beds (a kerb of mud brick, wet clay inside, a skin of water) with men treading, a heap of chopped straw, a shaduf lifting from a pool (two mud-brick posts, a palm-trunk pole, a bitumen-coated basket on the long end, a clay counterweight on the short), drying fields (rows of bricks 0.37 × 0.37 × 0.10 moulded flat, older rows stood on edge, six open hacks of crossed courses), wooden moulds lying about, two updraft kilns of clay 5 m across and 3.5 m high with the stacked brick showing at the top and an arched stoke-hole at the foot (ash before it, reeds and dung cakes for fuel), five stacks of fired brick, the pot of bitumen over a fire of three stones, a shade of reed mats for the water jars; the people at their places (treaders, the man at the shaduf, moulders squatting, carriers, stokers, the boiler of bitumen, the overseer, a donkey with its load, children bringing water, a dog); smoke from the kilns and the bitumen fire [inferred: the layout, the kiln's form (the sources give no Ur kiln), the numbers].

### 5.10 The city rampart and its gates (L3, built 2026-11-16)

- **Sources.** The walled city was an irregular oval about 1,200 m long and 800 m wide; the rampart a mud-brick retaining wall and revetment about 8 m high where best preserved, its base 17–29 m wide, with a steep sloped face; a burnt-brick battlement or wall ran along the top, of which little survived; "no gates could be found", though a gate is suspected at two places and sally ports conjectured [stated: Woolley 1974 ch. X–XI]. The wall's novelty "worth study", no traces surviving of the defences built against invaders from the north-west [stated: stevewatson, "Notes on architectural novelties of Ur III"]. The west harbour about 105 m across with an entrance 9 m wide, its moles of mud brick; the north harbour larger, behind a mud bank [stated: Woolley 1974].
- **Ours** (`rampart`, src/styles/sumerian-ur/rampart.js, a ring at radius 368 m, compressed with the whole town): a section 14 m at the foot and 5 m at the crest, 7 m high, the outer face steeper (the faces banded in mud brick and plaster for the courses of reed matting), a battlement of burnt brick 1.1 m thick and 1 m high with a merlon every 2.4 m, built round the whole circle except at the harbour; three gates on the three land streets (north, east, south), each a gate tower of baked brick 28 × 14 × 10.5 m with a passage 5 m wide under a lintel, cedar leaves open, two guards with spears; the lots in front of the south gate cleared for its road; no planting on the wall [inferred: the section's numbers within the source's, the gates' places and form (none is attested), the guards].

### 5.11 The royal mausoleum of Shulgi (L3, built 2026-11-16)

- **Sources.** The mausolea of Ur-Nammu, Shulgi and Amar-Sin lay just outside the temenos; chambers built below the ground level of the time, steps leading down to a floor of baked brick set in bitumen, roofed with baked-brick corbel vaulting, funerary chapels above them; they were looted in antiquity and only traces of gold leaf remained [stated: stevewatson, "Notes on architectural novelties of Ur III"; a later reading takes them for tombs of people privileged by the cult, not kings: Moorey 1984, cited in the same page's comment]. The Dungi (Shulgi) mausoleum: a courtyard, a foundation-deposit chamber (Room 4), a room with incense hearths and an altar (Room 5), a stepped entrance (Room 6), a tomb shaft with stairs leading to a north-west chamber and a south-east tomb chamber, "the vault over the NW flight of stairs", the corbelled roof of the tomb chambers [stated: Woolley 1974 ch. X–XI, plate captions].
- **Ours** (`ur_mausoleum`, src/styles/sumerian-ur/mausoleum.js; 60 × 62 m outside the temenos's east side, south of the east street, its lots cleared): the ground cannot be cut (the planet's sea level would flood a pit), so the complex stands on made ground 3 m high — a battered ring of mud brick round a core of baked brick laid in layers with the tomb's voids left in it — and the chambers lie at the town's level, below the court's pavement. A stair of 19 steps from the lane to the gate (between two towers, cedar leaves open); the forecourt; a partition with a doorway; the court of offerings (an altar of libation with its drain, two bitumen-lined basins with water, brick benches along the walls, six palms in brick pits); the front wall of the cult rooms with three doorways and, behind it, Room 5 the incense room (a stepped altar of baked brick with horns and a blackened top, two hearths of incense with fire, a limestone pedestal with the king's stela, a table of offerings, benches, lamps, mats), Room 4 the foundation deposit (a cist of baked brick with a copper figure and its peg, a stone tablet, jars) and Room 6 the stepped room whose floor opens on a stair of 19 steps down 3 m into a trench, through a passage under the dividing wall, into two chambers 10 × 4.4 m each with a floor of baked brick in bitumen, the walls rising 1.4 m and then corbelled in six courses of 0.3 m to a roof of baked brick, a link between them; in the chambers two brick biers with reed mats along the walls, jars of offerings, a lamp in a niche, a trace of gold leaf, bronze vessels. Roofs of mud on reed mats on palm trunks; lamps and fires lit; smoke from the hearths; a priest at the altar of the incense room, one at the court's altar, the keeper at the gate [inferred: the plan's proportions and the rooms' furnishings (only the names and the order of the rooms and the corbelled vaults are attested), the made ground, the sizes].
