# Ming Suzhou, c. 1530 — dossier

L3 items (furniture, inscribed boards, penjing, people and the life of the streets, the interiors of the houses) recorded for later.

## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Humble Administrator's Garden (Zhuozheng Yuan, 拙政园), Suzhou. Laid out between 1509 and 1513 by the retired censor Wang Xianchen on the site of the burnt Dahong Temple, with his friend the painter Wen Zhengming. [stated: Wikipedia; J-STAGE 2013]
- **Moment shown:** 1533, when Wen Zhengming painted 31 views of the garden, each with a poem and a note, and wrote his *Record of the Humble Administrator's Garden*. [stated: Wikipedia; University of Colorado image record] Later rebuildings (Qing; the 1950s restoration of the present three-part garden) are left out: we show the central garden only, as its Ming core. [inferred: the Ming garden's buildings were fewer and plainer than today's; we keep today's positions of those halls that the 1533 album names or that stand on the Ming ponds, and leave out the Qing western garden's halls]
- **Townscape:** the canal quarter of Suzhou east and south of the garden — Pingjiang Road and its lanes, which keep the street pattern shown on the *Pingjiang Tu* stone map of 1229: canals with a street running beside each ("water and land side by side"), stone bridges, whitewashed houses on the water. [stated: Wikipedia, Pingjiang Road; Suzhou government]

## 5. The vocabulary of the style (Jiangnan, Ming)

- **Columns:** slender, round, lacquered dark chestnut, on drum-shaped stone bases. [stated: general for Suzhou gardens; colour inferred from surviving Ming halls of the region]
- **Walls:** white lime plaster with a grey brick plinth; garden walls 4–5 m high capped with a little roof of black tiles; moon gates; latticed "leaky windows" (lou chuang) in the walls of the galleries and courts. [stated: the features of Suzhou gardens — Wikipedia, Classical Gardens of Suzhou]
- **Leaky windows (L3, built).** Leaky windows in partition walls came in with the Ming, when the Jiangnan gardens began to divide their space with inner walls and galleries; the late-Ming *Yuan Ye* calls such walls "leaking brick walls" and says the ancients prized the lozenge pattern while the moderns liked the willow-leaf; most frames are square or oblong, others polygons, circles and fans, freer ones the begonia (crab-apple) flower; the geometric patterns use straight lines, arcs or both — eight-direction, swastika fret, tortoise shell, coin — often combined, e.g. cracked ice with plum; the regular ones are built of flat bricks for short straight lines, pan tiles for arcs, round tiles for small circles, wood for long lines, all set in lime mortar [stated: People's Daily, 2024]. Patterns are geometric, natural, objects and figures [stated: China Discovery]. Ours: frames of square, octagon, hexagon, circle, fan and begonia outlines, each filled with one of four traceries — lozenge, coin, cracked ice (every crack ending on another), tortoise shell — in grey plastered tile and brick, inside a frame of dressed grey brick standing proud of the plaster [the frame's material inferred: the grey brick surrounds seen on the gardens' walls]. Ming walls hold the earlier, simpler geometric kinds; the pictorial kinds are mostly Qing [inferred from the People's Daily account of Qing multiplication].
- **Gateways (L3, built).** Moon gates and doorways lined and framed with dressed grey brick; over the gates a framed brick plaque for the gate's name (*men e*), its field left plain [inferred: the brick-framed openings and inscribed plaques of the surviving gardens; the inscriptions are not modelled].
- **Wall copings (L3, built).** The garden and court walls end in a little double-pitched roof of black tiles laid on a course of grey brick corbelled out from each face; the tiles show their thickness at the eaves (a row of eave ends) and the overhang has an underside, so that one passes under it at a gate [inferred: the tiled copings of the Suzhou garden walls as they survive; the corbelled course from the brick cornices under them]. The plinth of grey brick stops at each gate (a door's threshold is the paving's own) [inferred].
- **Hanging fretwork (gua luo), L3, built.** Between the posts of the galleries and pavilions, under the beam, an openwork band of slender dark bars: a top rail, a bottom rail, between them a square wave stepping up and down the band (our reading of the "step by step brocade", *bu bu jin*), tied to the rails, and at each end a leg dropping below the band with a small bracket in the angle [inferred: the gua luo of the Suzhou gardens' galleries and pavilions; the pattern simplified to one fret].
- **Galleries (L3, built).** Under the roof the frame shows: a tie beam across at each pair of posts, short struts on it under two purlins, a taller one under the ridge purlin; on the walled side a leaky window in every third bay, each of its own outline and tracery, so that the views change as one walks [inferred: the wall-side galleries of the garden; the galleries' winding, "up and down, turning and twisting", stated for Suzhou galleries — Terebess, *Classical Chinese Gardens*]. Where two runs meet at a turn the floors overlap (one 3 mm over the other, not coplanar).
- **Doors and windows:** tall lattice doors (ge shan) between the columns, a solid lower board, lattice over paper above; in halls seen from all sides, lattice on all four sides.
- **Roofs:** small black tiles on a strongly concave profile; the corners swept high (*fa qiang*, the Jiangnan "flying corner"); the main ridge ending in curls; hip-and-gable (*xieshan*) for halls, pyramids for pavilions, gables for houses. [stated: general]
- **Rock:** Taihu stone (holed, eroded limestone) heaped along the shores and piled into hills and single standing stones. [stated: general] The most prized rocks came from Lake Tai, twisted by water erosion, and were valued for four qualities: thinness, openness, perforation and wrinkling [stated: St Olaf, Chinese Scholar's Studio]. Ours (L2–L3, built): each stone waisted and lumpy, its section flattened (thin), its axis wandering, furrowed in folds that wind up it (wrinkled), its top rounded; on the hills some tall stones are built of two legs leaning together under a cap, so that one sees through them (open) [inferred; true perforations are not modelled].
- **Houses:** granite plinths; whitewashed walls; one or two storeys; plank shutters and lattice fronts; a pent eave over the ground floor; stepped "horse-head" gable walls on some; steps down into the canals for boats and washing. [stated: the houses of Pingjiang Road — "whitewashed houses with grey-tile roofs"; details inferred]
- **Zigzag bridges in the garden (L3, built).** Long granite slabs from pier to pier, a long span carried on a pier at its middle; at each turn the slabs stop short and a landing slab — the pier's cap — fills the corner, its outer corner cut off; piers of two courses of blocks standing in the water, the lower a little wider; on the narrow bridges a low rail of square stone posts with low pyramid heads and a rail along them, open below, broken at the ends; the wide decks under the water pavilion and the covered bridge have none [inferred: the zigzag slab bridges of the Suzhou gardens; whether the Ming bridges of this garden had rails is not known to us, many surviving garden bridges have low ones or none].
- **Rockery paths (L3, built).** The islands' hills are climbed by paths among piled Taihu stone: stepping slabs of stone, each a step up, from the zigzag bridges' landings to the hill pavilions (Snowy Fragrance on the west island, Waiting for Frost from both sides on the east); stones from knee height to twice a man's crowd in on both sides, leaning away from the path, gaps between them to see out; on the way up to Snowy Fragrance a short grotto, a long stone lying across on two tall ones with another heaped on it, high enough to walk under. Suzhou's rockeries were walked through: at the Lion Grove (begun 1342) "stone paths connecting the grottos and caves, which are located high and low over the mountain, form a labyrinth" [stated: Terebess, quoting *Classical Chinese Gardens*]; the hills of the Humble Administrator's Garden are earthen, set with stone [inferred: their present form; our paths are a modest version of the Lion Grove's manner, scaled to these hills].
- **Bridges:** humpbacked granite bridges with a semicircular arch and stepped treads over the canals; flat slab bridges in the garden. The canal bridges (L3, built): the arch ring of voussoirs standing a little proud of the spandrels, parapet slabs whose tops run in one smooth curve over the hump with a rounded coping, square posts with round caps at the ends and over the arch's springing [inferred: the humpbacked bridges of the Suzhou canals and water towns]. Where the deck would be too thin over a full half circle, the arch is flattened a little (an elliptical arch) so that its crown stays half a metre under the treads [our concession, not attested].
- **Boats:** wooden boats with a bamboo-mat awning, rowed with a stern oar (yuloh). [inferred from the period's paintings] Ours (L3, built, the boats tied up at the landings): a flat-bottomed planked hull, its sheer rising to a square bow and stern closed by transom boards, a wale along each side, floor boards inside, decks of boards fore and aft, the awning in two hoods of matting on bamboo hoops (the fore hood sliding under the after one), the sculling oar on its pin at the stern, a punting pole along the deck, a rope from the bow post to the ring of the landing's mooring stone [inferred: Jiangnan canal boats as the scrolls and the water towns show them].

## 6. Ground and water

Streets level at 0; the garden 0.15 m above; canal water 0.75 m below the street over a bed 2.6 m down, between granite walls; the pond 0.35 m below the garden over a bed 1.6 m down; islands rising 2.6–3.4 m; rockery mounds 1.2–2.2 m. The canals and the pond cannot be waded; bridges cross them. (`ground.js`)

**The canal banks (L3, built).** The canal walls are of coursed granite under a coping of long slabs laid end to end, overhanging a little; at the waterline the stone is darker and wetter in a ragged band; mooring stones are set in the face every 6–12 m, each a block with a carved stone ring for the boats' ropes. Every 25–40 m on the street side a landing (*he bu tou*): a flight of granite steps laid along the wall from the street down to a slab just above the water, or two flights meeting at a slab in the middle, a mooring stone in the wall over the slab; at about half of them a boat is tied up. A water town's survey counts 256 landings and over 300 mooring stones along its river, the stones carved as bats, ruyi sceptres, lions and other figures [stated: Sohu, on a Jiangnan water town's river]; at Zhujiajiao a landing has two flights of six steps descending north and south to a platform of three stone slabs, with a mooring stone carved in its face (a vase with three halberds, the rebus for rising three ranks) [stated: Zhujiajiao]. Our mooring stones carry a plain ring; the carved figures are not modelled [inferred]. At the public landings with no boat tied up, one or two women squat on the bottom slab rinsing washing, a basket beside them, and now and then a neighbour waits at the top of the steps with hers [inferred: the washing at the canal steps of the water towns, as at the houses' own steps (below)].

**The life of the canals and streets (L3, built).** Qiu Ying, who lived in Suzhou, painted his version of *Along the River during the Qingming Festival* with Ming customs, costumes, boats and carts in place of the Song ones [stated: Wikipedia]; the Liaoning version is called an encyclopedia of Suzhou's urban life in the Ming, with busy markets, workshops side by side and over 2,200 figures [stated: iLiaoning, 2026]. Ours, after that picture and the town as it survives: shops with cloth banners on brackets, read along the street, pairs of paper lanterns under the eaves, goods on the counters (jars, baskets, sacks), shopkeepers and customers; tea tables set out in the street with people at them; washing on bamboo poles pushed out of the upper windows over the water; waterside houses with their own steps down to the canal, women washing at the bottom; boats with the boatman at the stern's sculling oar, carrying jars, sacks or baskets, or a passenger; people along the streets with shoulder poles and baskets, knots talking, dogs, hens, wheelbarrows; benches on the canal side with old men sitting [inferred: the scenes common to the Suzhou scrolls and Jiangnan canal towns].

**Houses that can be entered (L3, built).** The two shops and two homes nearest the way in by the south street are open (DESIGN §4.3): a floor of square grey bricks, the upper floor's boards and joists overhead. A shop: the counter with a way in at one end, an abacus and a steelyard on it, a stool behind, shelves of glazed jars and bolts of cloth along the back, a paper lantern. A home: the door leaf folded open; the hall's long table against the back wall with an incense burner and a vase under a hanging scroll, the square table between two armchairs before it, a tall cabinet, a washstand and stools, a canopied bed with its curtains half drawn behind a screen [inferred: the Ming hall's furniture as surviving Suzhou houses and Ming furniture show it].

## 7. Planting

Willows along the canals and the pond; on the islands and hills pines, maples and flowering fruit trees (plum, standing in for the garden's plums and crab-apples); bamboo by the garden walls; holm oak and camphor; loquats in their court; lotus in the pond north of the main hall and in the bays; moss and grass under the trees. [stated: lotus, bamboo, willow, loquat court — present garden; species stand-ins from our plant library are named in `ground.js`]

## 8. Sky and weather

Late spring, the plum rains near: soft hazy sun, cloud, drizzle, mist on the water. Latitude 31° N. [inferred]

## 9. Sound

In the garden, slow pentatonic phrases on a plucked zither (the qin, played here by our koto voice) and the end-blown flute; in the halls a voice in the soft Kunqu manner — Kunqu arose in the Suzhou region in the Ming — with the pipa; in the streets the pipa of the storytellers. [stated: Kunqu's origin near Suzhou is general knowledge; the instrumentation is inferred]

## 11. The halls furnished, boards and couplets, penjing, damp (L3)

Built in `src/settlements/ming-suzhou/style.js` (the style `suzhou-site`, the Jiangnan vocabulary plus these pieces).

- **A scholar's study.** Wen Zhenheng (1585–1645, Wen Zhengming's great-grandson), *Treatise on Superfluous Things* (*Zhangwu zhi*): a study that is also a sitting room "should contain no more than four chairs and a couch-bed"; in a small room the writing desk stands free, with "brushes, incense powder boxes, and incense burners"; shelves and cabinets keep books and paintings in order, "there should not be too many [shelves]; otherwise, the room looks like a bookstore"; in small rooms no painting is hung, elsewhere one to a room; flowers and incense burners are kept apart, as smoke harms plants; on the sitting table books, albums, paperweights, inkstones, brushes and water holders [stated: Pagoda Red, *Wen Zhenheng: Ming Dynasty Minimalist*]; the scholar's objects — inkstone, ink rest, water dropper, brush washer, paperweight, incense burner, vase — "formulated the scenario of literati's life" [stated: National Palace Museum, *Literati's Ordinaries*]; scholar's rocks valued for thinness, openness, perforation and wrinkling (§5). Ours, in the small hall of the residence's west court (x −34…−22, z 30…36, the door east): the couch-bed against the back wall with its three railed sides, a reed mat, the low table on it and a qin laid there (black lacquer, seven strings); the painting table across the room with the armchair behind it, on it the inkstone and ink stick, a brush rest with three brushes, a bamboo brush pot with brushes, a white water dropper, two jade paperweights on a sheet of paper, a stack of thread-bound books in their blue case; an open bookshelf of three shelves with books lying in cases and two lacquer boxes; on a high table the scholar's rock on its wooden stand and a white vase with a branch of plum in flower; the incense burner on its own small stand by the door, away from the flowers; two armchairs and a tea stand for a guest — four chairs in all [inferred: the arrangement within Wen's rules; no painting hung, as he asks of small rooms].
- **Halls set for guests.** The Hall of Distant Fragrance, the residence's main hall and the Exquisite Jade hall: against the back wall a long table with turned-up ends (qiaotou'an), on it a great rock in its stand and a pair of blue vases; down each side a row of southern official's hat armchairs facing across, a tea stand between each pair [inferred: the symmetrical hall arrangement of surviving Suzhou halls].
- **Furniture.** Ours in dark chestnut lacquer (standing in for the huanghuali and other hardwoods of Ming Suzhou), round legs and stretchers, waisted aprons; the armchairs with their round posts running up into the back, curved arms and the yoke rail [inferred].
- **Inscribed boards and couplets.** The garden's buildings are named after verses; an inscription by Wang Ji hangs at the Pavilion of Snowy Fragrance and Azure Cloud [stated: Wikipedia, Humble Administrator's Garden]. Ours: a horizontal board under the eaves over the doorway of the Hall of Distant Fragrance (north and south), the Leaning Jade Pavilion, the Exquisite Jade hall, the residence's main hall and the study — a black lacquer frame round a wooden field, the characters raised in malachite green; on the two columns of the Hall of Distant Fragrance's doorway a couplet on black boards in gilt. The characters are generic strokes in the proportions of characters, not real inscriptions [inferred: the green-on-wood boards of the Suzhou gardens; the inscriptions themselves not modelled].
- **Penjing.** The garden holds over 700 Suzhou-style penjing [stated: Wikipedia — of the present garden]. Ours: nine on granite stands — miniature pines with twisted trunks and cloud-like pads in blue glazed basins (with a small stone), and stones standing in white water basins — along the loquat court, either side of the path before the Hall of Distant Fragrance and in the study's court [inferred: their number in 1533 is not known; placing ours].
- **Damp.** Late spring and the plum rains: the climate's dampness set high (0.85), so the white walls carry rain streaks and the shaded feet of walls and the north-facing roofs moss [inferred].

## 12. North of the garden: the Bao'en Temple pagoda and the city wall (L3)

Code: `src/settlements/ming-suzhou/north.js` (style `suzhou-north`, built on `ming` with the `song` octagonal pagoda). Placed north of the garden, in the free ground between the north lane (z -108) and the disc's edge.

**The pagoda of the Bao'en Temple (Beisi Ta, the North Temple Pagoda), at (-40, -145).**
- History and form. A pagoda stood on the site from the 3rd century (Sun Quan, for his wet nurse), and a Liang one after it; the present design dates from the Southern Song rebuilding led by the monk Dayuan (1131-1162; the 1153 date of the brief is inferred from the usual dating, the source gives the span); it burned at the end of the Song and was rebuilt in the Ming; it is octagonal, nine storeys (originally eleven), 76 m high; the base and the walls are brick, the balustrades stone, the eaves and banisters wood; the double eaves and flying corners resemble those of the Liuhe Pagoda in Hangzhou [stated: Wikipedia, Beisi Pagoda, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beisi_Pagoda]. The timber galleries of the c. 1530 pagoda are [inferred] the Ming rebuilt ones (the standing outer galleries were rebuilt in 1582, a date not in the source we read, so we show the Ming form generally, not a dated state).
- Ours. A brick body ('brick_grey') of nine tiers, core radius 6.2 m at the foot, 2.84 m at the top, tiers 9.2 m high at the foot to 5.2 m at the top; at every storey a timber gallery (posts, railing) and a double bracketed eave of dark tiles with the corners turned up and an iron wind bell under each corner; doors on alternate faces, windows between; a crowning roof and a gilt finial (taca) 5.6 m high; total 76.7 m [stated for the height, 76 m; the taper and proportions are inferred from the Liuhe Pagoda (song-hangzhou) and the surviving pagoda's silhouette].
- The terrace [inferred: Chinese temple pagodas stand on a stepped stone base]: three octagonal stages of dressed granite (radius 15, 12.4 and 11 m), granite steps on the south to each, a stone balustrade (posts with caps, panels, a top rail) round the middle stage.

**The temple court** (x -76...-4, z -159.5...-111.8) [inferred layout: a temple of the south-facing axis, the gate on the south, the hall before the pagoda; Bao'en Temple is a Buddhist temple of long standing, its Ming halls are not documented in our sources]. A walled court with a gate (a framed doorway with a plaque) in the south wall on the pagoda's axis, a paved court, the main hall (five bays, hip-and-gable roof on a 1.15 m platform with steps) between the gate and the pagoda, and two side halls facing the axis.

**The city wall** (a stretch along z -168, x -118...118; the wall of Suzhou continues beyond the disc). The Wu city wall dates from 514 BC and the surviving walls were largely rebuilt in the early Qing (about 1662) over the Ming ones [stated: Wikipedia, Suzhou City Wall, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzhou_City_Wall]; the Pan Gate has a land gate for the road and a water gate for the canal side by side [stated: Wikipedia, Pan Gate, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Gate]. The dimensions are [inferred] from Ming city walls generally: 9 m high, 9.4 m thick at the foot battered to 7 m at the wall-walk, brick over a granite plinth with granite string courses, an outer parapet 0.95 m high carrying merlons (1.35 m wide, every 2.25 m, tiled caps, a loophole in each), a low inner parapet, drains omitted. Gates: the north gate (Qi Gate, 齐门, the wall's north gate in Suzhou) on the road from the town at x 17 with a 17 m gate hall (xieshan roof) on the wall; on each of the two canal streets a land gate and, beside it, a water gate where the canal passes under the wall (an iron grille raised in the arch), each a round arch ringed by voussoirs, the door leaves swung back; five bastions (mamian) project from the outer face, the two at the ends with a turret hall; two ramps (mading) climb the inner face; the moat is a stone-edged pool along the foot of the outer face with a stone arch bridge on the north gate's road, and the canals run on under the water gates for as far as the ground's channel holds. Limits: the ground's canal channel fades out within a few metres of the wall, so the moat is a raised pool at street level, not a cut one; the wall ends at the disc's edge in end bastions.
