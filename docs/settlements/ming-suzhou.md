## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Humble Administrator's Garden (Zhuozheng Yuan, 拙政园), Suzhou. Laid out between 1509 and 1513 by the retired censor Wang Xianchen on the site of the burnt Dahong Temple, with his friend the painter Wen Zhengming. [stated: Wikipedia; J-STAGE 2013]
- **Moment shown:** 1533, when Wen Zhengming painted 31 views of the garden, each with a poem and a note, and wrote his *Record of the Humble Administrator's Garden*. [stated: Wikipedia; University of Colorado image record] Later rebuildings (Qing; the 1950s restoration of the present three-part garden) are left out: we show the central garden only, as its Ming core. [inferred: the Ming garden's buildings were fewer and plainer than today's; we keep today's positions of those halls that the 1533 album names or that stand on the Ming ponds, and leave out the Qing western garden's halls]
- **Townscape:** the canal quarter of Suzhou east and south of the garden — Pingjiang Road and its lanes, which keep the street pattern shown on the *Pingjiang Tu* stone map of 1229: canals with a street running beside each ("water and land side by side"), stone bridges, whitewashed houses on the water. [stated: Wikipedia, Pingjiang Road; Suzhou government]

## 5. The vocabulary of the style (Jiangnan, Ming)

- **Columns:** slender, round, lacquered dark chestnut, on drum-shaped stone bases. [stated: general for Suzhou gardens; colour inferred from surviving Ming halls of the region]
- **Walls:** white lime plaster with a grey brick plinth; garden walls 4–5 m high capped with a little roof of black tiles; moon gates; latticed "leaky windows" (lou chuang) in the walls of the galleries and courts. [stated: the features of Suzhou gardens — Wikipedia, Classical Gardens of Suzhou]
- **Leaky windows (L3, built).** Leaky windows in partition walls came in with the Ming, when the Jiangnan gardens began to divide their space with inner walls and galleries; the late-Ming *Yuan Ye* calls such walls "leaking brick walls" and says the ancients prized the lozenge pattern while the moderns liked the willow-leaf; most frames are square or oblong, others polygons, circles and fans, freer ones the begonia (crab-apple) flower; the geometric patterns use straight lines, arcs or both — eight-direction, swastika fret, tortoise shell, coin — often combined, e.g. cracked ice with plum; the regular ones are built of flat bricks for short straight lines, pan tiles for arcs, round tiles for small circles, wood for long lines, all set in lime mortar [stated: People's Daily, 2024]. Patterns are geometric, natural, objects and figures [stated: China Discovery]. Ours: frames of square, octagon, hexagon, circle, fan and begonia outlines, each filled with one of four traceries — lozenge, coin, cracked ice (every crack ending on another), tortoise shell — in grey plastered tile and brick, inside a frame of dressed grey brick standing proud of the plaster [the frame's material inferred: the grey brick surrounds seen on the gardens' walls]. Ming walls hold the earlier, simpler geometric kinds; the pictorial kinds are mostly Qing [inferred from the People's Daily account of Qing multiplication].
- **Gateways (L3, built).** Moon gates and doorways lined and framed with dressed grey brick; over the gates a framed brick plaque for the gate's name (*men e*), its field left plain [inferred: the brick-framed openings and inscribed plaques of the surviving gardens; the inscriptions are not modelled].
- **Wall copings (L3, built).** The garden and court walls end in a little double-pitched roof of black tiles laid on a course of grey brick corbelled out from each face; the tiles show their thickness at the eaves (a row of eave ends) and the overhang has an underside, so that one passes under it at a gate [inferred: the tiled copings of the Suzhou garden walls as they survive; the corbelled course from the brick cornices under them]. The plinth of grey brick stops at each gate (a door's threshold is the paving's own) [inferred].
- **Hanging fretwork (gua luo), L3, built.** Between the posts of the galleries and pavilions, under the beam, an openwork band of slender dark bars: a top rail, a bottom rail, between them a square wave stepping up and down the band (our reading of the "step by step brocade", *bu bu jin*), tied to the rails, and at each end a leg dropping below the band with a small bracket in the angle [inferred: the gua luo of the Suzhou gardens' galleries and pavilions; the pattern simplified to one fret].
- **Galleries (L3, built).** Under the roof the frame shows: a tie beam across at each pair of posts, short struts on it under two purlins, a taller one under the ridge purlin; on the walled side a leaky window in every third bay, each of its own outline and tracery, so that the views change as one walks [inferred: the wall-side galleries of the garden; the galleries' winding, "up and down, turning and twisting", stated for Suzhou galleries — Terebess, *Classical Chinese Gardens*]. Where two runs meet at a turn the floors overlap (one 3 mm over the other, not coplanar).
- **Doors and windows (L3, built):** tall lattice doors (ge shan, *chang chuang*) between the columns; the foot of each leaf a dado panel (*qun ban*): a frame of stiles and rails round a recessed board with a raised medallion, then a rail, the lattice over paper, a rail above. A hall's window bays are the same lattice over a plastered sill wall; a plastered wall may hold a latticed window with the paper set back in the wall's thickness. The paper is a lighter warm tone and glows a little (more at night) so that the rooms behind do not read as black. [inferred: the doors' division into lattice above and a panelled *qun ban* below is general to Jiangnan halls; the medallion is our reading of the carved panel]
- **Lattice patterns (L3, built).** The Suzhou patterns, each a shader pattern on its own palette entry: *bing lie* cracked ice (a Worley net of irregular cells; "one of the most popular designs, especially in the Suzhou area" — chinesenotes.com, decorative designs in wood architecture); *bu bu jin* step-by-step brocade (upright bars every cell, rails every third row, and a rail stepping up and down one cell per column); *deng long kuang* the lantern frame (upright bars, the rails bunched at the top and foot of each frame); *wan zi* the swastika fret (each tile's arms turning to the corners and meeting the neighbours', so that the fret never ends); *hai tang* the crab-apple (rings on the cell centres and corners overlapping into four-lobed flowers). The patterns at the Suzhou gardens include ice-crack, swastika, lantern and step-by-step (chinese-showcase.com, traditional Chinese window lattice). [stated: that these patterns are used in Suzhou lattices; inferred: their exact geometry, which we reduce to what reads at the size of a door leaf]
- **Platforms (L3, built).** A hall stands on a platform (*tai ji*) of granite: a core under a bluestone floor, its top edge a course of long kerb slabs (*jie tiao shi*, with open joints), a corner stone (*jiao shi*) standing a little proud at each corner, and steps (*ta duo*) of stacked slabs, risers of about 17 cm, in front of every doorway, the kerb left open there and the floor running out to the edge. [inferred: the parts of a Chinese platform — edge stones, corner stones, steps — are general; Suzhou halls' low platforms are of granite or bluestone]
- **Walls of halls (L3, built).** Plaster over a skirt of grey brick, or timber boards with battens over the joints, with a window only where the bay is wide. [inferred]
- **Gable ends (L3, built).** At each gable of a hip-and-gable (*xieshan*) roof, and of a gabled hall's overhang, the plastered triangle has a bargeboard (*bo feng ban*) of dark timber 40 cm deep following the concave slope, a roll of tiles along the verge, the round ends of the purlins standing out of the board, and at the apex the hanging fish (*xuan yu*), a carved board of a fish's outline, its head under the bargeboards' meeting and its tail swallow-forked below. The main ridge runs to the gable lines. [inferred: bargeboards and the hanging fish ornament the gables of Chinese halls, the fish common on gabled buildings of Jiangnan; the fish's outline is our reading; the pages we tried for a source (zh.wikipedia on *xuan yu*) were blocked, so this is from general knowledge]
- **Beauty's rest (L3, built).** Where a gallery or pavilion looks on the water, the bench is the *mei ren kao* (also *Wu Wang kao*, "King of Wu's rest"): a seat plank on a low plastered wall, behind it a back of slender uprights curving out in an ogee over the water (their tops 1 m above the floor), a crest rail on top, a middle rail and a foot rail; elsewhere a plain bench, the seat on its low wall with no back. The galleries' bench follows the open side's lean and breaks where paths leave. [inferred: the curved outward-leaning back, set at the edge of galleries and pavilions over water, is how the beauty's rest is described in general accounts of Suzhou garden architecture; the zh.wikipedia page was blocked]
- **The Fragrant Isle (L3, rebuilt).** The hull is an outline of stone: a square stern against the shore, parallel sides, and the bow rounded in an ellipse over its last 4.2 m, the sides leaning in toward the keel, a bluestone deck and a granite coaming round its edge; the bow pavilion, the middle cabin and the two-storey stern stand on the deck itself, inside the hull's width and with doorways aligned from stern to bow. [inferred: the stone boat (*shi fang*) form of the real Xiangzhou; dimensions ours]
- **Roofs:** small black tiles on a strongly concave profile; the corners swept high (*fa qiang*, the Jiangnan "flying corner"); the main ridge ending in curls; hip-and-gable (*xieshan*) for halls, pyramids for pavilions, gables for houses. [stated: general]
- **Rock:** Taihu stone (holed, eroded limestone) heaped along the shores and piled into hills and single standing stones. [stated: general] The most prized rocks came from Lake Tai, twisted by water erosion, and were valued for four qualities: thinness, openness, perforation and wrinkling [stated: St Olaf, Chinese Scholar's Studio]. Ours (first version, L2; replaced by the carved stones below): each stone waisted and lumpy, its section flattened (thin), its axis wandering, furrowed in folds that wind up it (wrinkled), its top rounded; on the hills some tall stones are built of two legs leaning together under a cap, so that one sees through them (open) [inferred; true perforations are not modelled].
- **Houses:** granite plinths; whitewashed walls; one or two storeys; plank shutters and lattice fronts; a pent eave over the ground floor; stepped "horse-head" gable walls on some; steps down into the canals for boats and washing. [stated: the houses of Pingjiang Road — "whitewashed houses with grey-tile roofs"; details inferred]
- **The canal quarter's houses (L3, built; `houseJiangnan`, `layout.js` lots).** Suzhou houses are "usually not wide and have a deep interior", their scale varying with the owner's wealth; the court (patio) is the basic unit, a typical house having a front section with the gate, a patio and a rear main hall, the large ones several or dozens of courts along axes; many combine home and business ("front shop and back house", "lower shop and upper house"); exterior walls white, timber mostly natural, doors and windows light brown or claret, roofs grey; big eaves and overhanging gables shade and keep out rain [stated: *The Charm of Suzhou's Traditional Folk Houses*, China Story]. Streets and lanes run in front of houses with a river behind, the wider the land between street and river the larger the house [stated: same]. Ours, after that and the Pingjiang Road survey: lots of one to four bays (3.4–15 m) and of differing depth; a shallow lot a single hall; a deep one a front hall, a brick court (some with a tree) and a rear hall, with walls between (two *jin*); the richest (about a dozen) three *jin* behind a gate wall with a **gate tower (menlou)**: two grey-brick piers, a granite lintel, a frieze band of carved-brick panels, black lacquered leaves with gilt studs, a tiled hip roof on brackets, a lantern at each side; ground floors 2.95–3.35 m and upper floors 2.45–2.85 m, so that eaves stand at differing heights; one and two storeys, a few halls higher (the rich hall 4.4 m to the eaves). *Roofs:* plain gables with their rake overhanging; **horse-head walls (mǎtóu qiáng)** on a third — stepped walls of two or three steps standing on the end walls, each step capped with tiles and the lowest ending in an upturned tile; the **Guanyin-hood (guānyīn dōu)** gable on a tenth of them (wide houses only) — a smooth arch of plaster rising over the roof, its coping of tiles; **hipped roofs** on the houses at street corners and lane ends and on some others; the ridge ends turned up. [inferred: the proportions and the share of each type; the types themselves are the ones named for Jiangnan and Huizhou walls (horse-head walls fire-stop the row; the hood gables belong to Suzhou's Taihu-side houses); no census of the 1530s exists]
- **Fronts (L3, built).** *Shop streets:* a row of lacquered posts and a beam, between them in each bay a dark backing and plank shutters (*pái mén bǎn*, boards 0.29 m wide slotted into a sill) — in some bays taken down, a counter in the opening with the goods on it and the boards stacked beside — and a black shop board with gilt strokes under the beam; *lanes:* a plaster wall, the door between granite jambs under a little tiled hood on two posts, one or two small high windows with lattice and stone sills; *upper floors:* lattice windows of the existing lattice materials (plain, cracked-ice, plain boards), each in its frame with a sill, about one in three with its paper lit from within at night (the glow material lamp_white behind the lattice), some with a bamboo blind rolled half down, now and then a bamboo pole on two brackets with cloths hanging. The upper floor either stands back behind a pent eave over the ground floor (*yāo yán*) or, on about a fifth of the houses and half the shops, juts out over the street on beam ends. [inferred: from the scrolls and surviving Pingjiang Road and Shantang Street fronts; the plank shutters (paimen ban) are the standard shop front of Jiangnan towns]
- **Over the canal (L3, built).** On the waterside rows about two in five of the two-storey houses have a **veranda on stilts (shuǐ gé, diào jiǎo lóu)**: a deck on posts standing in the canal down to its bed, a rail of lacquered posts with a lattice panel, a tiled pent roof on four posts; another fifth a balcony on brackets from the upper floor. [inferred: the water pavilions and hanging-foot buildings of Jiangnan water towns]
- **Wear (L3, built).** The plinth in two courses of granite, the upper one a little proud; the lower 0.95 m of every wall in the grey of damp (plaster_damp), with a few ragged tongues of it up the lane fronts; a rain-streaked band under the eaves and drips below it; patches of bare brick and of a different plaster on a fifth of the houses; the plaster itself in four tones (clean white, older grey, patched, damp-grey); roofs in two tones of tile (the older, greyer on about one in five) with a few slipped tiles and bare patches on each slope. [inferred]
- **Edge of the quarter (L3, built).** The quarter does not end at a ring of grass: the streets continue to the rim as lanes 2.4 m wide between rows of houses (the back streets east, west and south of the canal rows, lanes every 26 m, cross alleys between), the canals run on to 200 m; the houses thin out and drop to one storey from 150 m, the outermost lots become walled yards with a tree rising over the wall and a gate, and from 105 m (the east, west and south districts, the far north) they are cheaper, single-hall houses — the same lots, types and roofs, less detail. [inferred: the dense urban fabric of a Ming city beyond the garden quarter; the plan of the outer districts is ours, the 1229 Pingjiang map shows a grid of parallel canals and streets (§6)]
- **Zigzag bridges in the garden (L3, built).** Long granite slabs from pier to pier, a long span carried on a pier at its middle; at each turn the slabs stop short and a landing slab — the pier's cap — fills the corner, its outer corner cut off; piers of two courses of blocks standing in the water, the lower a little wider; on the narrow bridges a low rail of square stone posts with low pyramid heads and a rail along them, open below, broken at the ends; the wide decks under the water pavilion and the covered bridge have none [inferred: the zigzag slab bridges of the Suzhou gardens; whether the Ming bridges of this garden had rails is not known to us, many surviving garden bridges have low ones or none].
- **Rockery paths (L3, built).** The islands' hills are climbed by paths among piled Taihu stone: stepping slabs of stone, each a step up, from the zigzag bridges' landings to the hill pavilions (Snowy Fragrance on the west island, Waiting for Frost from both sides on the east); stones from knee height to twice a man's crowd in on both sides, leaning away from the path, gaps between them to see out; on the way up to Snowy Fragrance a short grotto, a long stone lying across on two tall ones with another heaped on it, high enough to walk under. Suzhou's rockeries were walked through: at the Lion Grove (begun 1342) "stone paths connecting the grottos and caves, which are located high and low over the mountain, form a labyrinth" [stated: Terebess, quoting *Classical Chinese Gardens*]; the hills of the Humble Administrator's Garden are earthen, set with stone [inferred: their present form; our paths are a modest version of the Lion Grove's manner, scaled to these hills].
- **Taihu stone, rebuilt (L3, built).** Lake Tai rocks were the most prized garden stones: limestone eroded irregularly by the lake, valued for spareness and delicacy with a rugged presence, holes passing right through and strangely twisted forms, set as solitary specimens, in rockeries and along the edges of ponds and streams [stated: Univ. of Washington, *Rocks and Mountains*; St Olaf (above)]. Ours is no longer a lumpy lathe but a carved solid: each stone is a distance field (two to three leaning peaks, a waisted, wrinkled body only 0.4–0.6 m thick, folds winding up it) from which 3–10 tunnels (0.1–0.26 m radius, bored mostly through the thin way, some across a neck) and 3–7 hollows are subtracted, so the holes are true openings one sees through (*lou*, *tou*), the surface mesh extracted by surface nets; the pale body is lightened Taihu grey and the walls of the holes and hollows are the darker stone material, as water-worn hollows read dark [inferred: the look of surviving Suzhou stones; the shapes are ours]. Fifteen shapes (tall spires and peaks 2–3.5 m, twin peaks, low pierced piles, knobs, a slab, a natural arch, the lintel of the grotto), each at two levels of detail (near paths 5–7 k triangles, far 1–3 k), placed as instances so that the whole garden's ~200 stones cost about a dozen meshes. Standing stones on rough stone feet (*zuo*) flank the terrace of the Hall of Distant Fragrance, stand in the loquat court's corner and in the residence's side courts (a stone set alone in a court is the usual form: the Garden of the Master of Nets even gives one its own pavilion) [inferred: placements]; the hills' peaks stand at their tops; along the shores single stones and pairs stand every 3–10 m, some in the water, none on a path or at a bridge.
- **The revetment (*bo an*, L3, built).** Rocks lined the garden ponds, "arranged with great care so they would look natural" (above). Ours: along the whole shore of the pond and of every island, two courses of rubble — a lower course standing in the shallows, its tops 0.0–0.2 m over the water, and an upper course on the bank, its tops uneven, 0.05–0.3 m over the grass — blocks 0.5–1.25 m long, rounded (superellipsoid blocks with bumped surface, sunk 0.3 m, tilted, smooth normals), dark joints (a backing ribbon) and small stones wedged in the gaps, set along the shore's line and following it; the bank itself climbs from the water to the garden's level within a metre and a half (`ground.js`), so that the revetment is a stone face and nowhere does turf simply touch the water. It stops at the bridges' landings, the buildings on piers and the rockery paths, where the landing slabs and steps are the edge. Of yellowstone (*huangshi*) and grey Taihu among them: no yellowstone material exists in the palette, so a warm grey-brown rock stands in for it, with grey granite and Taihu stone mixed in [inferred: Suzhou ponds are edged with irregular rubble of yellowstone and Taihu stone; the courses and heights ours].
- **Lotus and water lilies (L3, built).** The lotus of the pond is the garden's old subject (the Hall of Distant Fragrance is named for it) [stated: §4]. Ours: in clumps 1–3 m across with open water between (north of the hall, in the west and east bays and the inlet), each of 10–30 leaves — cupped leaves on stalks standing 0.3–1.2 m above the water, tilted every way, and flatter leaves floating, rippled at the edge, in four sizes — with now and then a flower (7 and 5 petals in two rings, a golden receptacle with stamens), a bud, or a seed pod (an inverted cone, flat topped, dotted with holes) on a stalk 0.7–1.45 m high. In the smaller bays, water lilies: small round leaves with a wedge cut out, lying flat, with a few white star flowers [inferred: the lotus's habit; the flowers' pink is the nearest palette material, *marble_pink*].
- **Pebble-mosaic paving (*hua jie pu di*, L3, built).** The courts and paths of Suzhou gardens are paved in patterns of pebbles, roof tiles set on edge and shards of porcelain; the Huntington's Liu Fang Yuan, built by Suzhou craftsmen, has twelve such patterns of clay tile, limestone shards and river rocks of various colours [stated: Huntington / ArcGIS StoryMaps, *The Paving Patterns of Liu Fang Yuan*]; the usual figures are coins, plum blossoms, lotus, cranes and tortoise-shell hexagons [inferred: general knowledge of the craft; the pages fetched do not name them]. Ours: courts with a border of dark tile edges and a band of pale pebbles round a bed of pebbles, a field of one repeating figure (honeycomb hexagons, some pale with a turquoise porcelain centre, some buff; or coins with a square hole and a diamond between), and a medallion within a ring of pale pebbles and tile: a **crane** with the sun in blue porcelain and wisps of cloud in the court behind the Hall of Distant Fragrance (where one arrives) and before the east hall of the residence; a **lotus** (eight long and eight short petals, a golden heart) before the study; a **plum blossom** (five round petals, ochre heart) in the loquat court and the residence's west court. The paths are ribbons of pebbles edged in tile and a pale line, with a coin, plum, lotus or hexagon medallion down the middle every couple of metres. ## 6. Ground and water

Streets level at 0; the garden 0.15 m above; canal water 0.75 m below the street over a bed 2.6 m down, between granite walls; the pond 0.35 m below the garden over a bed 1.6 m down; islands rising 2.6–3.4 m; rockery mounds 1.2–2.2 m. The canals and the pond cannot be waded; bridges cross them. (`ground.js`)

**The canal banks (L3, built).** The canal walls are of coursed granite under a coping of long slabs laid end to end, overhanging a little; at the waterline the stone is darker and wetter in a ragged band; mooring stones are set in the face every 6–12 m, each a block with a carved stone ring for the boats' ropes. Every 25–40 m on the street side a landing (*he bu tou*): a flight of granite steps laid along the wall from the street down to a slab just above the water, or two flights meeting at a slab in the middle, a mooring stone in the wall over the slab; at about half of them a boat is tied up. A water town's survey counts 256 landings and over 300 mooring stones along its river, the stones carved as bats, ruyi sceptres, lions and other figures [stated: Sohu, on a Jiangnan water town's river]; at Zhujiajiao a landing has two flights of six steps descending north and south to a platform of three stone slabs, with a mooring stone carved in its face (a vase with three halberds, the rebus for rising three ranks) [stated: Zhujiajiao]. Our mooring stones carry a plain ring; the carved figures are not modelled [inferred]. At the public landings with no boat tied up, one or two women squat on the bottom slab rinsing washing, a basket beside them, and now and then a neighbour waits at the top of the steps with hers [inferred: the washing at the canal steps of the water towns, as at the houses' own steps (below)].

**The life of the canals and streets (L3, built).** Qiu Ying, who lived in Suzhou, painted his version of *Along the River during the Qingming Festival* with Ming customs, costumes, boats and carts in place of the Song ones [stated: Wikipedia]; the Liaoning version is called an encyclopedia of Suzhou's urban life in the Ming, with busy markets, workshops side by side and over 2,200 figures [stated: iLiaoning, 2026]. Ours, after that picture and the town as it survives: shops with cloth banners on brackets, read along the street, pairs of paper lanterns under the eaves, goods on the counters (jars, baskets, sacks), shopkeepers and customers; tea tables set out in the street with people at them; washing on bamboo poles pushed out of the upper windows over the water; waterside houses with their own steps down to the canal, women washing at the bottom; boats with the boatman at the stern's sculling oar, carrying jars, sacks or baskets, or a passenger; people along the streets with shoulder poles and baskets, knots talking, dogs, hens, wheelbarrows; benches on the canal side with old men sitting [inferred: the scenes common to the Suzhou scrolls and Jiangnan canal towns].

**The moving life (L3, built; `movers.js`, the engine's movers).** Boats are sculled along the canals and people walk the streets, lanes and bridges, near the visitor only (a few dozen walkers within 150 m, eight boats within 250 m), drawn by the engine's movers (`src/engine/movers.js`) from this settlement's data (`src/settlements/ming-suzhou/movers.js`).
- *The boats.* Suzhou's canal boats were moved by the *lu* (the yuloh): a large, heavy sculling oar whose socket sits on a pin at the stern, worked by pushing and pulling on a rope from the handle to the deck, so that the oar rocks side to side on its pivot and its blade angles itself to drive the boat; one oar can move a large heavy boat, and the technique is known from ancient China [stated: Wikipedia, *Stern sculling*, citing Needham]. A yuloh moves a 2–3 tonne sampan at up to 3 knots, slow but very manoeuvrable [stated: Duckworks, *Easy Go yuloh*]. The scroll of 1759, *Prosperous Suzhou*, shows some 400 boats, barges and passenger boats among its 4,800 figures [stated: Wikipedia, *Prosperous Suzhou*; Qing, later than our moment, but the city's water traffic is the same in kind]; boats of the Grand Canal country had a "semi circular lattice work of straw" for an awning on a narrow, flat-bottomed hull with flat bow and stern [stated: Royal Museums Greenwich, model of a *liu-p'eng ch'uan*]. Ours: the covered boat (the ming style's canal boat, its awning of matting in two hoods) and the open cargo boat (the same hull with sacks of rice and baskets), 6.4–7.4 m long and about 2 m in the beam, at 1.0–1.45 m/s [inferred: the yuloh's 3 knots is a top speed; a canal boat goes at a walking pace]. The boatman stands on the stern deck across the boat and rocks forward and back from the ankles, pushing and drawing the oar, whose handle swings about 0.3 m athwartships on the pin while its blade sweeps the other way in the water and turns on each stroke (one stroke in about 1.8 s); the rope runs from the handle to the deck [inferred from the account above; the stroke rate is ours]. Boats keep to their right of the canal's middle, clear of the boats moored at the landings and those standing in the water; slow and keep to the middle under the arch bridges; give way to one another; turn at the two junctions with the south canal; at a canal's end (the rim, the moat beyond the north water gates) they tie up. A faint wake: the bow wave's two arms spreading at the Kelvin angle, foam along the hull, stirred water where the oar works. Some covered boats carry a passenger sitting cross-legged under the awning in robe and square cap.
- *The walkers.* After Qiu Ying's Suzhou scroll and the town as it survives (above): men in long robes, some with the Ming square cap (*fangjin*), some bare-headed with the net-bound topknot; women in long robes with their hair in a bun; labourers in jacket and trousers with straw hats; porters with the shoulder pole (*biandan*) and two baskets, the pole flexing and the baskets swinging on their cords at each step [inferred: the scenes of the scrolls; the figures are the street life's own (src/objects/figures.js), so the walkers match the still people]. Oil-paper umbrellas (bamboo frame, tung-oiled paper) became common in the Ming [stated: Wikipedia, *Oil-paper umbrella*]: in the rain the robed walkers open one (28 ribs, the paper yellow-brown, brown, olive or red-brown) and the labourers, porters and boatmen put on the palm-fibre rain cape (*suoyi*), long familiar in Jiangnan, sleeveless, of pure palm fibre, about 5 kg [stated: Garland, *Suoyi*], over the straw hat [inferred]. The walk: the legs swing from the hips (a robe's skirt bent with them), the foot that swings forward lifted, the hips rising and falling over each step, a little sway, the arms swinging opposite (the left only where the right hand holds a pole or an umbrella); 1.0–1.4 m/s, the porters in shorter steps [inferred]. They keep to the right of their way, slow behind one another, step aside for those coming, go round what stands in the street (tea tables, stalls, the people standing and sitting, benches), turn at junctions and at dead ends, cross the arch bridges over their steps; the market streets are busiest by day (the crowd weighted by the share of shop fronts along each street), and few walk at night or in the rain [inferred].
- *Not yet:* lanterns carried at night; the boats under way that town.js poses standing in mid-canal (`riverLife`, the sampans with a boatman at the oar) are now redundant and sometimes block a stretch the moving boats then stop short of: they could be left out.

A pagoda stood on the site from the 3rd century (Sun Quan, for his wet nurse), and a Liang one after it; the present design dates from the Southern Song rebuilding led by the monk Dayuan (1131-1162; the 1153 date of the brief is inferred from the usual dating, the source gives the span); it burned at the end of the Song and was rebuilt in the Ming; it is octagonal, nine storeys (originally eleven), 76 m high; the base and the walls are brick, the balustrades stone, the eaves and banisters wood; the double eaves and flying corners resemble those of the Liuhe Pagoda in Hangzhou [stated: Wikipedia, Beisi Pagoda, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beisi_Pagoda]. The timber galleries of the c. 1530 pagoda are [inferred] the Ming rebuilt ones (the standing outer galleries were rebuilt in 1582, a date not in the source we read, so we show the Ming form generally, not a dated state).
- Ours. A brick body ('brick_grey') of nine tiers, core radius 6.2 m at the foot, 2.84 m at the top, tiers 9.2 m high at the foot to 5.2 m at the top; at every storey a timber gallery (posts, railing) and a double bracketed eave of dark tiles with the corners turned up and an iron wind bell under each corner; doors on alternate faces, windows between; a crowning roof and a gilt finial (taca) 5.6 m high; total 76.7 m [stated for the height, 76 m; the taper and proportions are inferred from the Liuhe Pagoda (song-hangzhou) and the surviving pagoda's silhouette].
- Eaves [inferred: from the form of Chinese timber-and-brick pagodas, deep bracketed eaves at every storey]: each storey's eave is a thick tiled roof (tile rolls, hip ridges, a drip lip, a fascia board, a soffit with rafter ends under it, corners swept up with an iron wind bell and a cap) under the gallery above; the crowning roof is a steep concave octagonal pyramid with hips. The halls of the temple and the gate tower use a roof whose corner curve is spread over a longer run, with fascia, drip tiles, rafters and hip rafters, and tie beams and purlins under the ceiling of the main hall.
- The terrace [inferred: Chinese temple pagodas stand on a stepped stone base]: three octagonal stages of dressed granite (radius 15, 12.4 and 11 m), granite steps on the south to each, a stone balustrade (posts with caps, panels, a top rail) round the middle stage.

**The temple court** (x -76...-4, z -159.5...-111.8) [inferred layout: a temple of the south-facing axis, the gate on the south, the hall before the pagoda; Bao'en Temple is a Buddhist temple of long standing, its Ming halls are not documented in our sources]. A walled court with a gate (a framed doorway with a plaque) in the south wall on the pagoda's axis, a paved court, the main hall (five bays, hip-and-gable roof on a 1.15 m platform with steps) between the gate and the pagoda, and two side halls facing the axis.

**The city wall** (a stretch along z -168, x -118...118; the wall of Suzhou continues beyond the disc). The Wu city wall dates from 514 BC and the surviving walls were largely rebuilt in the early Qing (about 1662) over the Ming ones [stated: Wikipedia, Suzhou City Wall, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzhou_City_Wall]; the Pan Gate has a land gate for the road and a water gate for the canal side by side [stated: Wikipedia, Pan Gate, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Gate]. The dimensions are [inferred] from Ming city walls generally: 9 m high, 9.4 m thick at the foot battered to 7 m at the wall-walk, brick over a granite plinth with granite string courses, an outer parapet 0.95 m high carrying merlons (1.35 m wide, every 2.25 m, tiled caps, a loophole in each), a low inner parapet, drains omitted. Gates: the north gate (Qi Gate, 齐门, the wall's north gate in Suzhou) on the road from the town at x 17 with a 17 m gate hall (xieshan roof) on the wall; on each of the two canal streets a land gate and, beside it, a water gate where the canal passes under the wall (an iron grille raised in the arch), each a round arch ringed by voussoirs, the door leaves swung back; five bastions (mamian) project from the outer face, the two at the ends with a turret hall; two ramps (mading) climb the inner face; the moat is a stone-edged pool along the foot of the outer face with a stone arch bridge on the north gate's road, and the canals run on under the water gates for as far as the ground's channel holds. Limits: the ground's canal channel fades out within a few metres of the wall, so the moat is a raised pool at street level, not a cut one; the wall ends at the disc's edge in end bastions.

## 13. Smoke, steam and mist (L3)

- **Kitchen smoke.** About a third of the houses (not walled yards) smoke at the three meals, around 06:40, 11:40 and 17:40 (the evening's the strongest); which ones is shuffled at each meal, so a scattered share burns at a time. A Jiangnan town house's kitchen, with its brick stove (*zao*), stood at the back; in the shop-houses the rooms on the river side served "as the kitchen, the bathroom or a warehouse" [stated: China Story, *The Charm of Suzhou's Traditional Folk Houses*, in Sources]. The smoke leaves the roof near the ridge: thin, bluish grey, widening and fading as it rises, bent over by the wind [inferred: the flue/vent form and the meal hours are not in our sources; a roof vent or gable opening is how such houses let wood smoke out].
- **Incense smoke.** At the temple's censer, on the court's axis before the main hall's steps, all day: thin curling threads, slow and light. Censers stand in the main court of Chinese Buddhist temples, between the bell and drum towers and the halls, for burning incense [stated: Temple Tales, *The Main Courtyard*, https://templetales.thetempleguy.org/2019/11/the-main-courtyard.html]. The temple plan has no censer model yet; the smoke's point is `CENSER` in `ming-suzhou/smoke.js` (1.4 m up) [inferred position].
- **Steam.** From a tea urn on the counter of each tea shop, by day (about 06:00-20:00): short white puffs [inferred: shops that sold tea served it hot].
- **Mist.** A faint low mist over the canals at dawn (about 04:30-07:00), banks 6-8 m wide drifting slowly [inferred: a canal town in a humid climate at early morning].

## 14. The sounds of the water, the streets and the temple (L3)

Code: `src/engine/soundscape.js` (the schedule and the sources; `LIFE` in Suzhou's profile), `src/engine/audio.js` (the synthesis). All synthesized with the Web Audio API; no audio files. The engine has no listener position, so nothing is placed in 3D: what is near water is read from the engine's `water` level (the canals and the pond alike), a passing boat or watchman is given a pan that drifts across the stereo field and a distance by a lowpass.

- **Canals.** Water slapping the stone walls: wet slaps of band noise and the hollow glug of a gap in the wall, at random, more often the more water is near. By day, near water, a sculled boat now and then: bouts of 4-8 strokes two seconds apart, each with the creak of the oar's peg (a rough, wavering saw squeal rising on the push, falling on the pull) and the swish of the blade; at longer intervals a boatman's call (a sung "ei-yo, ho" on formant filters, no words). [inferred: the yuloh is a stern oar worked from side to side on a peg, and its creak is the sound of Jiangnan boats; the Wu boat songs and haozi work chants are sung in the region. No Ming recording exists.]
- **Streets, by day.** Hawkers' cries: a sung call on a few notes, vowels only (the flower sellers' "gardenia, magnolia", the sweet-soup sellers', a pedlar's), by a man or a woman; a pedlar's rattle-drum (*bolang gu*) twirled; the bamboo clapper of a snack seller; the soft hubbub of the market streets (three bands of noise swelling at syllable rates, out of step); a teahouse's cups and lid, and now and then the storyteller's block (*xingmu*) slapped on a table. [inferred from the Qingming scrolls' street life (§6) and from later records of peddlers' calls and instruments; Shanghai flower sellers' cry "ZI zi hoo, ba lam hoo": Shanghai Daily, 2018, https://www.pressreader.com/china/shanghai-daily/20180807/281560881613364. The calls are our own tunes.]
- **Night watches (*da geng*).** The night ran in five watches of two hours from about 7 pm (戌 亥 子 丑 寅) [stated: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_timekeeping]; outside the drum-tower cities a watchman announced each with a gong or a clapper of wood or bamboo, calling "the weather is dry, beware of fire" (*tian gan wu zao, xiao xin huo zhu*) [stated: China Daily, 2018, "On patrol with the last traditional night watchman": the gong struck twice at 9 pm, three times at midnight, four times at 3 am, a single stroke at a crossroads]. Ours: at the start of each watch the clapper beats (one, then two quick), as many gong strokes as the watch's number, and the call; between watches, at night and not in rain, a watchman passes in some street with clapper beats and now and then the call. [inferred: the stroke counts and the rhythm; no Ming source for the pattern was found.]
- **The temple.** The Bao'en Temple's bell and drum, *chen zhong mu gu* (morning bell, evening drum): in the morning the bell and then the drum, in the evening the drum and then the bell, then the service with the wooden fish (*muyu*) beating the time of a low chant of a chorus of voices ("na-mo-e-mi-tuo-fo" vowels). The bell is struck 108 times at dawn and dusk, in three runs of 18 (fast, slow, between) twice over [stated: https://www.sohu.com/a/213979662_671412; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_tower_(Chinese_Buddhism)]; the Chan rule had the big bell at the fifth watch wake the monks [stated: https://emptysqua.re/blog/zen-monastery-timekeeping/]. Ours is shortened to 6 + 6 + 6 strokes (a day passes in thirty minutes) at 4:36 and 18:30; the order bell/drum and the chant are [inferred from the saying and Pure Land practice in Jiangnan]. Hanshan's bell at midnight, far off across the water, stays (§9; Zhang Ji's poem [stated]). Heard everywhere in the town: the engine cannot place the chant at the temple hall (see below).
- **Rain.** The small grey tiles (`xiaoqing`, a lighter tick than a heavy fired tile, a quarter of the drops with a hollow tock from the air under the roof) [inferred]; rain pocking the canal (a loop of tiny rising chirps, only near water); drips from the eaves (random plinks, more of them the harder the rain).
- **Left for a listener position.** If the engine gave the audio a listener (`place({ at: {x, z}, yaw })` from `main.js`), the oar and the boatman could be heard from the canal's actual nearest reach (`canalDistance` and `CANALS` of `layout.js`), the watchman from the streets, and the chant and the bell from the temple court (`TEMPLE_COURT`, x -76...-4, z -159.5...-111.8) in place of the town-wide mix; the wooden fish near the hall would then sound only in the court.

## 16. Weathering and rain (materials, L3)

How the town's surfaces age and take the rain, as the shared materials draw them (`src/engine/materials.js`, "Weathering"):

- **White walls are not white.** The limewashed walls of Jiangnan were "an organic co-production of rains, mosses, and lichens"; Ming residents even fed moss and vines on their walls with rice porridge because a weathered look was preferred, and early accounts describe grey and even black walls [stated: CCA, *White Walls and Black Tiles*]. High humidity, heavy rain and long sun degrade the plastered walls of the water towns; damage follows the nearness of water and vegetation, orientation and layout [stated: Chen et al. 2024, Anchang, Shaoxing]. Drawn: dark-grey rain streaks down the faces, strongest on the white plaster, starting and stopping at random heights (where copings, eaves and sills shed the water) [inferred]; moss higher up the north faces [inferred].
- **The foot of the walls.** Rising damp leaves a distinctive tide mark where salts are deposited at its evaporating edge, a flour-like dusting or crystalline blooms [stated: Building Conservation, *Rising Damp*; Homebuilding, *salt banding*: the line close to ground level that shows how high the damp has reached]. Drawn: the foot darker up to a wavering tide line 0.3–0.7 m up, a thin pale line of salts on it, the grime of the lower metre, and green-black algae and moss in the damp foot, thickest on the north side and in narrow lanes where little sky reaches [inferred: the heights].
- **The waterline.** Canal walls and revetments standing in still water carry black-green algae and silt from below the surface to 0.3–0.6 m above it (the water rises and falls with the rains), a paler band above where its lime and salts dried [inferred, from the water towns as photographed]. The level is the canals' (−0.75 m); the pond (−0.35 m) shows the upper part of the same band.
- **Wear.** The tops of paving, kerbs and steps are worn smooth in patches and along the paths: a little lighter, glossier, the joints shallower and the arrises rounder [inferred].
- **Rain.** A wet porous surface is darker because the water filling its pores and coating it reduces the contrast of refractive index, so more light is scattered forward into the material and absorbed (Lekner & Dorf 1988); it is also glossier, a film of water lying on it. Drawn: outdoors only (by how open the place is to the sky), paving, walls and roofs darker (the porous most: lime, brick, stone) and glossier, the flat paving holding puddles in its hollows that mirror the sky; soaking in within a minute or two of rain and drying over some twenty minutes after. On water and puddles the rain makes rings: a drop's impact sends out a train of gravity–capillary ring waves that spreads and decays [stated: *Gravity–capillary rings generated by water drops*, J. Fluid Mech.]; drawn as many small rings (to ~12 cm in 0.6 s), with the ripples rougher [inferred: sizes].
- **Cloth in the wind.** Banners and washing lean downwind with the gusts and flutter, more the farther below their pole or bracket (materials `<cloth>+wind`; the object builders still have to give their hanging cloth that material and its `hang`).

Sources: [CCA, *White Walls and Black Tiles*](https://cca.qc.ca/en/articles/issues/33/acts-of-looking/96757/white-walls-and-black-tiles); [Chen et al., *Impact of environmental parameters on moisture damage in plastered heritage buildings: Anchang historical town*, J. Chinese Architecture and Urbanism 7(2), 2024](https://accscience.com/journal/JCAU/7/2/10.36922/jcau.4606); [Building Conservation, *Rising Damp*](https://buildingconservation.com/articles/risingdamp/risingdamp.htm); [Homebuilding, *What is salt banding on a damp wall*](https://www.homebuilding.co.uk/structure/walls-ceilings/what-is-salt-banding-on-a-damp-wall); [Lekner & Dorf, *Why some things are darker when wet*, Applied Optics 27, 1988](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/scps/staff/pdf/darkerwhenwet.pdf); [*Gravity–capillary rings generated by water drops*, J. Fluid Mech.](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-fluid-mechanics/article/abs/gravitycapillary-rings-generated-by-water-drops/329DFD5008A3D8E83EB4D01FAC19ED51).

## 17. Animals that move (L3, built; `src/engine/fauna.js`, `src/settlements/ming-suzhou/fauna.js`)

- **Goldfish and carp in the pond.** Goldfish were kept in ornamental ponds in China from the Song (an imperial pond stocked with red and gold fish in 1162), and the Ming bred new varieties, many in tanks, so a Jiangnan garden pond of 1530 holds red and gold goldfish and red carp (*hongli*) [stated: The Diplomat, "Goldfish: from Tang dynasty ponds to 21st century aquariums"]. Ours: loose schools of five goldfish (0.26-0.4 m; orange, gold, red, pale gold, some with cream patches) and three pairs of larger red carp, circling in loops that are checked to stay inside `pondDistance` with a margin; some rise to the surface and leave a ring. The patterned "koi" of Japan are a later breeding, so no koi: [inferred].
- **Dark carp in the canals.** Common carp and crucian carp live in the Jiangnan canals [inferred]; ours are grey-green fish of 0.55-0.8 m swimming along each canal inside `canalDistance`, three pairs a canal.
- **Water is opaque in the engine**, so fish are drawn as soft tinted shapes laid on its face and fade into it [engine detail].
- **Birds.** Barn swallows (*Hirundo rustica*) nest under the eaves of Jiangnan houses and hunt low over water; the Eurasian tree sparrow (*Passer montanus*) is the town sparrow of China and common in the Shanghai-Yangtze delta; the little egret (*Egretta garzetta*) is very common in its waters [stated for the sparrow and egret: 10000 Birds, "The 20 most common birds in Shanghai, part 2"; the swallow's habit inferred]. A grey heron (*Ardea cinerea*) stands at the pond; collared crows (*Corvus torquatus*) of the delta circle at dusk over the temple [inferred].
- **Behaviour.** Swallows loop along the canals and the pond by day (the loop follows the visitor along its canal), rising at the turns; sparrows hop and peck on the street paving and along the copings of the garden walls (4.4 m) and fly off when the visitor is within 4-5 m; herons stand a while, neck swaying, and leave and return; crows circle at twilight; none at night; fewer in rain.
