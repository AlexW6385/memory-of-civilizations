# Han Chang'an, c. 100 BC — dossier

Settlement id: `han-changan` · Styles: `han` (own) and `life` · Region: east Asia (the Wei River plain, Shaanxi) · Code: `src/settlements/han-changan/`, `src/styles/han/`
Status: built from nothing in this batch: the Weiyang Palace front hall on its stepped rammed-earth terrace (enterable, with the throne dais), the palace walls and the que gate towers, a city gate with three passageways, the walled lǐ wards with their houses, the West and East Markets with their market towers, the Changle Palace, the Kunming Lake, the Imperial Academy, people in Han dress, Silk Road caravans, sound.

Deepening pass (Suzhou standard): see §5b, §6b, §9b. Still not built: the city's east and north gates (outside or at the edge of the disc), upper floors of the ward watch-tower houses, the interiors of the Changle Palace's side halls beyond the furnished halls, the Stone Ditch and Heavenly Fortune pavilions' interiors, (the bracket cascades are now built, §4b).

## 1. The moment and the real place

- **Moment shown:** about 100 BC, in the reign of Emperor Wu of Han (141–87 BC). Chang'an has been the capital for a century; the walls are long finished, the palaces stand, the markets are busy with the trade that has begun to run west. [stated: the walls were built under Emperor Hui, begun 195 BC, "completion reported in 191 BC, and the city largely finished by 189 BC" — ancient-history-sites.com; Wikipedia "Chang'an"] Emperor Wu is the ruler under whom the Han send Zhang Qian west and the Silk Road opens; Chang'an is its "eastern terminus". [stated: Wikipedia "Chang'an" names the Nine Markets as "the main market and the eastern terminus of the Silk Road"; the dates of Zhang Qian's missions (138 and 119 BC) are general knowledge, [inferred] here]
- **Place:** the south bank of the Wei River, in the Wei plain north of the Qin mountains; the city's walls are irregular ("the perimeter was irregular rather than a simple rectangle") — the north wall bent like the Big Dipper, the south like the Southern Dipper, a later tradition. [stated: ancient-history-sites.com for the irregularity; the Dipper legend is [inferred] from general accounts and not used in the layout]
- **Why this moment:** Weiyang Palace was completed in 198 BC and "became the official center of government from Emperor Hui onward" [stated: Wikipedia "Chang'an"]; under Wu the Front Hall is in use. The Kunming Lake was dug in 120 BC and the Imperial Academy (Taixue) founded in 124 BC [inferred from general accounts of Emperor Wu's reign; not confirmed by the pages fetched]. Wang Mang's Biyong ritual halls (AD 4) and the later great Taixue campus are later and are left out. [inferred]

## 3. Ground, climate, sky

Flat loess plain of the Wei, a pale yellow-brown soil (dust, packed earth in the streets); the lake and the moat sunk. Continental climate: dry cold winters, hot humid summers; light haze; `damp` low. [inferred, general] The streets planted with trees — the avenues "planted with elm, pagoda tree (sophora), pine and cypress" [inferred: the Sanfu Huangtu tradition, not fetched; a Han Chang'an commonplace]. Willow on the lake, mulberry and jujube in the ward courtyards, millet and wheat fields outside. [inferred]

## 4. The Weiyang front hall (the landmark)

- **Facts:** the foundation is the largest rammed-earth platform of the Han (§2); the buildings above it are lost, "timber posts and beams above the platform carried the roof … earthworks and fallen tiles often survive after the timber frame decays" [stated: ancient-history-sites.com]. So the hall's form is reconstructed from Han evidence: tomb models, the stone and brick reliefs, the que, the excavated tiles. [stated: "ceramic tomb models show courtyards, towers, wells" — ancient-history-sites.com]
- **Ours:** the terrace is three tiers each 3 m high (9 m), faced with rammed earth plastered with ochre earth; a central ramp-stair of brick steps 10 m wide climbs from the forecourt to the crown (the Han "ramp"); on the crown stands the hall: nine bays of 6.6 m (59.4 m) by four of 6 m (24 m), floor 0.9 m above the crown on a low base with three flights, round timber columns 7 m high painted red, the architrave painted, bracket arms (dou-gong of the Han: a simple cap block and a straight arm, no tier cascade) carrying the eaves; a hipped roof (wudian) of grey tile, nearly straight in its slopes and slightly turned at the corners, the ridge ending in simple upturned ridge-ends; the eaves end in round tiles (wadang) all round [inferred from the Han tile-ends, the round tile cover is the Han signature: circular eave-tile ends are the form of the period — general knowledge, not on the pages fetched]. Inside: the floor of polished dark earth, the central three bays open, the side bays with lattice windows, the walls plastered, the ceiling coffered; at the back (north) the throne dais, 1.2 m high, 14 × 7 m, three steps, a lacquered screen behind it, hanging curtains in red, rows of low lacquered tables and reed mats for the court (the Han sat on mats), bronze lamps and censers. [inferred]
- **Side halls:** the east and west chambers (dongxiang, xixiang) on the crown, 18 × 14 m, hip-and-gable roofs; the Jiaofang Hall behind. [stated: Jiaofang north of the Front Hall — travelchinaguide.com; the shapes inferred]

## 5. Style vocabulary (style `han`)

- **Rammed earth (hangtu):** "packing and compacting successive layers of soil within temporary formwork. The dense mass could form a wall or a raised building platform." [stated: ancient-history-sites.com] Ours: ochre earth, faint layers, plastered where worked; palace terraces and walls with a plaster face, the city wall bare earth with a tile cap.
- **Que (gate towers):** "a freestanding, ceremonial gate tower … peaked during the Han dynasty"; "stone copies of earlier wooden and/or earthen towers placed in pairs before palaces, temples, and government buildings"; "they developed from gate towers that were part of a building or city wall" [stated: Wikipedia "Que (tower)"]. Ours: the main tower, an earth shaft 11 m high with an upper pavilion under a hip roof, and the lower subsidiary tower (zi que) attached on the inner side; in pairs. The east gate of the palace has them. [stated: the east and north gates had towers — Wikipedia "Weiyang Palace"]
- **Roofs:** grey ceramic tiles, round-ended cover tiles; hipped roofs for halls and gate towers, hip-and-gable (xuanshan) and gable roofs for houses. [inferred]
- **Houses:** rammed-earth walls plastered, timber frames, tiled roofs; the courtyard house with a gate and a tower-like gatehouse; the "L"-plan house; the watch-tower house of several storeys known from tomb models. [stated: "ceramic tomb models show courtyards, towers, wells, and grain-processing equipment" — ancient-history-sites.com; the forms inferred]

## 5b. Furniture and small things (src/styles/han/props.js, court.js)

- **Bronze ding, drums, lamp trees, banners, steles** in pairs along the paved axis of the forecourt; the drum on a post under a feather canopy (jiangu) and the bronze lamp tree (lianzhi deng) are Han court furniture known from reliefs and tomb finds. [inferred, general; the Han "construction drum" and "branch lamp" are standard Han objects]
- **Chariots and carriages (che)** in rows in the courts, a canopy on some, two horses abreast under a yoke: the Han carriage had a single central pole with a yoke across it. [inferred from Han reliefs and the Qin bronze chariots of the generation before]
- **Interiors:** the low couch (ta) with a folding screen, the low lacquer table (an), reed mats (xi) for sitting, chests, bundles of bamboo slips, the bronze lamp, the earthen stove (zao) with an iron pot and a steamer, the foot-treadle pounder (dui) over a stone mortar, the upright loom, grain vats, a smith's hearth with bellows and anvil — all objects of the Han tomb models and reliefs (stoves, pounders, granaries and wells are among the models of the Han tombs). [stated: "ceramic tomb models show courtyards, towers, wells, and grain-processing equipment" — ancient-history-sites.com; the forms inferred]
- **Orchestra:** a rack of bronze bells (bianzhong) over a row of stone chimes (bianqing) and the great drum, with a zither, flute and drum players, at the foot of the aisle in the Front Hall. [inferred: the Han court's ritual music (yayue); gong mode on D]
- **Strings of dried corn, gourds and jujubes** under the eaves; vine pads on the coping; woodpiles and water vats at the doors. [inferred; no paper lanterns and no glazed jars imported from the later kits are placed by us — but the generic street life (src/objects/life.js, kit east_asia) still adds its own door things]

## 6. The town

- **Wards (lǐ):** the wall round each ward, one or two gates with a gatehouse and the gate keeper; inside, lanes and courtyard houses; no shops in the wards (trade is in the markets). [stated: "enclosed neighbourhood units overseen by minor officials", "walls and gates set them apart from an open street grid"; the market-only rule — general, [inferred]]
- **Streets:** avenues 45 m wide, three lanes (the middle for the emperor), planted with trees, side ditches. [stated for the width; the rest inferred]
- **The markets:** walled, with gates and officials' offices; a market tower (qiting) with the flag and the drum from which the market officer watches; shops in long rows (liesi), each with its counter; goods: silk, hemp, lacquer, bronze, iron tools, salt, grain, pottery, horses, the imports of the Western Regions (grape, alfalfa, glass, horses). [inferred from the Han market reliefs and the Shiji / Hanshu goods lists]
- **The Silk Road:** the Bactrian two-humped camels with loads of silk and goods, the Hu merchants (Sogdians and Central Asians) in caps and long coats leading them in at the west gate; the Han envoys. [inferred; Emperor Wu's western policy]

[stated: the 6.13 m moat — Wikipedia "Chang'an"; the bridges and the berm inferred]

[inferred]

## 7. People (dress, bearing)

Men: the shenyi robe or the long jacket crossed right over left (youren), a sash, trousers for labourers; the cap (guan) and the hair in a topknot for officials, the headcloth (ze) for commoners; officials with a tablet or a staff, scholars with brush and bamboo slips; soldiers in lamellar armour; women: the quju (curved-hem) robe, hair in a bun; children in short jackets; Hu merchants: pointed cap, tight coat, boots, beards. [inferred, general; consistent with the Han tomb figures]

## 8. Sound

Wind in the eaves, drums and gongs from the market tower, the calls of vendors, cart wheels on packed earth, camel bells, the bells and chimes of the palace (the bronze bells and stone chimes, the court's ritual music: the zither, the sheng, the drum), geese and crows. Music: the Han court's pentatonic music, the qin and the yu (a free-reed organ), bells and drums. [inferred]

## 9. Weathering

Dust, the loess's yellow on every foot of wall, rain-gullied rammed earth, moss on the north faces of tile in the damp months, soot over the kitchen doors, whitewash (limewash) flaking. `damp` low; `waterline` at the lake level. [inferred]

## 9b. Weathering added

Rain streaks (plaster_streak) down the earth walls from the coping and patches of fallen plaster at their feet on every ward front; vine pads on the copings; the soot above shop doors; the sparrows on the copings; dust is the ground's own loess. [inferred]

## 6c. The service quarters below the ramp (release pass)

Two blocks of the palace's service quarters (clerks, guards, stewards) lie west and east of the paved court at the ramp's foot, 25-80 m from the arrival: fourteen houses in four fronts (walled court, row on the lane, open-fronted store under an awning, two-storey lou). The wards' houses (`han_house`) have solid halls, so these are built by their own style, `src/styles/hanlodge` (`hanl_house`), from the same kit: the hall has a doorway, a floor, a ceiling and a lit room furnished as a dwelling, kitchen, weaver's, scholar's, granary or workshop, with someone at home (`han-life-households`). The ceiling slab keeps the eave over the platform kerb from shutting the doorway in the walker's collision.

## 4b. The hall's bracket zone, frieze and ridge (detail pass, `src/styles/hanlodge/hall.js`)

- **The way:** from the arrival at the ramp's head down the ramp-stair to the paved court, along the lanes of the two service quarters (clerks', guards', stewards' houses), round the east quarter's end and along the road to the palace's east gate between its que, through it to the great avenue, up the avenue past the offices (shu) to the street east, in at the East Market's west gate and along its cross lane to the market tower: about 590 m. [inferred: the order of the places is the layout's (§2); the Han court's officials did enter by the east gate: travelchinaguide.com, Weiyang page, "officials used the north gate, vassals the east gate" is the opposite, so the east gate is used here only as the way out to the avenue, not as a ceremonial entry]
- **Opened places on it:** the quarters' row houses and stores (a doorway 1.8 m wide, a lit furnished hall, a household at home), the avenue's offices (a gate 4.2 m wide, the hall's middle bay open on its desk and shelves), the East Market's shops (the counter's flap 1.7 m wide, the shelves of the trade's goods, the ledger table and stool, the vat; a keeper behind the counter in every shop on the walk). The wards' houses, enterable by the lot's seed (§6d), lie off this walk and keep their rule. [inferred from the Han market-relief scenes and tomb models, §6]
- **Doors and gates of the style:** the hanlodge houses' gates are widened to 2.0–2.4 m (rank by standing) and the hall door to 1.8 m so that the walker's body passes; the office lots that stood across the cross streets (the street east to the East Market, the road to the Changle gate) are removed from the layout.
- **Things not done:** the shops of the West Market, and the ward lanes, are not on the walk; no Han place of worship stands on it (the ward altars, the "li she", are not built).

## 14. Night (`nightlight.js`, `nightplace.js`)

- **What lit the city:** oil lamps of bronze and pottery, in the palace halls and the households (the bronze lamp trees of the forecourt and the crown, §5b, are lit: three cups each); the courtyard torch (庭燎) before the hall — the Shijing's ode "Tingliao" names the court torches burning for the king's audience before dawn [stated: the ode's title and subject; their form here is ours, a post with a bowl of burning reeds]; iron fire baskets as watch fires at the palace gates, the city gates, the ward gates and the market gates [inferred: the Han gate watches and the Commandant of the Capital Guard (zhi jinwu, from 104 BC) patrolling at night — general knowledge, not fetched]; a lamp in the market tower and in the que's crowning pavilion, lamps in the tower-ship's cabins [inferred]. The markets themselves are dark (shut at dusk); no paper lanterns (not yet invented).
- **Households:** lit or dark by the hash of the lot (rank 2 about 70%, rank 1 50%, rank 0 30%, the service quarters 65%): a dark house's lamps are removed from the plan's lights too; a lit one shows lattice windows glowing, a pool on the doorstep and a warm sheet across the open door. [inferred]
- **Method:** all light is geometry in the engine's `halo` material (visible at the low preset, night only); the palace halls' lattice windows glow in 85% of cases. No flame meshes (they cost a draw each tile).
