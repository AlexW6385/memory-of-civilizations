## 1. The real example and the moment

- **The place:** Karakorum (Qara Qorum), the capital of the Mongol Empire, founded by Chinggis Khan's camp in 1220 and built up by Ögedei from 1235 [stated: Wikipedia "Karakorum"; UW Silk Road "Karakorum"]. Little of it is visible now; a stone tortoise plinth is one of the few surface traces [stated: UW Silk Road].
- **The moment:** 1254, the year the Flemish Franciscan William of Rubruck was at the court of Möngke Khan (he was there 1253–1255) [stated: UW Silk Road]. The scene is as he saw it: the town in the season of the court's feast, the khan's gers outside, envoys of many nations in the street.
- **Honesty about what stands where:** what excavation found is the Chinese commercial quarter, kilns, workshops, paved roads, adobe and brick buildings, and the "Great Hall" (below). The palace of Möngke and the silver tree left no trace; both are rebuilt from Rubruck's text. The plan of the town (which quarter lies where, where the temples stand) is [inferred] from the survey's density pattern and Rubruck's order, not excavated.

## 3. Layout of the settlement (radius 190 m, +x east, +z south)

- **The walled town [stated: wall]:** "surrounded by a mud wall and has four gates"; at each gate a market: millet (grain) at the east, sheep and goats at the west, oxen and carts at the south, horses at the north [stated: Rubruck]. The real wall enclosed about 1.5 × 2.5 km [stated: UW]; here it is compressed to a rectangle 270 × 200 m (x −135…135, z −100…100) so the town fills the class radius at Suzhou's density [inferred; DESIGN §4.5 magnifies crowded places]. The wall is rammed earth, about 6 m high, battered, a parapet on top [inferred from Central Asian and Chinese rammed-earth practice], a tiled gate-tower over each gate [inferred: Chinese-style gate, as the roof tiles are Chinese].
- **Two quarters [stated: Rubruck]:** the Saracens' with the markets, and the Cathayans', all craftsmen. The Saracen (Muslim merchants') quarter lies west of the cross-street and along the main street; the Cathayan (Chinese craftsmen) quarter south-east [inferred: excavation concentrated on the Chinese commercial section]. "Large palaces" of the court secretaries stand in the town [stated: Rubruck]: here two courtyard houses with tiled roofs on the main street.
- **Streets [stated/inferred]:** a main street paved in stone [stated: UW] running west–east between the west and east gates, and a cross-street from the north to the south gate [inferred]; lanes between compounds of sun-dried mud-brick [stated: Hyperallergic: collapsed mud-brick compounds].
- **Open ground [stated: Hyperallergic: 40 % of the walled area empty]:** left for gers and carts: the north-west quarter of the town and the lanes' ends hold gers on the ground and on carts, herds' pens and hay.
- **The khan's palace [stated: Rubruck]:** "near the city walls, enclosed by a brick wall like a monastery's enclosure" with the great hall and many storehouses/barns for provisions and treasure [stated: Rubruck, via UW]. North-east corner of the town, gate to the south [inferred].
- **Temples of many faiths [stated: Rubruck]:** twelve idol (Buddhist/Daoist) temples "of different nations", two mosques, one Nestorian church "at the extreme end of the city". Placed: the two mosques in the Saracen quarter, the church at the south-west end, the idol temples spread, one the large Great Hall.
- **Outside the walls:** the court's gers: "the ordu of a rich Mo'al seems like a large town" [stated: Rubruck]; when the court was present Mongols camped in gers on the surrounding land [stated: UW]. Two ger camps south of the town; herds of horses and camels grazing round; the four gates' markets spill out with pens and wagons.

## 4. The landmark: Möngke's palace and the silver tree

- **The hall [stated: Rubruck]:** "like a church, with a middle nave and two sides beyond two rows of pillars, and three doors to the south"; before the middle door, inside, stood the silver tree; at the north end a raised place where the khan sat, with two stairs up to it (one for the cup-bearer, one to go up and down); the space between tree and dais empty; men sat on the khan's right, women on the left [stated by Rubruck as I know the Rockhill translation (ch. on Mangu's court), but NOT re-verified word for word in this pass: the fetched excerpts (UW Silk Road) confirm only the high raised place, the tree and bowls filling a large part of the palace, the four-day feast in one colour from boots to turbans — treat the nave, pillars, three doors and two stairs as [inferred] until checked]. "The silver tree and its bowls took up a large part of the palace" [stated: Rubruck via UW text]. The khan sat "on a high and raised place" [stated].
- **Measurements [inferred]:** the real Great Hall's module is the best evidence we have of a Karakorum timber hall: columns on a regular grid on a rammed-earth podium, Chinese-type tiles [stated: DAI]. The palace is built with the same vocabulary: podium 1.6 m high, hall of 5 bays wide (nave 7.2 m, two aisles of 4.8 m = 16.8 m) by 7 bays deep (4.8 m = 33.6 m), two rows of 8 columns, three doors on the south, a double-eave tile roof with upswept corners, painted red timber [inferred]. Its size is modest ("Saint-Denis is ten times bigger" [stated: Rubruck]).
- **Interior [inferred, grounded in Rubruck]:** walls and ceiling hung with gold-thread and coloured silk (the Mongol court's cloth-of-gold hangings), felt and carpets on the floor, the throne dais at the north end with the khan's seat of gilded wood, benches along both sides; the four-day feast, each day in a single colour from boots to turban [stated: Rubruck].
- **The silver tree [stated: Public Domain Review/Rubruck]:** a silver tree wrapped in silver serpents, with branches, leaves and fruit; an angel with a trumpet on top; four silver lions at the roots, each with a conduit, "all belching forth white milk of mares"; four pipes from the branches pouring wine, fermented mare's milk (qara qumiss), rice wine and honey mead into silver basins below. Made on site by the Parisian goldsmith Guillaume Boucher (William Buchier). The drinks were stored in a cellar outside, poured into the roots and siphoned up; a man hidden beneath blew the trumpet [stated]. Built: a silver trunk 0.6 m across and about 5 m high, four silver serpents spiralling up and meeting at the crown, branches carrying fruit, the angel, four lions about 1.1 m long around the base on a low octagonal silver-clad platform; four basins.
- **Compound [inferred]:** brick wall with tiled coping, a three-door gate pavilion on the south, a forecourt with the khan's great white ger and carts, storehouse barns along the east wall, cellar and kitchens, the wine cart [inferred].

## 5. The Great Hall (the Buddhist temple of the Cathayan quarter)

- [stated: DAI] 38 × 38 m, rammed-earth podium in alternating layers, 64 granite column bases (8 × 8), probably a stupa at the centre over a votive deposit, Chinese-type roof tiles and Chinese-model walls, Buddha statues and reliefs; the reconstruction shows four Buddhas on lotus thrones. Rebuilt as a hall with 5.2 m bays (columns on an 8 × 8 grid, the four central ones left out for the stupa), a double-eaved tiled roof over a lantern storey, a white stupa with a gilt finial and thirteen rings in the middle, four Buddhas on thrones round it [inferred for the proportions].

## 6. The style (`src/styles/mongol/`)

- **Ger [stated: Rubruck]:** "a circular frame of interlaced sticks", a round hoop at the top with a collar acting as a chimney, covered in white felt; felt often coated with chalk, white clay or powdered bone and embroidered with vines, trees, birds, beasts; felt patchwork hangs before the entrance; made as large as 30 feet (9 m) wide; the door always turns to the south; the master's couch on the north side, the women's side east, the men's west; belongings in felt-covered coffers on tall carts, set on either side of the dwelling; a great house drawn by twenty-two oxen [all stated: Rubruck]. Built: lattice (khana) of 5 sections, doorway with a painted wooden door (red), uni roof poles to the toono ring, white felt in three layers, ropes (hair rope bands) round the walls, felt apron over the door; on the ground ger r 3.0–4.5 m, wall 1.4 m, ring at 2.9 m; on a cart a ger of r 3–4 m over a wagon bed 3.2 m wide with big spoked wheels.
- **Carts [stated: Rubruck]:** tall carts; "a man might as well say an axle like the mast of a ship"; the wagon with coffers; oxen team.
- **Mud-brick houses of the Saracen quarter [inferred]:** flat roofs on beams, courtyard houses; the mosque with a square brick minaret and a dome, glazed turquoise tile trim [inferred, from Central Asian architecture of the period].
- **Chinese-type halls (Cathayan quarter, temples, palace, gates) [stated: tiles Chinese; inferred: form]:** timber hall on a rammed earth platform, grey or green-glazed tile roof with upswept eaves. Takes `chinese`/`song` style vocabulary where useful (read-only).
- **Nestorian church [stated: Rubruck]:** a small mud-brick church with a cross over the door, at the town's far south-west end [inferred form].
- **Craft [stated: UW]:** ceramic kilns for roof tiles, pipes and tableware, metalworking with water-powered bellows, glass beads, cart axle rings: kilns and forges built in the Cathayan quarter, workshops with open fronts, tile stacks, charcoal heaps.

## 7. Ground, weather, planting

- **Ground [inferred]:** the Orkhon valley floor, about 1,300 m above sea level, a broad plain of short steppe grass, gravelly soil, the Khangai hills far off; here nearly flat (the town levelled), swells beyond the wall.
- **Climate [stated/inferred]:** continental, dry, windy; clear cold bright sky; strong wind (felt flaps and banners, ger ropes taut). Damp very low: dust, bleached timber, dry earth.
- **Planting [inferred]:** short dry steppe grass (feather grass), sagebrush (Artemisia) stand-ins, a few willows and poplars where the town's wells and the Orkhon feed them, the patches of millet outside the east gate [stated: Rubruck: millet at the east gate].

## 8. People and dress [stated: Rubruck; inferred]

- Mongols: the long right-lapel robe (deel/qutu) belted with a sash, boots, a hat with a brim or a fur-trimmed cap; men's hair braided in two plaits behind the ears; women in the tall boqta headdress [stated/inferred]; the Mongol women drove the carts [stated: Rubruck]. Muslim merchants: long coats and white turbans [inferred]. Chinese craftsmen: tunics, round caps [inferred]. Envoys: Franciscans in grey habits (Rubruck himself), Armenian monks, Russian, Alan, Georgian, Hungarian, Greek people [stated: Public Domain Review: a cosmopolitan population including Hungarians, Greeks, Armenians, Alans, Georgians]. Nestorian priests. Buddhist monks in saffron/maroon.

## 9. Animals

- Horses (the northern gate's market, herds on the steppe); camels (caravans of the Saracens); oxen (the southern gate, carts); sheep and goats (the western gate); dogs at the gers; birds: ravens, kites, larks [inferred].

## 10. Sound

- Wind in felt and banners; horses and camel bells; the drum of the Buddhist temples, the call to prayer of the mosques, Nestorian bells/semantron; hammers from the forges; the khan's feast: voices, a lute and the horse-head fiddle (morin khuur, later but of the type) [inferred]. Music: pentatonic, a long song drone with throat-voice, a ney/flute (the available timbres: drone 'voice', lead 'ney').

## 12. Deepening pass (Suzhou standard): what was added and how it is grounded

- **Courtyard houses, enterable [inferred]:** every house of the Saracen quarter now has a door, latticed windows, a furnished main room (family: sleeping platform under felts, painted chests, a low table, a brazier; merchant: bales, a strongbox, rolled carpets, a scale; craft: a loom with a half-woven web, wool, a dye trough), a kitchen room behind a partition (hearth, cauldron, water jars, firewood), and in the taller houses a stair to an upper room with carpets and cushions. Grounding: flat roofs on poplar beams, courts and ovens as in Central Asian towns of the period [inferred]; the felt floors and carpets from Rubruck's gers and the court's hangings [stated: Rubruck]. The court holds an oven, dung-cake stack, jars, a bench, a notched ladder to the roof, a laundry line and a felt-roofed cheese-drying rack [inferred]. Weathering: plaster fallen to show brick, cracks, a slumped breach with rubble in some court walls, matching the survey's collapsed mud-brick compounds [stated: Hyperallergic].
- **Shops [inferred]:** the bazaar rows are now bays of 5 m depth (4.6 m on the cross-street) with a counter at one side and the way in beside it; eight trades (cloth, carpets and felts, saddlery, spices, pottery, metalwork, food, wine and kumiss) with stock, hanging goods, a name board, a blade sign, a lantern and an awning. The main street's fronts stand 7.6 m from its axis (the old 9.6 m left it reading empty); the cross-street gets rows on both sides. Trades follow Rubruck's craftsmen and merchants and the excavated workshops [stated: UW; DAI].
- **Mosque halls [inferred]:** walkable, with rows of prayer carpets, timber columns, a mihrab niche in the west wall (the qibla from Karakorum lies west-south-west [inferred]), a three-step minbar, a Qur'an stand and brass lamps.
- **Temples, workshops, storehouses:** the idol temples carry offering tables, incense burners, attendants, painted panels, banners, a drum and a bell, monks' cushions; the workshops are a potter's (wheel, drying shelves, tile stacks) and a smith's (anvil, bellows, charcoal, quench tub) [stated: UW: kilns, metalworking with bellows]; the palace storehouses hold sacks, chests, bolts of silk and jars [stated: Rubruck via UW: barns for provisions and treasure].
- **Wall:** rubble at the foot, rain gullies, buttresses of rammed earth every 16 m inside (not by the gates) [inferred from rammed-earth practice].
- **Gers:** about a third of the gers on the open lots and four in the second court camp are opened and furnished (the ger of the landmark furnishing); yards with dung stacks, churns, drying racks, carts, hay, washing.
- **Palace court:** a flagged way from the gate to the hall's steps, hitching rails, store sheds, the cellar's mouth, wine carts with their oxen, kitchens with cauldrons, about thirty courtiers, envoys and retainers in knots [stated: Rubruck: envoys of many nations at the feast].
- **Planting:** poplar in the courts, willows round the wells, feather-grass tufts (halfa stands in) and tamarisk (for saxaul and sagebrush) on the steppe; the Mediterranean maquis of the first pass is gone [inferred: the Orkhon plain].
- **Still not built:** the Great Hall's upper storey is shut; no street-level music zone; the minaret is not climbable.

### 12. The first view and the court at eye level (2026-11-12)

- **The arrival is on the court's axis in the gate**, and the court's walkers keep to its two flanks (2.4 m off the axis), so nobody walks through the view.
- **The khan's household behind the hall:** six gers of his wives and servants (two of them rich, one open) behind the hall and by the east wall, each with its yard, a woman at the work of the day, a child, a dog, a horse tethered at two [inferred: a court lived in gers; Rubruck's great ger stands in the court — §4]. Their hearths smoke at the meals.
- **The court at eye level:** the yards of the great ger and the wagon-house (dung cakes, the churn, meat drying, washing); the gate guards' bench, fire-wood and barrels inside the gate; dogs asleep in the sun, children at knucklebones by the banners; a felt-maker's yard by the west storehouses (rolled felts, wool, aaruul — dried curd — on trays) [inferred].
- **Birds:** ravens on the banner staves and the great hall's ridge, doves on the gate hall's eaves, a falcon and a kite over the court.
- **Ground and planting:** the court's floor trodden along its axis and scuffed and patched with dry grass and dung at the margins, grassy at the foot of the walls; poplars along the walls' feet.

## 3b. The fronts of the shop rows (round 5)

[inferred] Within 120 m of the arrival stand 106 shop bays (each a furnished, walkable shop of one of eight trades with its seller inside), four workshops, five idol temples and the court's gers. The rows were not all one design: the Cathayan east rows (craftsmen from North China) now keep a Chinese front — red lacquered posts between the bays, a grey-tiled eave with a green ridge on painted brackets; the Saracen rows along the cross-street and some on the main street carry a plastered upper storey with latticed windows over the shops; the rest keep the mud-brick bays with cloth awnings. Every other bay now has a lamp inside (a point light) besides its paper lantern.
