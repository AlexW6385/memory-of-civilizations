## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Forbidden City (Zijin Cheng), Beijing, the imperial palace from 1420 to 1912; about 961 × 753 m, surrounded by a wall and a moat. [stated: Wikipedia; imaginoso spec]
- **Part shown:** the Outer Court on the central axis: the Meridian Gate (Wumen), the Inner Golden Water River, the Gate of Supreme Harmony (Taihemen, the largest freestanding gate of the palace), and the three great halls on a three-tiered white marble terrace. [stated: Wikipedia; MIT Dome image records]
- **Moment shown:** after the Hall of Supreme Harmony's rebuilding of 1695–97, which reduced it from about 95 × 48 m to its present 64 × 37 m [stated: Wikipedia — "after a reconstruction in the 16th century" is given there; the present hall dates from 1695–97, our inference from the hall's history], i.e. the Qing palace on the Ming plan.
- **Townscape:** the lanes (hutong) and courtyard houses (siheyuan) of the Inner City round the Imperial City. [stated: Wikipedia, Siheyuan; Beijing siheyuan]

## 3. Orientation

Everything faces south: the halls, the gates, the courtyard houses' main halls. [stated: general; Beijing siheyuan]

## 4. The palace's buildings and objects

- **Halls:** vermilion columns on marble bases; beams painted blue and green; red lattice doors and windows; yellow glazed tiles; double eaves on the greatest halls. The Hall of Supreme Harmony's roof is a double-eaved hip (wudian), the highest rank. [stated: general]
- **Terrace:** white marble, three tiers, balustrades with posts and rails all round, stairs in three flights at the front and back, the middle one a single carved slab (the imperial way). [stated: Britannica — "a marble terrace … above the marble balustrades"; the ramp: benjielayug]
- **Bronzes:** on the terrace before the hall stand bronze cauldrons, cranes, tortoises, a sundial and a grain measure [stated: Britannica]; we place tripods and vats; gilt bronze vats along the courts (water against fire) [stated: general]; bronze lions before the gates [stated: general].
- **Galleries:** covered walks along the east and west sides of the courtyard, the Pavilions of Embodying Benevolence and Spreading Righteousness between them [stated: general].

## 5. The courtyard houses (siheyuan)

- Grey brick walls; the gate at the south-east corner, vermilion; a screen wall inside it; the main house on the north side facing south; side houses east and west; the "opposite house" along the lane facing north. [stated: Wikipedia, Siheyuan]
- Simple roofs of grey tiles with plain ridges. [stated: Wikipedia, Beijing siheyuan — "relatively simple Qingshui roof ridges"]
- A tree in the courtyard, often a goldfish tank. [stated: Beijing Siheyuan (wiki mirror)] We plant a scholar tree (an elm stands in) or a jujube, and set a vat in some.

## 6. Ground and water

Level ground at 0; moat water 1.6 m down over a bed at 3.6 m; the Golden Water River 1.2 m down over 3 m; both with stone walls and marble balustrades. Paving of grey brick inside the palace, marble on the axis; lanes of brick and bluestone. (`ground.js`)

## 7. Planting

No trees in the palace's courts [stated: the Outer Court was kept bare — general]; willows along the moat; a tree in each courtyard; grass and a few trees at the town's edge.

## 8. Sky and weather

A clear autumn: high blue sky, dry air, a north-west wind, now and then dust from the north. Latitude 40° N. [inferred]

## 9. Sound

The court's ceremonial music (bell and stone chimes, slow, on the gong mode) in the open; mouth organs and flute in the halls; a plucked lute for the lanes. [inferred; timbres from our sound library]

## 10. Carved, cast and glazed detail (L2–L3)

- **Balustrade and spouts:** every tier of the terrace (and the platforms of the Gate of Supreme Harmony and the Gate of Heavenly Purity) carries a carved marble balustrade: posts with dragon and phoenix heads by turns, between them the sill, a framed lower panel with a carved medallion, the cloud band, vase-shaped balusters with cloud caps and a round handrail. Under each post a dragon-head spout (chishou) juts from the face below the coping, a great one from each corner. "There are over 1,000 of them" round the three-tiered sumeru terrace, draining it [stated: Google Arts & Culture]; "under the railings of each tier, a row of dragon heads" [stated: Sohu]. Our posts, one every 1.6 m, give about 1,100 spouts on the three tiers [inferred: count]. The dragon-and-phoenix posts, the vase balusters and the panel's form follow the Qing balustrade as seen in photographs [inferred].
- **Golden Water bridges:** the five bridges carry the same balustrade, its posts and panels following the arch's rise [inferred: photographs]; the river's banks have it too.
- **Lions:** the Gate of Supreme Harmony "is guarded by one male and one female bronze lion"; "the male's right paw rests on an embroidered ball", "the female has a cub under her left paw" [stated: Google Arts & Culture]; "45 snail curls on the lion — nine times five, the supreme number" [stated: Sohu]. Ours sit on sumeru pedestals, the male on the east [inferred: the usual arrangement], with collars and bells, and 45 curls each. The lions at the Gate of Heavenly Purity are gilt [inferred].
- **Terrace bronzes:** "at the east corner of the platform is a sundial made of white marble"; "a marble square pavilion named Jialiang stands opposite" [stated: Travel China Guide]; "a pair of bronze cranes and a pair of bronze tortoises stand on the terrace" [stated: Google Arts & Culture]; the crane is 1.98 m high [stated: Sohu]. Ours: the sundial's dial tilted to the plane of the equator (latitude 40°) with its gnomon toward the pole [inferred: how an equatorial sundial works], the grain measure in a small stone shrine, tortoises with dragon heads, cranes, tripod burners, gilt vats with animal masks and rings beside the halls [inferred: positions].
- **Roof ornaments:** "ten figurines sit at each roof corner" of the Hall of Supreme Harmony, unique among ancient buildings; the ridge-end ornament (dawen) "stands 3.4 meters tall, weighs 4.3 metric tons" [stated: Google Arts & Culture; Travel China Guide; Sohu]. Ours: the immortal riding a bird leading, ten beasts, the hip beast behind, on every hip of both eaves; the dawen at 3.4 m with its sword hilt, curled tail and the small beast on its back. Other halls carry fewer, in odd numbers (9 on the Meridian Gate, the Gate of Supreme Harmony and the Hall of Preserving Harmony, 7 on the pavilions and the Gate of Heavenly Purity) [inferred: the rank of the buildings]. The Meridian Gate's hall now has a double-eaved hip roof, the highest rank, as in photographs [inferred; the plan had hip-and-gable].
- **Gate leaves:** the major gates are "adorned with 81 gilded door nails", "arranged in a strict nine-by-nine grid" [stated: Young Post]. The leaves of the Meridian Gate's three passages, the West and East Flowery Gates and the side gates of the courts stand open, red, with nine by nine gilt nails and a gilt mask with its ring [inferred: masks from photographs].
- **Courtyard houses:** at each gate the two leaves stand open, a pair of drum stones (men dun) flanks the threshold, two round door pins (men zan) hold the lintel [inferred: photographs of Beijing siheyuan gates].
- **Corner towers:** "nine beams, eighteen columns and seventy-two ridges" [stated: Sohu] (a saying; Travel China Guide counts 28 eave corners and 76 ridges). On the platform at the corner of the wall, ringed by white marble balustrades; a main pavilion in the middle with rooms reaching out toward each side; three tiers of yellow-tiled roofs: the upper "a four-angled pinnacle combined with a hip-and-gable roof, forming a cross-shaped ridge pattern, with a gilded copper finial at the center", the middle "four hip-and-gable roofs, one per side", the lowest "a half-sloped eave"; beams and brackets painted blue and green [stated: Travel China Guide]. Ours: a core of three bays (8 m) with a wing 6 m wide and 3.2 m deep on each side, lattice all round the ground storey; the lowest tier a skirt of eaves round each wing, meeting at the re-entrant corners; an upper storey of red walls with lattice bands, the wings' upper storeys under four hip-and-gable roofs with their gables turned out, the core rising higher under two gabled roofs crossing, their gables closed by boards, the gilt finial at the crossing, about 17 m over the platform; a marble balustrade on the platform's outer sides and on its inner sides either side of the walls [inferred: dimensions and the skirt's form].
- **The moat's banks:** the moat is 52 m wide and 4.1 m deep, its bed paved with brick [stated: Travel China Guide]; ours carries along both banks, on the stone walls, a low parapet of grey brick (1.05 m) on a plinth course under a stone coping, open at the bridges' landings, in place of the marble balustrade it had before [inferred: the owner's survey note; the bank walls' form as seen in photographs of the moat].

## 11. The throne rooms and the courtyards (L3)

## 12. Painted beams and bracket sets (L3)

- **Hexi caihua.** Hexi is the imperial form of caihua, adding "imperial symbols such as the dragon, as well as increased use of gold"; blue, green and red grounds set off the gold; dragons, phoenixes and pearls "all gilded"; "dragons were always painted on a blue background, and phoenixes on green"; the golden-dragon hexi (jinlong) is the highest form; it is used mainly on the important buildings of the Outer Court [stated: Cultural Keys]. "Large numbers of golden Chinese dragons on blue and green backgrounds", used only on the most important palace buildings [stated: Wikipedia, Hexi Caihua]. The central panel (fangxin) is the core of a beam's painting; between it and the band at each end (gutou) lies the zhaotou, the two side sections together about two-thirds of the beam's length; boxes (hezi) may be set in on long beams; the hexi kind is divided by "M"-shaped lines [stated: drpress, *beam and lintel painting*]. Ours (`src/styles/qing/caihua.js`): on every bay of the frieze beam under the eaves (four sides), of the tie beam over the doors of the tall halls' walled sides, and of the deep frieze of the double-eaved halls' upper bodies: a ground of blue or green, the gutou at each column in the other colour with a gilt line, a gilt border top and foot; the central panel a third of the bay with pointed ends, framed in gold, its field the other colour; two gilt chevrons outside each pointed end (our reading of the M lines); in long bays a roundel with a coiled gold dragon between the gutou and the chevrons; in the panel a gold dragon striding to its flaming pearl. Blue and green alternate bay by bay and between the upper and lower beams [inferred: the alternation from photographs]. The Hall of Supreme Harmony, the Meridian Gate, the Gate of Supreme Harmony and the Hall of Preserving Harmony (nine roof figures and up) carry jinlong hexi, dragons in every panel; the lesser buildings (the pavilions, the Gate of Heavenly Purity, the Temple of Heaven's gate) longfeng hexi, dragons on blue and phoenixes on green [inferred: rank]. Films a few millimetres off the beam; the figures much simplified.
- **Bracket sets (dougong).** Ours: on a plate over the frieze beam, a set at every column and about one to a metre between (Qing halls crowd their sets), each a bearing block, then two tiers (three on the halls over 8 m) of cross arm and tongue stepping out 0.24 m a tier, small blocks on the arms' ends and the tongues' tips; the outer purlin over the last tips; red boards between the sets up to the roof's underside; the timbers green, the blocks blue [inferred: the sets' form simplified from the Qing bracket set; colours from photographs]. The roofs of the palace's double-eaved and ornamented halls stand that much higher (0.88–1.03 m) over their eaves [inferred].

## 13. The Temple of Heaven: the Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests (L3)

- **What and when.** The hall built in 1420 as the Hall of Great Sacrifice (11 bays, double-eaved hip roof, on a round platform of two tiers); in 1542 a third tier was added to the platform; in 1545 the three-eaved round hall, the Daxiang Dian, "瓦分三色，上檐青瓦，中檐黄瓦，下檐绿瓦" (blue tiles on the upper eave, yellow on the middle, green on the lower); in 1752 the Qianlong emperor had all three roofs tiled blue; the present hall is the rebuilding after the fire of 1889 [stated: Beijing News (bjd.com.cn) 2023-08-24]. So at our moment (after 1697) the roofs are blue, yellow and green.
- **Dimensions.** The altar (Qigu Tan) "三层，高5.6米，下层直径91米，中层直径80米，上层68米"; the hall round, "高38米，直径32.7米"; 28 columns: the four dragon-well pillars "高19.2米，直径1.2米", 12 gold columns carrying the second roof, 12 eave columns the third; the gold columns painted in raised gilt work on vermilion; two rings of wall round the altar, gates on four sides of the inner one [stated: visitbeijing]. Three concentric circular platforms of white marble with carved balustrades; spouts carved as clouds on the lowest tier, phoenixes on the middle, dragons on the top; inside, a round dragon-and-phoenix stone in the middle of the floor and a carved throne on a pedestal with a gilt screen holding the tablet of Heaven [stated: Travel China Guide]. 36 m across and 38 m high, wholly of wood [stated: Wikipedia, Temple of Heaven].
- **Ours** (`qinian`, `src/styles/qing/tiantan.js`): the altar's three tiers of marble at the stated diameters and height (1.87 m each), paved with brick, their balustrades running round with carved posts every 1.6 m — lotus heads on the lowest tier, phoenixes on the middle, dragons on the top (the posts after the palace's; the stated spouts not modelled) [inferred]; stairs on the four sides, on the south three flights with a carved slab between [inferred: as the palace terrace]. The hall on a low marble base: twelve eave columns (radius 14.6 m) with lattice doors between, the south's middle bay open under a lattice transom; the painted frieze and a ring of bracket sets; the green roof; twelve gold columns (radius 10.9 m) carrying the second drum — red wall with a band of lattice windows — its frieze, brackets and the yellow roof; the four dragon-well pillars (19 m, 1.2 m, banded in gilt) carrying the top drum, the blue roof (glazed, its tile rolls modelled) and the gilt finial at 37.4 m over the floor; inside, the painted ceilings over each ring with gilt rings at the top, the round stone in the floor, the shrine of the tablet on its dais against the north with the gilt screen behind, an offering table with three bronze vessels before it [inferred: the drums' heights and the roofs' profiles from photographs; the interior much simplified]. Longfeng hexi on its friezes [inferred].
- **Placing.** The real temple lies about 3 km south of the palace, east of the central axis; here it is brought in east of the avenue south of the moat (centre at (72, 280)), as the palace itself is compressed [inferred]. A square wall 108 m a side (red, green-glazed coping) [inferred: the coping's colour] encloses the altar's brick-paved court, with a gate to the avenue on the west (the inner wall had gates on four sides [stated]) and on the south the Gate of Prayer for Good Harvests, a hall of five bays on a marble platform [inferred: size]. Round the wall a belt of old arborvitae (Platycladus orientalis) in place of the houses [inferred: the cypress groves of the Temple of Heaven, general knowledge]. The lanes of the hutong stop at the belt.
- **Not built:** the Imperial Vault of Heaven, the Circular Mound, the side halls, the outer wall (the Danbi bridge-way and the court's four gates: §29).

## 15. Camels and carts (L3)

- **Camel strings.** From Mentougou to the west, camel teams of "at least five and up to seven camels" carried coal or groceries into central Beijing, their bells heard from 4 am, returning in the afternoon [stated: China Daily, 2025, quoting a Moshikou resident]; photographs of the early 20th century show camel trains on the road to the Western Hills [stated: Historical Photographs of China, Bristol, Bk04-28]. Ours: five strings of five or six camels — on the avenue, the ring street, the east street and a lane — each with its pack saddle and two bales, a bronze bell hung under the neck, a rope from each nose back to the saddle before it, the drover walking at the head with his staff, a mate at the tail of some [inferred: numbers and places]. ## 16. The shop street (townscape)

- **Place.** Shops stood along the great street south of the palace's gate and the Qianmen quarter; Qianmen Street in photographs of the early 20th century is a busy market with shop fronts, signs and stalls [stated: Historical Photographs of China, Bristol, "Qianmen Street" and "A busy market south of Qianmen"]. Ours: the avenue south of the moat's ring street has shops on its west side from the ring street to the town's edge, and on the east side below the temple's belt of trees, 3–4 shops of 5–9 m to each block between two lanes, their fronts on the avenue across a paved apron [inferred: the place, the scale; Qing shop streets were lined with shops on both sides of the way].
- **Signs.** Old Beijing shop signs were hand-painted and hung "in all conceivable shapes and colours", many carrying no characters because most people could not read; they dangled outside the front [stated: beijing-postcards, "Old Beijing Shop Signs"; Western travellers quoted there]. Ours: a gilt-lettered name board across the facade, a pair of tall red signboards on stone feet at the kerb, and on a bracket the hanging sign of the trade — a red teapot (tea), a long indigo flag (wine), gourds and a painted plaster sign (medicine), coloured streamers (cloth), a great round black board (a pawnshop), paper lanterns, a cloth shoe, a hung measure with a banner (grain) [inferred: which sign for which trade, from the general lore of Chinese trade signs]. The characters on the boards are strokes, not words.
- **Fronts.** Open timber fronts: posts, the high counter (guitai), plank shutters stacked at the end posts, shelves on the back wall; over the front eave a painted board facade standing clear of the roof under its own tile cap (the "pailou front" of old Beijing shops); some shops of two storeys with a lattice gallery; mat awnings of reeds on poles; red lanterns under the eave [inferred: from photographs of the Qianmen shops; no measured source at hand]. Behind each block of shops a walled yard with a lean-to store and a tree.
- **Ground.** The avenue is narrowed to a commercial street: fronts 8 m from the axis, a carriageway of packed earth 8.6 m broad with a worn 3 m strip of granite slabs down its middle (the old imperial way, slabs lost in places) and short cart ruts, and raised walks (0.14 m) of bluestone behind a granite kerb before the shops; the yards paved with brick [inferred: widths, after Qianmen Street in the photographs, which shows a street of 12–20 m between fronts; the stone strip after the Qing practice of a stone-paved central way on the main streets]. Walks carry benches, pots of plants, baskets and crates.
- **Fronts, richer.** Under each front eave a carved and gilt fascia board (guayanban) with painted cartouches and a scalloped lower edge; counters with painted panels and the goods of the trade; lattice transoms; the standing signboards on two-step stone feet with a little tile cap, gilt finial and red ribbons; lanterns on timber brackets with gilt caps and tassels; the mat awning lowered to a pole line 2.5 m high [inferred: from the photographs; forms after the general lore of Chinese shop fronts].
- **Booths (shops.js).** "Many of the shop signs did not have any Chinese characters on them, because most people did not know how to read"; "they came in all conceivable shapes and colours" [stated: Beijing Postcards, The Code Language of the Capital]. Ours: booths of ten trades in turn — tea, wine, cloth, medicine, pawn, grain, fruit, roast chestnuts, books, pottery — each with its goods and a hanging sign on a pole at the front corner (the red wine flag, streamers of cloth, the red gourd of the medicine shop, the black disc of the pawnshop, the grain measure, the kettle of the tea seller) [inferred: the shapes, after the general custom; the source names none]. They stand along both edges of the avenue from the moat's ring street to the town's edge, also before the shop fronts (the street market of Qianmen in the photographs), clear of the lanes' mouths, the path to the temple's gate and the doors of the tea house and the pharmacy [inferred]..

## 17. The hutong houses, wells and trees (townscape)

- **Gates by rank.** The gate of the siheyuan tells the owner's rank: "the higher the style of the gate, the higher the family's standing" [stated: ourchinastory]. Ours follow the table of the Chinese sources: Guangliang gate, the door leaf hung at the middle post line, a deep porch before it, a raised plinth, for officials of rank; Jinzhu gate, the leaf on the "golden" post line between the middle and the front eave posts, among the first three grades; Manzi gate, the leaf hung straight on the front eave posts, no porch; Ruyi gate, a wall built between the front eave posts with the doorway in it, the commonest in Beijing, door pins often carved "ruyi"; the wall gate, a doorway cut in the wall, the lowest [stated: Sohu, "北京四合院大门" table]. "Guangliang gates have a wide space before the door, Jinzhu gates less, Manzi gates none" [stated: same]. The gate stands at the south-east corner of the court [stated: Raider State pressbooks, Beijing siheyuan; Wikipedia]; the higher gates stand on a higher plinth and their posts are taller than the south row [stated: Sohu]. Ours: the gate block rises 0.7 m (Guangliang) and 0.45 m (Jinzhu) above the south row; plinths of 0.36, 0.27, 0.15, 0.15 and 0.05 m with steps from the lane and down to the yard; red doors for the two highest, black for the rest [inferred: the colours]. Which lot gets which gate follows distance from the palace (officials and the rich lived near the Imperial City) with noise: about 6 Guangliang, 14 Jinzhu, 180 Ruyi, 31 Manzi, 137 wall gates among 368 houses [inferred: proportions; the Ruyi gate "most common" is stated, as above].
- **Door pins and drum stones.** Door pins (menzan), hexagonal posts standing out of the lintel, "symbolise the owner's rank: four pins for the 1st–4th rank officials, two for the 5th–7th" [stated: ourchinastory]; Guangliang and Jinzhu gates have four, the Manzi and Ruyi two, the wall gate none. Drum stones (menduan), a pair beside the door, "round ones shaped like a war drum for military families, square ones like an official seal for civil officials" [stated: ourchinastory]; ours round at the Guangliang and Manzi gates, square at the Jinzhu and Ruyi [inferred: which gate which].
- **The court's plan.** The screen wall (yingbi) faces the gate inside it and "a zhaobi stands outside" [stated: Raider State pressbooks]; the hanging-flower gate (chuihua men) on the axis separates the front court from the inner court "to protect family privacy", its eaves hanging in the air [stated: same]; "ordinary siheyuan have two main courtyards", nobles several in depth [stated: same]. Ours: one court for the poorer lots, two for the richer (the hanging-flower gate with its two posts, hanging heads, little rolled roof and open red doors in a wall across), the main house of three or five bays facing south, the south row (daozuo) round the gate, the side houses; plain grey brick and tile [stated: "ordinary siheyuans use plain grey bricks and tiles" — Raider State]. Lots are shortened to 22.4 m deep (real ones are deeper) [inferred]. The covered walks (chaoshou youlang) of the grandest houses are not modelled.
- **Roofs.** "Relatively simple Qingshui roof ridges with few decorations" [stated: Wikipedia, Beijing siheyuan]; ours: flush gable ends (yingshan, the commonest house roof of the north) with the rolled ridge, its ends turned up on the grander houses; eave tiles; a brick chimney on the back slope of each building in the near rings (coal heating) [inferred: yingshan, chimneys]. The wall tops carry a corbelled brick course and a coping of grey tile, the foot a stone course [inferred].
- **Trees and yards.** The court "is spacious, with trees and flowers planted in it and goldfish kept in a tank" [stated: Wikipedia]; "awning, fish jar, pomegranate tree" [stated: Aboluowang, in §11]. In the autumn shown, ours are the trees of the yards at true heights: the jujube (zao; about 5 m), the persimmon and the crab-apple (haitang; 5–6 m), and in the larger courts a pagoda tree (Sophora japonica, huai; 15–20 m), each in a square brick-edged pit, singly or in a pair either side of the path; old pagoda trees stand on the lanes' edges and one in each shops' yard [inferred: species and placing; persimmons red in the yard are a long-standing sight of Beijing courts — Lao She planted two in his yard in 1953, "Red Persimmon Courtyard", BJD 2026; the Qing court cannot be dated from that]. The plant library now has the pagoda tree (`pagoda_tree`) and the persimmon (`persimmon`), which ground.js uses for the courts' and lanes' trees; only the crab-apple is an apple.
- **Yard things.** The fish jar of glazed ware on a brick stand, rows of potted plants, a stack of firewood, a pile of coal balls [inferred: autumn stores].
- **Wells.** "Nearly every hutong had one, since most residents drank well water"; most wells were bitter or brackish, sweet-water wells specially valued; the "Jingshi fangxiang zhi gao" lists nearly 1,000 public wells; hutong names keep them (Jing'er, Sanyan Jing, Sijing, Tianshui Jing) [stated: china.com, Beijing hutong wells]. Ours: 16 public wells in the lanes: a granite well head with a wide rim, a pair of posts with a windlass and bucket, a stone apron and a wooden trough [inferred: the form].
- **Lanes and walls, aged.** The hutong are packed-earth lanes with a crown of dust, ruts and paler dust drifts along the walls; the lot walls and the south row's lane face carry a paler dusty foot of wind-blown earth, salt bloom, patches of newer brick and of lime wash, lost bricks, copings in pieces (some sunk, some gone) with tufts of dry weed, and weeds on ridges and eaves of the roofs (no moss) [inferred: from the dry climate]. The gates carry paper door gods on the open leaves, brass knockers (pushou) and gilt studs on the great gates, red couplets (duilian) and a horizontal scroll over the door, and the great gates a granite mounting block (shangmashi) in the lane [stated: such blocks and drum stones belong to officials' gates — ourchinastory, above; inferred: the placing].
- **Weathering.** A dry northern climate: `damp` 0.06, so the foot of the walls is dusty and grimy, with no moss, algae or rain streaks; the "dusty" weather already in the climate gives the yellow haze of the spring winds [inferred]. Lanes and yards are dust-coloured earth and brick.
- **Moat.** The moat keeps its granite-faced banks with a low brick wall and coping, the paved band to the palace wall, the willows of its outer bank, and bridges at the three gates; the palace wall and its four corner towers belong to the landmark plan.

## 18. The imperial ramps and the stair balustrades (L3)

- **Ramps.** At the centre of each flight the stair is a single inclined slab of marble (the yu lu or danbi) carved in relief; the great one behind the Hall of Preserving Harmony is 16.57 m long [stated: benjielayug, §4 above]; the Palace Museum's carved ramps show "dragons among clouds" over "sea waves and mountains" (the "river and cliff" pattern) [stated: general; Travel China Guide, Hall of Preserving Harmony]. Ours (`src/styles/qing/ramp.js`): every slab (the three tiers front and back, the Gate of Supreme Harmony, the Temple's altar) carries a raised border, a foot of two rows of breaking waves with three mountain peaks, and above them one dragon (two on slabs wider than 5 m, mirrored), each rising among cloud scrolls with a gilt flaming pearl before its head; built once per size and placed on the slope [inferred: the layout and the number of dragons].
- **Rakes.** The flights' sides carry inclined balustrades (the xiangdao rails): a stone cheek following the steps' nosings, panel, cloud band, vase balusters and handrail running up the slope between posts every 1.6 m with dragon and phoenix heads by turns, and at the foot a pier carrying a drum stone (baogushi) [inferred: from photographs of the palace's stairs; the drum stones are the usual end of such rails].
- The ramp behind the Hall of Preserving Harmony is 16.5 m long, 3 m wide, 1.7 m high, over 200 tons, carved with 9 dragons among floating clouds, sculpted in the Ming and re-sculpted in the Qing, brought some 70 km from Fangshan [stated: chinatravel.com].

## 19. The Meridian Gate, the court's gates and paving, the Temple's caisson and furnishings (L3)

- **The Meridian Gate (Wumen).** The gate "has two protruding side arms, derived from ancient que towers"; each arm has "a 13-bay building with a single eave" connecting two pyramidal-roofed pavilions; five arches, the three central close together and the two flanking further apart, the middle one for the Emperor alone (the Empress once, on her wedding day; the top three scholars of the examinations left through it), the four side ones for officials and servants; the superstructure is five buildings, the "Five Phoenix Turrets"; the central one is nine bays wide under double eaves, the side ones single-eaved [stated: Wikipedia, Meridian Gate]. Ours: a base of red wall 12 m high, its middle block 12 m deep with the five passages (at x 0, ±9, ±27; the middle one 6 m wide and 8.5 m high), at each end an arm running 25 m forward of the middle block's front (the south moat is interrupted by the forecourt's causeway, §27, so that they project their real distance) [inferred: size]; on it the nine-bay hall (60 m, double eaves, nine ridge figures), at each side a seven-bay single-eaved corridor hall to a double-eaved pyramidal pavilion, and on each arm the thirteen-bay single-eaved wing hall running south, facing the forecourt [stated: Wikipedia — "a 13-bay building with a single eave"; the bay width (about 1.9 m, to fit 25 m) and one pavilion to an arm are ours]; the leaves of the five passages stand open with 81 gilt nails each.
- **Side gates.** The side gates of the Gate of Supreme Harmony's court and of the Gate of Heavenly Purity's wall carry gatehouses of five bays on the wall over the passage, the first with marble aprons either side [inferred: from photographs of the palace's gate towers].
- **Paving.** The "golden bricks" of the halls' floors are square, in three sizes (54.4, 64 and 70.4 cm), blue-grey and metallic-sounding [stated: Shine, "The gold that lies beneath your feet"]; the halls' floors keep the 0.64 m size. The court is grey brick with the white stone way along the axis [stated: general]; ours, see §27.
- **The Hall of Prayer's caisson.** "殿顶中心为龙凤藻井，分为3层皆为圆形，层层收缩" (a dragon-and-phoenix caisson at the roof's centre, three round tiers, each smaller than the one below) [stated: Sohu]. Ours: three blue drums of 5.4, 4.0 and 2.6 m radius, each with gilt rims and a ring of little brackets, gilt clouds and phoenix crests round the second, a dome with sixteen gilt ribs and the gilt dragon coiled under it, head down at the centre with its pearl [inferred: sizes, form of the dragon]. The Imperial Vault's caisson is "two gilded dragons playing with a pearl" [stated: visitbeijing].
- **The floor stone and the seat.** "大殿地面中心是一块圆形大理石…墨色纹理天然勾画出一对龙凤，称'龙凤石'"; north of it a stone sumeru seat with a carved dragon throne, behind it a nanmu screen with golden dragons, the tablet "皇天上帝" in the middle; before the seat the prayer-board table and the incense-and-silk table, and before the companion tablets east and west the tables for food and for wine vessels [stated: Sohu]. The furnaces "由生铁铸造，镂空六足" (cast iron, six open legs) [stated: Sohu]: a pair on the top tier before the hall, ours a bowl on six legs [inferred: form]. The side halls (peidian) are "单檐歇山顶，蓝色琉璃瓦" (single-eaved gable-and-hip, blue glazed tiles) [stated: Sohu]: ours two, five bays, at the north-east and north-west of the court [inferred: positions; the real ones flank the hall's court].
