# Mughal Agra, 1632–1653 — dossier

Settlement id: `mughal-agra` · Style: `mughal` · Region: India (the Yamuna plain) · Code: `src/settlements/mughal-agra/`
Status of this dossier: L1–L2 (the Taj Mahal complex: riverfront terrace, plinth, mausoleum, minarets, mosque and jawab, charbagh with channels and pool, walls and towers, great gate, forecourt; the town of Taj Ganj; ground, planting, weather, music). L3 recorded for later: the pietra dura and calligraphy in full (the faces' bands, spandrels and dado are built, §4), the jali screens and the cenotaphs inside, the forecourt's bazaars and caravanserais in full, the subsidiary tombs, the river ghats. Phase D (§11): the two northern katras of the Taj Ganj with a furnished cell each, a furnished house, the houses' doors, windows and weathered feet, caravan animals, water carriers and porters.

## 1. The real example

- **Landmark:** the Taj Mahal, the mausoleum of Mumtaz Mahal on the southern bank of the Yamuna at Agra, built 1632–1653 for Shah Jahan [stated: Wikipedia; visiearth]; the complex about 580 × 300 m with a garden of 300 × 300 m [stated: archetravel].
- **Moment shown:** completed, 1653.

## 3. Orientation

The axis runs north–south: the tomb on the river at the north end, the gate on the south. [stated]

## 4. The style

White Makrana marble for the tomb [stated: visiearth]; red sandstone for the terrace, walls, gate, mosque and jawab; bulbous double dome with lotus crown and brass finial [stated: dream-it24]; chhatris; minarets leaning slightly outward [stated: Wikipedia] — ours stand straight.

**The tomb chamber inside (L3, built).** An octagon of 7.3 m sides, entered from each face in design but only by the south door from the garden; the walls about 25 m high under a false inner dome with a sun motif [stated: middleheight; thetilesofindia]. Double-height, each face with two storeys of niches; the niches on the axes have jali screens on the outer face that let in light; muqarnas at the upper level turn the octagon toward the dome's ring [stated: Archnet]. The dado frames and spandrels carry pietra dura of yellow marble, jasper and jade [stated: slideshare after Koch]. The octagonal marble screen was set up in 1643 in place of the first one of enamelled gold; each side has three panels, one opening to the cenotaph [stated: tajmahal.gov.in; Archnet]. Mumtaz's cenotaph is exactly in the centre of the hall [stated: tajmahal.gov.in]; Shah Jahan's was added after his death (1666), so in 1653 only hers stands [inferred from the dates]. Ours: walls to 20 m and a flat ring to the inner dome (our mausoleum is a little smaller); the axial niches glazed with light behind a marble lattice; carved dado panels with flower bosses; inlay of red, green, gold and blue stones in the spandrels; the screen as a grid of slender bars between posts; a vestibule from the south iwan's door to the chamber.

**The screen and the cenotaph (third pass, built).** "The screen is octagonal. Each side of the octagon is divided into three by marble frames"; "The corners are fortified by posts ending in kalasha finials"; the frames are filled with jalis — the only ones in the Taj pierced in plant arabesques rather than geometric forms; it is "topped with ornamental crenellations, kanguras", of vase-shaped elements and acanthus volutes; its flowers are "inlaid in semi-precious stones"; "the entrance arch in the centre of the south side" rises above the screen, with a matching closed arch on the north. "Each cenotaph consists of a single block of stone, shaped like a sarcophagus, set on a stepped plinth", the plinth on a wider platform whose upper surface has "a framed flowery scrollwork pattern"; on Mumtaz's, "inscribed on the top and the sides of the block are Quran'ic verses in formal Sulus script" [stated: tajmahal.gov.in, Interior decoration]. No hanging lamp is recorded for 1653 (the lamp hanging there now was given by Lord Curzon in the twentieth century), so none is built. Ours: the screen 1.9 m high, each panel a slab of marble pierced with staggered almond-shaped openings and little lozenges between them, so that what stands reads as a net of stems and leaves; a plinth rail and a top rail on each panel, with a row of inlaid flowers (carnelian, jade, lapis, yellow, jade leaves) along both faces of each; the corner posts turned, with a carnelian ring and a kalasha (a pot with a lid and a bud) on top; a cresting of vase-shaped kanguras; in the middle of the south side the opening under a seven-lobed cusped arch on tall stiles rising 0.75 m above the screen with a finial, the north side's twin over a jali. The cenotaph: a platform 2.7 × 4.5 m framed by a dark line, a scroll of inlaid flowers round it; three steps, each face with a row of flower sprays (four petals round a yellow heart, jade leaves and stem); the block with a foot, sloping sides and a flat top bearing a raised tablet, a band of black letters in a frame on each long side and rows of letters on the tablet [inferred: the heights, the piercing pattern, the cresting's outline, the steps' sizes, the letters as abstract strokes]. Shah Jahan's cenotaph (1666) is still left out.

**The faces' inlay, the mosque and jawab, the fountains (L2–L3, built).** "The calligraphy is made by jasper inlaid in white marble panels"; "higher panels are written slightly larger to reduce the skewing effect when viewing from below"; the archway spandrels carry "pietra dura inlays of highly stylised" vines, flowers and fruits; the lower walls have "white marble dados that have been sculpted with realistic bas relief depictions of flowers and vines"; the inlay stones are "yellow marble, jasper and jade" [stated: indianmarvels]. The mosque is of red sandstone, its outside "covered in pietra dura work, especially across its spandrels, and calligraphy", with four octagonal towers and three domes on its roof; on the jawab "instead of Koranic inscriptions, there are wonderful flower designs", in "white marble on the red sandstone background" [stated: ednet student portfolio — a weak source, kept for the facade only]. At the crossing of the walks is "a raised platform (chabutra) of white marble with an ornamental pool (hauz) containing five fountains" [stated: tajmahal.gov.in]; "five fountains are located within the tank, one at each of its four corners and one in its center", and "a line of equidistant water fountains runs down the center of the nahr" [stated: CPREEC ecoheritage].
Ours: on every pishtaq face of the tomb and the great gate, an inscription band framing the great arch — white marble field (on the red gate), dark borders, black letters (baselines, uprights, bowls and dots) growing larger with height; a vine of inlaid stones following the arch's extrados with flowers of red, yellow and blue stone and jade leaves; a rosette of red and green stones in each spandrel with a spray of stems ending in flowers; the dado in panels framed in dark stone, each with a flowering plant in white relief, between the openings [inferred: composition after photographs]. The mosque's facade with the inscription band round its central arch, the jawab's with a scroll of flowers instead, both with inlaid spandrels, the dado, white marble frames round the side arches and a white band under the parapet; their arches now open into bays with dark doors at the back (the hall's mass filled them before). The central tank is now a rim round a basin (it was a solid block that hid the water) with its five fountains; fifteen jets at 8 m intervals down each half of the north–south channel [inferred: count and spacing]; each fountain a marble lotus with a copper nozzle and its jet, the jet of broken white water, part translucent (`water_jet`; a white paint before). The inlay of the spandrels, the bands of flowers, the chamber's niches, the screen's rails and panels and the cenotaph is now in the palette's inlay stones — carnelian (red), jade (green), yellow jasper, lapis (blue) — in place of the coloured plasters that stood in for them, after "yellow marble, jasper and jade" [stated: indianmarvels]; the gilt and painted sun of the inner dome stays as it was [inferred].

**The iwans' recesses (second wave, built).** The arched recesses of the pishtaqs carry muqarnas [stated: MIT Dome, G. E. Kidder Smith collection, "Taj Mahal, exterior, detail of muqarnas motifs on arched recess, or pishtaq"]. Ours: every iwan and niche of the tomb and the great gate is now a closed recess, half an octagon in plan (the back narrower than the opening), its walls rising to the springing, two tiers of muqarnas cells along the springing, a pointed half-dome meeting the arch at the face with a net of thin ribs over it; on the back wall a wooden door with gilt bosses below and a marble jali (over darkness) above, framed in dark stone (the raised niches have the jali alone); a blind panel framed in dark stone on each slanting side; the south iwan, the way in, is a rectangular vaulted recess before its door [inferred: the plan, the proportions and the net pattern after photographs; before, the recesses opened onto the dark interior].

**The landmark seen close (round two, built).** The pishtaq recesses' muqarnas "of the arched recess, or pishtaq" [stated: MIT Dome, Kidder Smith collection] are now rows of faceted cells standing out of the half-dome, every other row over the joints of the one below, their relief fading toward the arch (eight rows of twenty on the great iwans, six of twelve in the niches) in place of a smooth shell with thin ribs [inferred: counts and relief after photographs]. The inscription bands' lettering is bolder and denser, in four letter forms on a baseline ribbon (tall uprights with hooks, bowls with dots, loops, stroke and two dots), still growing with height after "higher panels are written slightly larger" [stated: indianmarvels]; outside the bands, where the niches leave room, a narrow border of carnelian and jade lozenges with yellow hearts between dark lines [inferred]. The dado panels on the faces and on the plinth carry flowering plants in relief — a tuft of blades, a swaying stem, lens-shaped leaves with midribs, a side stem with a bud, a lily, a poppy or an iris — "sculpted with realistic bas relief depictions of flowers and vines" [stated: indianmarvels; the species are inferred after photographs]. The four roof kiosks and the minarets' crowns are open chhatris: a moulded octagonal base, eight piers with capitals, eight pointed arches cut through thin panels, a ring-beam and a chhajja, merlons, a ribbed dome on a lotus collar, a bud and a spike [inferred after photographs]. The great dome's finial is two stacked pots, a crescent with horns pointing up and a spike with three rings, gilt [stated: Wikipedia, the finial combines Persian and Hindu motifs; the form after photographs].

## 5. The town

Flat-roofed houses of two or three storeys, plastered brick or red sandstone, with a jharokha balcony over the door. [inferred]

**The forecourt's bazaar streets and gates (third pass, built).** "The Taj complex is now entered through one of three gates leading into the Jilaukhana, or forecourt"; "Two identical bazaar streets lead from the east and west gates to the Jilaukhana"; "The streets are lined with rows of small unconnected rectangular cells without windows", "fronted by an arcaded verandah with multi-cusped arches supported by columns of distinctive Shah Jahan type"; the Khawasspuras (attendants' quarters) on the north against the garden wall and the two tomb complexes (Saheli Burj) on the south; outside, a short flight up to the Taj Ganj [stated: tajmahal.gov.in, Outlying buildings]. Visitors "would dismount from their horses or elephants" there; the cells "were used to sell goods from when the Taj was built until 1996"; a caravanserai on the north-west was "Katra Omar Khan" [stated: Wikipedia, Origins and architecture]. Ours: in our shortened forecourt (250 × 56 m) a bazaar street 9 m wide runs along its middle from a gate in each end wall to 48 m from the axis, lined on both sides by 22 cells 3.2 m wide and 4 m deep, each with a door 1.4 × 2.5 m in a plastered front, its wooden leaves folded back, dark inside; before them a verandah 3 m deep on a plinth of 0.35 m with two steps, its front an arcade of seven-lobed cusped pointed arches on Shah Jahani columns (a cusped square base, a faceted tapering shaft with two rings, a bell capital, an impost block), a white marble line and two rosettes over each arch, a chhajja on brackets and a crenellated parapet; the east, west and south gates of red sandstone 20 m wide and 11 m high, a pointed arch one walks through on each face framed by a scroll of inlaid flowers in white marble, the passage between solid piers under a plastered ceiling, merlons and four domed kiosks on the roof; a paved street outward from each side gate [inferred: the cell, verandah and gate dimensions, the lobes, the column's profile, the gates' form]. The Khawasspuras, the Saheli Burj and the caravanserais are left for later.

**Life (third pass, built).** The forecourt was where visitors "would dismount from their horses or elephants and assemble in style", and its cells were used "to sell goods" [stated: Wikipedia, Origins and architecture]. Ours (`life.js`): on about two-thirds of the verandahs goods set out before the doors — clusters of water pots and dark pots, sacks of grain, baskets of cloth — and on most of them the keeper sitting cross-legged; buyers and passers-by in the streets, the men in the jama (a long robe) with a sash and a turban, the women robed and veiled; six saddled horses held by grooms in the forecourt and a few visitors before the great gate; thatch-roofed barges and rowing boats on the Yamuna below the riverfront terrace [inferred: the goods, the dress after Mughal paintings, the boats; no elephant figure exists yet].

## 6. Ground and water

The level plain; the riverbank falling to the Yamuna on the north. (`ground.js`)

## 7. Planting

The flowerbeds are "divided by stone-paved raised pathways", sixteen to each quarter; the trees are "either that of Cyprus (signifying death) or of the fruit bearing type (signifying life)", arranged symmetrically [stated: tajmahal.org.uk "Taj Garden"]. **Built (second wave):** raised walks of red sandstone 3 m wide with marble kerbs, 0.55 m above the beds, three each way in every quarter (at 46, 80 and 114 m from the axes) so that each quarter holds sixteen sunken beds, and a walk 4 m wide round the inside of the walls; a line of cypresses along the edge of every walk, fruit trees in the beds behind them (apple trees stand in for the orchard), grass [inferred: the walks' height and the spacing of the planting]; neem and banyan in the town.
**The riverfront (second wave):** the terrace's face to the Yamuna built as an arcade of blind arches of red sandstone, crenellated, between the corner towers, from the river's bed to a parapet over the terrace [inferred: articulation after photographs of the riverfront].

## 11. Phase D: the Taj Ganj's caravanserais, a furnished house, the houses' fronts, the street life (fourth pass)

**The katras.** The Taj Ganj — first "Mumtazabad" — took "the characteristic form of a square divided by two cross axial streets with gates to the four cardinal points"; "Bazaars lined each street and the resultant squares to each corner housed the caravanserais": "the north western one was known as Katra Omar Khan", "the north eastern as Katra Fulel (Perfume Market)", "the south western as Katra Resham (Silk Market)" and "the south-eastern as Katra Jogidas"; built first to house the builders, then for trade whose revenue kept the tomb; contemporary sources dwell on the northern half, likely the only one imperially funded and the finer [stated: Wikipedia, Origins and architecture of the Taj Mahal]. A sarai of the same years and patronage — Amanat Khan's, the Taj's calligrapher, dated 1640–41 — is almost square (168 × 165 m) with gateways in the middle of two sides, rooms about 3.5 m square with vaulted ceilings, a three-domed mosque and a well 2 m across in the court [stated: Wikipedia, Sarai Amanat Khan]. Ours (`katra`, `layout.js` `KATRAS`): the two northern katras either side of the bazaar street on the axis, 44 × 31 m each (much smaller than Amanat Khan's — our town is compressed): on the north and south rows of vaulted cells 3.6 m wide behind arcaded verandahs of cusped arches on Shah Jahani columns (the forecourt's bazaar row, §5); east and west walls of red sandstone with blind arches and merlons, the gate in the middle of the wall on the street, its arch framed in inlaid flowers under kiosks; in the court a well with its parapet, posts, beam and pulley, a stone trough, bales stacked by a verandah, a charpoy; one cell of Katra Omar Khan and one of Katra Fulel furnished as a merchant's lodging — plastered inside, a dhurrie, a charpoy with its quilt and pillow, a saddlebag and bales along the back, a hookah, the water pot on its stand and a brass lota, an oil lamp on a ledge by the door; camels (some unloaded), zebu bullocks and a cart in the courts, merchants seated among sacks on the verandahs, a water carrier (bhishti) with his goatskin, porters with head loads, a keeper with his staff (`life.js`) [inferred: the plan, the size, the mosque left out, the furnishing; the hookah: tobacco smoking was established at the Mughal court from the early 1600s — not sourced here].

**The furnished house.** Ours (`haveli_open`, `layout.js` `HAVELI_OPEN`, built with the shared courtyard house `src/styles/mughal/courthouse.js`): on the bazaar street south of the katras, the street door on the west in a frame of red sandstone with studded leaves and knockers into a vestibule whose inner door is set aside; the court paved in red sandstone round a raised platform (chabutra), the water pots (matka) on their stands, a charpoy set out; on the north the dalan, open to the court by three arches, each in a frame of red sandstone with marble rosettes in the spandrels, a chhajja on brackets over them and a crenellated parapet; in it a striped dhurrie, a takht (a low platform of wood) spread with a carpet with a long bolster (gao takiya) and cushions, a hookah, a brass tray on its stand, oil lamps and ewers on ledges; on the east a room with two charpoys, a chest and the bedding folded on it; the master seated on the takht, a woman by the water pots [inferred: the house type and its furnishing after Mughal paintings].

**The houses' fronts.** Every `haveli` now has its door in a frame of red sandstone with two studded leaves and a step, small latticed windows in red sandstone frames high in the front, and the foot of its walls darkened to 0.6–1.1 m by the lane's dust (DESIGN §5) [inferred].

## 12. Batch pass: the town's fronts, the inlay workshop, the living town

**The streets and houses.** Taj Ganj was laid out as a square "divided by two cross axial streets with gates to the four cardinal points", the bazaars lining the streets [stated: Wikipedia, Origins and architecture of the Taj Mahal]. Ours (`layout.js` `STREETS`, `houses()`): the bazaar street on the axis from the forecourt's gate (16 m between the katras), the cross street beyond the katras, the two bazaar streets continued out from the forecourt's east and west gates, and a street north–south at each side of the town; the houses stand shoulder to shoulder along them with their fronts on the street (a hand's width between party walls), and more loosely behind and beside the garden, each turned to its nearest street [inferred: after the plan of the Taj Ganj and the havelis of Agra; lot widths 5.5–10 m, depths 10–13 m].

**The house** (`src/styles/mughal/townhouse.js`; two or three storeys of plastered brick, ground floor 3.5 m, upper floors 3.1 m [inferred]): at the floor line a continuous chhajja, a sloping stone eave on brackets, a typical element of Mughal houses; a jharokha, a bracketed oriel with latticed (jali) panels, a slab roof and a small dome, over the door or the middle of the shop [stated: the jharokha over the door, the sources of §5; the form inferred after photographs of Agra havelis]; lattice windows with sills and lintels on each upper floor; a cornice and a parapet with a row of merlons (kangura); on the roof the stair head (mumty) and on some a chhatri. On the bazaar streets the ground floor is an open shop: a raised platform (thara) 0.35 m high behind a verandah of columns under a wooden beam, shelves at the back, goods by the keeper's trade (cloth in bolts, rolled carpets, brass and copper, spices and grain, pots), and on two thirds of them a striped cloth awning over the street. The doors, sandstone frames and darkened feet of §11 are kept on the houses without shops.

**The inlay workshop** (`workshop.js`, `WORKSHOP` in `layout.js`, `rooms`: `inlay workshop`). The Taj's inlay was done by craftsmen Shah Jahan brought, the technique, known in Italy as pietra dura, having come to India with them; Agra's inlay craftsmen "are believed to be descendants of the craftsmen who built the Taj Mahal" [stated: Outlook India, "Parchin Kari"]. Stages of the work: the design traced on the marble, hundreds of tiny pieces of stone (agate, lapis, malachite, turquoise, carnelian, jade) cut by hand on a wheel, test-fitted and their outline engraved with a stylus, a groove carved a couple of millimetres deep, the pieces set in with adhesive flush with the surface, polished; the work is divided among craftsmen each specialised in one stage; a flower or leaf is often made of several pieces [stated: Google Arts & Culture, "Stone Crafts of Agra"; Outlook India]. The 17th-century tools (a bow-driven grinding wheel, hand chisels, a stylus) [inferred: the modern shops use an electric wheel and diamond tools]. Ours: a hall open to the axis street, 11 × 12 m on a sandstone plinth, a beam on three columns, a lightwell in the roof; two stone-cutters at wheels with their water pots, three inlayers at low benches with a marble panel part set with flowers of carnelian, jade, lapis and yellow jasper (palette `inlay_*`), a tray of cut stones and a pot of adhesive each, stacks of marble slabs and panels leaning on them, a finished panel in a sandstone frame on the back wall, the master on his cushion with a hookah and a tray; five craftsmen and the master seated on the mats (`life.js`).

**Living town** (`movers.js`, `fauna.js`, `smoke.js`, `life.js`). Walkers: men in the jama with a turban, merchants with a shoulder cloth, craftsmen and porters, women in long robes with the head veiled, water carriers; 40 by day and 5 at night, on the town's streets and the forecourt (the Taj garden's raised walks are left out: they stand above the plain). Keepers sit on the platforms of two thirds of the shops, a customer at some. Washermen (dhobi) stand at flat stones on the Yamuna's bank east of the terrace with cloth spread on the sand — the bank at Agra was a washing and bathing place [inferred]. Animals: crows over the town, pigeons and swifts round the dome and gate, sparrows on the paving, an egret and a heron on the river's bank. Smoke: kitchens through the roofs at the three meals, cook-shop steam in the bazaars, incense at the tomb's door.

**Not yet:** moving boats on the Yamuna (static ones stand at the bank), ghats with steps to the water (the bank's slope is steeper than treads can follow without reshaping the ground), the Taj garden's visitors, furnished shop interiors beyond the shelf, the caravanserais Katra Resham and Jogidas (south).

## 13. Deepening pass: the garden's people, the river's life

**Garden and plinth.** The garden had no visitors: now gentlemen with an attendant and veiled women with a maid stroll the lanes either side of the channel, pilgrims sit on the great tank's rim, gardeners (mali) carry water in pots on their heads, hoe the beds and sweep, sweepers work on the plinth, and readers of the Qur'an sit and kneel on carpets before the south door of the tomb (Qur'an recitation at the tomb is attested for the Taj from Shah Jahan's time: the endowment paid for readers [stated: Wikipedia, Taj Mahal; the placing inferred]). Guards stand under the gate's arch. Planting: the beds hold fruit trees and flowering shrubs, not apple — the Mughal char bagh was planted with mango, lemon, pomegranate and plantain, with cypresses for death along the walks [stated: tajmahal.gov.in, The Taj gardens; Wikipedia, Taj Mahal gardens]. The engine has no mango: carob (dark, round, 8 m) stands for it, jujube and fig for the lemon and the pomegranate, oleander for the flower beds [inferred]. The Yamuna's banks east and west of the terrace carry tamarisk and date palms.

**River.** Boatmen pole and row the boats tied under the terrace (barges carry sacks, rowboats a man amidships), buffaloes stand in the shallows with a herdsman, two women fill pots, a boy fishes, nets dry on the sand, sheets hang by the washermen; three country boats under sail move on the open lane of the river (`movers.js`, `waterways()`), clear of the moored ones [inferred: after the Yamuna boats in Mughal paintings and the Daniells' views of the 1780s-90s].

## 14. Deepening pass: the town

**Houses.** One house type is now five fronts: the jharokha with a domed or a flat-slab roof, a loggia (shops: an open upper arcade with a lattice rail), and plain shuttered windows; six wall washes (warm stucco, lime, red sandstone, ochre, rose, cream) [inferred: the washes of Agra's havelis]. Shops are entered: the verandah's platform runs back to the rear wall; shelved on three walls with the trade's wares, sacks below, the keeper's dais (gaddi) with white sheet, bolster, the ledger on its rahle, a lamp, a hookah, and scales (tarazu) on cords from a beam; a timber stair up the left wall to the room over it (dhurrie, charpoy with quilt, chest, jars, niche lamp; latticed windows cut through the wall), and on three storeys a solid top. Of the other houses about half stand open on a room with a plastered inside, the door's leaves folded back against the reveal, a latticed window cut through; the rest keep shut studded doors. 90% of the lots on the bazaar streets and 30% elsewhere are shops. Weathering: plaster fallen to show brick, rain streaks under the sills, damp at the foot, soot above lamp niches [inferred].

**Eye level** (`styles/mughal/fronts.js`, `settlements/mughal-agra/town.js`: one instanced element per lot): marigold garlands over doors, matka jars on stands, stone benches (chabutra) with a neighbour sitting, charpoys set out, cakes of dried dung (upla) stacked and slapped on walls or heaped in beehive stores (bitora), firewood, hens, goats, zebu, a tray of chillies, washing on poles from upper windows, on shops the hanging lamp, the cloth banner on its bracket, sacks, baskets and jars of the trade spilling onto the street, a mat of produce [inferred: after photographs of Agra's lanes; dung-cake use is universal in the Gangetic plain]. Streets: a course of flagstones along each frontage; tea stalls with stools and seated men, water stands (pyau), hawkers on mats, handcarts, zebu cows and dogs at the frontage, wells with their women at the crossings.

**Caravanserais.** Katra Resham (silk, with dyed silk hung out to dry in its court) and Katra Jogidas now stand south of the cross street, mirroring the northern two [stated: the four katras, §11].

**Halls.** The mosque and the jawab are opened: their arches pass into a hall 21 × 58 m under three inner domes (rings in the ceiling, gilt suns), the floor white marble in a black border with the outlines of the prayer rugs (the Taj's mosque has 569) [stated: Wikipedia, Taj Mahal], the mihrab an arched niche in marble inlaid with black, a three-step minbar, mosque lamps on chains and Qur'an stands; the jawab the same without the mihrab or rugs. The garden's two pavilions: a flat painted ceiling, a divan along the back, a carpet. Worshippers kneel in rows in the mosque (front rows fuller). The great gate is now walkable: its two iwans open (rectangular recesses of 13.5 m with a door in each back wall) into a passage between the guard rooms; the visitor's route runs forecourt, passage, then the garden's east lane (the channel and tank are water; the south wall's raised walk stops either side of the gate). The gate's side blocks are hollowed to guard rooms (chowki), each with a door through the iwan's side wall: three charpoys, a dhurrie, hookah, water jars, spears racked and shields on the walls, a guard on his bed and another at the door [inferred]. Music: the forecourt is a `market` zone, the halls a recitation (voice) over the drone.

**Not yet:** ghats with steps; the gate's upper chambers (naubat khana) and the tomb's crypt; stairs to the roofs of the houses; the mosque's floor rugs outlines are rectangles (the real ones have pointed tops).
