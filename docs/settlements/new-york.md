## 1. The real example

- **Empire State Building:** opened on 1 May 1931 at Fifth Avenue and 34th Street. It has 102 storeys and stands 381 m to the roof (the antenna came later). It has setbacks under the 1916 zoning law and an observatory on the 86th floor, and its mast was designed for mooring airships [stated: Wikipedia "Empire State Building"].
- **Chrysler Building:** completed in 1930 at Lexington Avenue and 42nd Street, 319 m tall. It has a terraced stainless-steel crown of sunbursts with triangular windows, eagle gargoyles and radiator-cap ornaments [stated: Wikipedia "Chrysler Building"].
- **Chanin Building** (1929, 197 m) and **Lincoln Building** (1930, 205 m) on 42nd Street [stated: Wikipedia articles of those names].
- **Grand Central Terminal:** opened in 1913. Its Beaux-Arts front on 42nd Street has three great arched windows [stated: Wikipedia "Grand Central Terminal"].
- **New York Public Library Main Branch:** opened in 1911 on Fifth Avenue between 40th and 42nd Streets, with Bryant Park behind it [stated: Wikipedia "New York Public Library Main Branch"].
- **Sixth Avenue Elevated:** ran over Sixth Avenue until 1938 [stated: Wikipedia "IRT Sixth Avenue Line"].
- **The grid:** the Commissioners' Plan of 1811. It runs about 29° east of true north [stated: Wikipedia "Commissioners' Plan of 1811"].
- **Moment shown:** autumn 1931, just after the Empire State opened.

### The Chrysler Building's crown and ornament

- The crown is "composed of seven radiating terraced arches", a cruciform groin vault. Its steel is "ribbed and riveted in a radiating sunburst pattern" with "many triangular vaulted windows", in Krupp's austenitic stainless steel sold as Nirosta. The spire was 38 m (125 ft) long. "The corners of the 61st floor are graced with eagles", and the corner ornaments of the 31st floor are "replicas of the 1929 Chrysler radiator caps" [stated: Wikipedia "Chrysler Building", via an IPFS mirror]. Built: seven tiers stepping in (half-widths 13 → 4.2 m). Each tier is a square drum with a semicircular arched head on every face, roofed by the groin vault of two crossing barrels. On each face: a rim and the sunburst's ribs, 5–9 triangular windows pointing out along the arch like rays, and a band of small triangular windows under it (lit glass). The spire's foot is hidden in the last vault. Four eagle gargoyles lean diagonally out from the 61st floor's corners (a neck, head and beak on a base). Four winged radiator caps stand on stepped bases at the 31st floor's corners. The eagles used to stand at the middles of the faces; that was wrong and has been fixed [stated: positions; inferred: the shapes and the tiers' sizes].

### The street of 1931

- **Water tanks:** each stands on a braced steel frame with a timber platform. The tub is of upright staves with five steel hoops, under a conical roof with a vent, and has a ladder up the side [inferred: the type still seen on Manhattan roofs; sizes ours].
- **Fire escapes** on about half the mid-rise lofts and houses, on the street front: a balcony of iron at each floor from the second, with its railing, and a stair between each that zig-zags. A drop ladder hangs over the shop front [inferred: the type of the period; the street front from the lot's half-block].
- **Traffic:** the avenues and cross streets run two-way with the traffic keeping right (Fifth Avenue became one-way only in 1966 — inferred). Motor cars of 1930 are black, cream and blue. About four in ten are cabs in yellow: the Yellow Cab colour dates from 1907, and "Checker Cabs produced large yellow and black taxis that became the most common taxis in New York City"; during the Depression the city had as many as 30,000 cab drivers [stated: Wikipedia "Taxicabs of New York City", via an IPFS mirror]. Yellow was ordered for all medallion cabs only in 1967 [stated: the same], so not every cab is yellow: the plain yellow stands for both liveries, and the Checker's black band is not modelled [inferred]. The cars on the Sixth Avenue keep clear of the El's columns.
- **People** on the avenues' pavements: men in dark suits and brimmed hats, women in coats with cloche-like caps [inferred]. Lamp posts every 30 m along the avenues.

### 1a. The Empire State Building, rebuilt (2026-11-06)

- **Massing:** "The building's tower sits on a five-story base"; the tower "is dramatically set back above its base" and has "shallow setbacks at the 21st, 25th, 30th, and 72nd floors", a more pronounced one at the 81st, and a last one at the 85th that marks the observatory [stated: NRHP nomination, NPS]. "The setbacks are located at the 21st, 25th, 30th, 72nd, 81st, and 85th stories" [stated: cavacopedia mirror of Wikipedia "Empire State Building"]. Built: the base over the whole lot to 22 m, then 3.6 m a floor: stages from the 5th to the 21st, 25th, 30th, 72nd, 81st and 85th floors (half-widths 44 → 14.5 m E–W, 18.5 → 9 m N–S); the shaft (30th–72nd) and the stage over it (72nd–81st) with a broad pavilion on each face standing 1.6 m (1.0 m) proud of the recessed corners [inferred: the stage sizes and the pavilions from photographs; the lot is compressed, §2].
- **Skin:** "dull aluminum spandrels with modernistic moldings"; "Each window is enframed by a strip of nickel-chrome-steel"; "Windows protrude slightly from the limestone tower" [stated: NRHP]. "vertical nickel-chrome steel mullions" with "horizontal aluminum spandrels" between floors; limestone cladding; granite on the storefronts [stated: cavacopedia]. Built: on every stage face, window bands every ~1.55 m with a chrome strip down each side, a limestone pier between bands, a spandrel (lead-grey metal standing for dull aluminium) at every floor; flush limestone copings at the setbacks [inferred: the band spacing and pier width].
- **Base and entrance:** "Each storefront has a black granite base" [stated: NRHP]. "The Fifth Avenue facade centers on the building's main entrance, a central pair of doors flanked by revolving doors"; "Two giant molded-stone piers, topped by stylized stone eagles, frame the entrance, and the words 'EMPIRE STATE' are inscribed above" [stated: NRHP]; "a triple-height transom with geometric patterns" above the doors; the golden letters above the fifth-floor windows [stated: cavacopedia]. Built: storefronts of plate glass between black granite piers on a black granite base, a chrome transom bar, a limestone fascia; the base's four storeys banded like the tower; on Fifth Avenue a 2.2 m deep recess with a stepped chrome transom over the central doors and two revolving doors, two piers with mouldings and stone eagles (stylised, wings raised), and EMPIRE STATE in gilt letters at the top of the base [inferred: the shapes of the eagles and the transom's pattern].
- **Observatory and mast:** the 86th floor "contains both an enclosed viewing gallery and an open-air outdoor viewing area" [stated: cavacopedia]. "Rising above the 86-story office building is the aluminum, chrome-nickel-steel and glass mast": "Four progressively smaller rectangular levels" form its base, "from which springs a cylindrical shaft"; at the top an enclosed observation level, a smaller open-air level and a truncated cone housing the mooring mechanism [stated: NRHP]. Built: the promenade with its parapet round the enclosed observatory; four ribbed tiers of aluminium with chrome ribs and windows; four winged fins at the corners rising to the shaft; the fluted shaft with slit windows; the glazed 102nd-floor gallery; the open gallery with its railing; the cone with its ribs and the mooring tip at 381 m [inferred: the fins' form, all sizes]. No antenna (added later) and no fence on the 86th floor (1947) [stated/inferred].

### 1b. The Sixth Avenue El and its stations

- The 33rd and 42nd Street stations "had two tracks and two side platforms"; they opened on 5 June 1878 and closed on 4 December 1938; the 33rd Street station's neighbours were 28th and 38th Streets [stated: Wikipedia "33rd Street (IRT Sixth Avenue Line)" and "42nd Street (IRT Sixth Avenue Line)", kiwix mirror]. The line's stations were "designed by famed Hudson River School painter Jasper Francis Cropsey, a trained architect" [stated: Wikipedia "14th Street (IRT Sixth Avenue Line)", kiwix mirror].
- Built: two tracks on a viaduct over the avenue's middle — latticed columns (two channels joined by lattice bars, a cast foot) at x ± 3.9 m every 15 m with knee braces, a plate cross girder on every bent, two lattice girders under each track, timber ties every 0.75 m, running rails, guard timbers, the third rail on porcelain insulators (the Manhattan els ran electrically from about 1903 — inferred); stations at 33rd, 38th and 42nd Streets, each two side platforms 60 m long on brackets, a station house of green-painted boards and battens under a gabled slate roof with iron cresting and finials, doors and windows to the platform, a canopy on iron posts, benches, railings, and a stair from each platform down to the pavement mid-block, with its landing out over the kerb (people and lamp posts are kept off the stairs) [inferred: the station houses' form and colour from photographs of the Cropsey stations; the sizes].

### 1c. Grand Central's 42nd Street front

- "Fronting on 42nd Street there are three large arched windows, surrounded by massive pillars", "surmounted by a large statuary group about a clock" — Mercury with Hercules and Minerva, by Jules Coutan; the steel frame "was sheathed with granite at street level and with limestone above"; the terminal is 673 ft long and 301 ft wide; the Reed and Stem design "called for an elevated driveway around the Terminal" [stated: NRHP nomination of Grand Central Terminal, NPS]. The group "stretches 48 feet high", "the clock below it measures 13 feet in diameter"; the clock face is stained glass [stated: Untapped New York].
- Built: the lot widened to 88 m on 42nd Street (x 136–224; the real front is 92 m) and kept 38 m deep; a granite ground storey with the street doors; on it the elevated roadway (asphalt, a stone balustrade, lamp standards) in front of the limestone body set 8 m back; end bays with two framed windows each; three arched windows (10.2 m wide) with iron mullions and transoms, sills and keystones, soffits round the heads; pairs of engaged columns on pedestals between and beside the arches; the entablature, a cornice with modillions, the attic; the clock (4 m, glass in a gilt frame, hour marks, hands) on a block with Mercury standing over it and Hercules and Minerva seated either side (simplified figures, read from the street 40 m below) [inferred: the columns' order and all proportions from photographs]. Park Avenue's viaduct of 1919 carries the roadway from the front over 42nd and 41st Streets on steel spans and down between granite-faced walls to 40th Street (8 m wide here); Park Avenue's cars keep to the lanes either side [inferred: the date and route from the Park Avenue Viaduct's history; the width ours]. Cars and people are no longer placed inside the landmarks' lots.

### 1d. The street and the yard

- Shop fronts: on the street front of each lot (and its side at a corner) each shop has a plate-glass window on a painted stall riser, its door at one end with a transom, a chrome bar over the glass; about a third have a canvas awning on an iron frame sloping to a scalloped valance, about a fifth a painted blade sign on brackets [inferred: the shop front of the period from photographs].
- Newsstands: about twenty wooden booths at the avenues' corners with 34th, 38th and 42nd Streets, the papers stacked on the counter, magazines pegged up the back and sides [inferred].
- Rear yards and washing: the low buildings stop 3.5 m short of the block's middle line (the towers fill their lots), leaving yards; in the tenement blocks east of Lexington washing hangs on pulley lines from back windows to a tall pole in the yard [inferred: the tenement yard of the period; the district from the Third Avenue tenements].

## 3. Orientation

Manhattan's avenues run about 29° east of true north [stated: Wikipedia "Commissioners' Plan of 1811"; Charles Petzold, "How Far from True North are the Avenues of Manhattan?" — "The avenues of Manhattan deviate from true north by 29°"]. Built so since 2026-11-06: the whole layout is drawn in the grid's own frame (+x along the cross streets toward the East River, −z up the avenues) and turned 29° into the settlement's frame (`GRID`, `toCity` / `toGrid` in `layout.js`), so that uptown is N 29° E. Everything turns together: the avenues and streets, the lots and their buildings, Broadway, the El and its stations, the viaduct, the landmarks, Bryant Park's lawn and planting, the ground's gentle rise, the newsstands and yards, the traffic, the people and the lamps. A plan's own `yaw` turns only by quarter turns (its colliders stay on the axes), so the plans stand unturned at the origin and their elements are built inside the style's `turned` element (`src/styles/artdeco/turn.js`), which turns their geometry, lights and obstacles exactly and lays their floors and ramps as strips of axis-aligned rectangles inside the turned ones; the life of the streets (single placed things) is moved and turned in `town.js`. The arrival (`src/planet/arrivals.js`) turned with it: Fifth Avenue at 35th Street, looking down the avenue at the Empire State. [inferred: the method]

One consequence: the generic street life added to every settlement (`src/objects/life.js`) finds no house fronts in New York now (the buildings are inside `turned`), so it no longer sets benches, crates and stalls by the doors; New York's own traffic, people, lamps, newsstands and washing are unchanged. ## 4. The style

Steel-framed towers clad in limestone and brick that step back as they rise; Art Deco crowns in steel; Beaux-Arts marble and limestone; loft buildings with water tanks on their roofs. [stated]

## 5. The town

About 800 buildings on the blocks: lofts of 10–20 storeys west of Fifth (the garment district), brownstones in Murray Hill, and towers clustering toward 42nd Street. [stated: districts; counts ours — inferred]
